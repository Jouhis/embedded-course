Bench lab 7: The flaky LED strip

Drive a WS2812 strip from the PIO, as lesson 65 did. Then power the strip from 5 V and feed it 3.3 V data: sometimes it works, sometimes it flickers. Find the reason in two datasheets and fix it with one logic chip.

This bench lab is optional, and a stretch. It needs a Pico 2, a strip of 8 WS2812B LEDs, a 74HCT125 quad buffer, a 330 Ω resistor and a 100 µF capacitor. It doesn't count towards your progress. Time: about 55 minutes.
By the end of this lab you can check an interface's logic levels against both datasheets, explain why a marginal signal works on one desk and fails on another, and fix it with a level-shifting buffer.

Two datasheets, one wire

A WS2812B is an RGB LED with a small controller inside. It takes one data line at 800 kHz: each bit is a high pulse whose length says 0 or 1, and 24 bits set one pixel. Each pixel keeps the first 24 bits it receives and passes the rest on to the next, and a low of at least 50 µs latches all the colours (WS2812B datasheet). Lesson 65 generated this waveform with the PIO, and this lab's ws2812.pio is the same program from pico-examples.

Now read the electrical table of that datasheet. The LED runs from 3.5 to 5.3 V, and its data input needs a high of at least 0.7 × VDD. Powered from 5 V, that's 3.5 V. A Pico 2 pin driving high reaches at most its IOVDD, 3.3 V, and is only guaranteed to reach 2.62 V under load (RP2350 datasheet, section 14.9.4). So the high level from the Pico is below what the LED is specified to accept.

"Below the specified minimum" doesn't mean "never works". The real threshold of a given LED is usually lower than the guaranteed 0.7 × VDD, so many strips do work at 3.3 V, on a short wire, at room temperature. Then the supply rises to 5.2 V, or the wire gets longer, or a different batch of LEDs arrives, and the first pixel starts misreading bits. That's the worst kind of bug: it passes testing and fails in the field.

The fix is a buffer whose input accepts 3.3 V and whose output swings to 5 V. The 74HCT125 is such a buffer: powered from 5 V, its HCT inputs need only 2 V for a high (TI SN74HCT125 datasheet, section 5.2), and its output swings close to its 5 V supply. That is what a level shifter does. The plain HC version won't do: with a 5 V supply, its input high threshold is far above 3.3 V.

Stage 1: in Wokwi

  1. Open projects/bench07-ws2812-strip/starter, build and run. The ring of 8 LEDs cycles through four colours, and the log names them: red, green, blue, white.
  2. Compare: when the log says red, the ring shows green, and the other way round. That's a firmware bug the simulator does show.
  3. Task 1: read the datasheet's "data transmission method" and fix grb(). The WS2812 takes green first, then red, then blue, most significant bit first. The PIO shifts out from bit 31, so the 24 bits go in the top of the word: g << 24 | r << 16 | b << 8. Run again: the colours match the log.

In Wokwi that's the end: the data line is ideal and has no voltage, so the level problem can't appear.

Stage 2: on the board

Power: 8 pixels at full white draw up to about 0.5 A, all of USB's 500 mA. The program uses quarter brightness. Power the strip from VBUS (pin 40), which is the USB 5 V, and put the 100 µF capacitor across the strip's 5 V and GND at the strip's end. Connect the strip's GND to the Pico's GND. Never power a strip from the Pico's 3V3 pin.
  1. Direct. Wire GP2 → 330 Ω → the strip's DIN. Flash the solution. Watch for a few minutes: does every pixel show the right colour every second? Some strips are perfect, others flicker or show wrong colours on the first pixel, then everything after it.
  2. Make it marginal. If yours works, make the data wire longer (30 cm or more) and watch again. You're reducing the margin you never had a guarantee for.
  3. Measure. With the multimeter on DC volts, measure DIN to GND while the strip shows white: it reads an average, but compare it with the same measurement in the next step.
  4. Fix. Put the 74HCT125 on the breadboard: VCC (pin 14) to VBUS, GND (pin 7) to GND, 1OE (pin 1) to GND to enable buffer 1, 1A (pin 2) from GP2, and 1Y (pin 3) → 330 Ω → DIN. Don't leave the other three buffers' inputs floating (bench lab 2): tie their A inputs (pins 5, 9, 12) to GND and their OE pins (4, 10, 13) to VCC, which disables them. Watch again with the long wire: the strip is solid.

If you have a logic analyzer, it won't show the problem: it measures logic levels with its own threshold, which a 3.3 V signal passes. Only the LED's own threshold matters here, and that's the point: an interface is correct when the driver's guaranteed output meets the receiver's guaranteed input, not when one particular board happens to work.

Common mistakes

  1. "It works on my desk." A signal below the receiver's VIH minimum works on some parts and fails on others. Check the numbers, not the demo.
  2. Using a 74HC125. With a 5 V supply, an HC input needs a much higher voltage for a high than 3.3 V. The T in HCT is what makes it accept 3.3 V logic.
  3. No common ground. If the strip's supply and the Pico don't share GND, the data has no reference and nothing works reliably.
  4. RGB order. WS2812 pixels take GRB. Wrong order shows as red and green swapped, in the simulator too.

Summary

  • A WS2812B at 5 V needs a data high of at least 0.7 × VDD = 3.5 V; a 3.3 V GPIO can't guarantee that.
  • Below-spec signals often work by luck, and fail with temperature, supply, wire length or part batch.
  • A 74HCT125 powered from 5 V accepts a 2 V high and outputs 5 V levels: a simple level shifter.
  • WS2812 data order is GRB, most significant bit first; a low of 50 µs or more latches the frame.
  • Wokwi's data lines have no voltage, so level mismatches never appear in simulation.
You checked an interface with both datasheets, found a margin that was negative, and fixed it properly. That's a design review you can now do for any two chips on a wire.
