Reading datasheets: ratings, characteristics and timing

A datasheet is the manufacturer's statement of what the part can do. Read it in the right order, and you will know which numbers are limits, which are guarantees and which are just examples.

By the end of this lesson you can find the absolute maximum ratings, the electrical characteristics and the switching times in a MOSFET datasheet, state the test conditions that each number depends on, and check a simulator model against the datasheet.

Where we are, and why this matters

Lesson 12 showed how a MOSFET switches a load, and lesson 15 showed that the switch wastes power as heat when it carries current. Lesson 17 taught you to read the drawing of a board. The next question is whether the drawing works: which part do you put in it, and can it survive the voltages, currents and temperatures your design will give it. The answer is in the datasheet, the document the manufacturer publishes for each part. Lab 1 depends on the same numbers, and so does any driver you will design after it.

Many real projects go wrong at this point. A MOSFET is picked from a list, and the first page shows a large current and a low on-resistance. The design is built, and the switch runs hot, or the gate is driven too weakly, or the simulator and the bench disagree. Almost every one of those failures comes from a number that was read without its condition. The rest of this lesson gives you the order to read a datasheet and the habit of checking each number against the condition it belongs to.

Three kinds of number

A datasheet has three kinds of number, and they mean different things. The first kind is the absolute maximum rating, which is a stress limit. Exceed one and the part may be damaged, even if only briefly, and the part is not guaranteed to work at that value. The second kind is the electrical characteristics, which are measured values with test conditions, usually given as minimum, typical and maximum. These are guarantees only at the conditions printed beside them. The third kind is timing: rise times, fall times and delays, which are measured under a stated drive and load, and which change when either changes.

A number without its condition is not a specification. The on-resistance of a MOSFET, written RDS(on), is only meaningful with the gate voltage and drain current that go with it. A reader who sees "0.025 Ω" and stops there has read half of the number. The other half is the sentence that says 5 V on the gate and 25 A through the drain.

Why the datasheet is ordered this way

The order of a MOSFET datasheet is designed for the reader who needs the limits first. The first page gives the headline current and voltage, the absolute maximum table gives the stress limits, and the characteristic tables give the guarantees and the timing. A designer reads the limits to decide whether the part can ever be used in the circuit. Then reads the guarantees to size the drive and the heatsink. Then reads the timing to decide whether the switching is fast enough. Reading in the other order, starting with the headline current, is how the mistakes in the introduction happen.

Absolute maximum ratings

The IRLZ44N, a logic-level N-channel MOSFET from Infineon's HEXFET family, lists a drain-source voltage of 55 V, a continuous drain current of 47 A with the case held at 25 °C, and a gate-source voltage of ±16 V (IRLZ44N datasheet, Absolute Maximum Ratings). The gate limit is the one people approach without noticing. The gate is a thin insulating layer, and the ±16 V rating is the voltage that layer can take. A 5 V logic output on the gate is far inside that limit. A gate driven from a 20 V supply is outside it.

The 47 A is a case-temperature figure, and the same table gives 33 A for a case at 100 °C (IRLZ44N datasheet, Absolute Maximum Ratings, ID at TC = 100 °C). A part that runs with its case hotter carries less current. The first page is the best case, not the design case. The same table gives a junction temperature range of −55 °C to +175 °C, which is the temperature the silicon inside the package may reach.

The 47 A is a case-temperature figure. At 100 °C the continuous current is lower, so read the condition row, not the headline number on the first page.

Thermal resistance: from power to temperature rise

Heat flows from the silicon, the junction, through the package to the case, and from there into a heatsink or the air. Thermal resistance is the number that tells you how much temperature rise a given power produces along that path. It is written Rθ, in degrees Celsius per watt. The IRLZ44N gives a maximum of 1.4 °C/W from junction to case, and a maximum of 62 °C/W from junction to ambient, meaning the part on its own in the air (IRLZ44N datasheet, Thermal Resistance). The two numbers are used differently. The junction-to-case value sizes a heatsink, and the junction-to-ambient value tells you what happens with no heatsink at all.

Electrical characteristics

The electrical characteristics table is where most design numbers come from. Two rows matter most for a switch. The gate threshold, VGS(th), is 1.0 V minimum and 2.0 V maximum, measured at VDS = VGS and ID = 250 µA (IRLZ44N datasheet, Electrical Characteristics, V(GS)th). The threshold is where the channel starts to conduct, and the current you measure at a given gate voltage is one that the part only guarantees under the stated test condition.

The on-resistance, RDS(on), is 0.022 Ω at VGS = 10 V and ID = 25 A, 0.025 Ω at 5.0 V and 25 A, and 0.035 Ω at 4.0 V and 21 A (IRLZ44N datasheet, Electrical Characteristics, R(DS)on). Each value has its own gate voltage and current. The on-resistance rises as the gate voltage falls, so a part that is 0.022 Ω at 10 V is not 0.022 Ω on a 3.3 V logic pin. The datasheet does not give RDS(on) at 3.3 V, so a 3.3 V design needs either an extrapolation, which is a guess, or a part specified at 3.3 V. Using the "typical" column for a worst-case design is a common mistake. Use the maximum for RDS(on) and the power loss, and the minimum or maximum for the threshold, depending on which way the error hurts you.

The total gate charge, Qg, is given as a maximum of 48 nC at a drain current of 25 A, a drain-source voltage of 44 V and a gate drive of 5.0 V (IRLZ44N datasheet, Electrical Characteristics, Qg). The input capacitance, Ciss, is 1700 pF typical at VGS = 0 V. Qg is the charge the gate driver must move to switch the device, and it tells you how much current the driver must supply to switch the device quickly. A 4 mA GPIO driver charges 48 nC in 48 nC ÷ 4 mA = 12 µs. That is slow for PWM, and lab 1 uses this number when it justifies the gate resistor.

Working the numbers: the conduction loss

Here is the calculation a designer does with these rows. Take the 5 V gate row and the maximum on-resistance, 0.025 Ω. Suppose the switch carries 2 A. The conduction loss is P = I² × RDS(on) = 2² × 0.025 Ω = 4 × 0.025 = 0.1 W. Multiply by the junction-to-ambient maximum of 62 °C/W, and the junction rises about 6.2 °C above the air on a bare board. That is small, and the switch is fine.

Now take the same switch at 25 A, the headline condition. The loss is 25² × 0.025 Ω = 625 × 0.025 = 15.6 W. Through the junction-to-case path, a heatsink with a case-to-ambient path must carry that power, and the junction-to-case rise is 15.6 W × 1.4 °C/W = 21.8 °C. On a bare board the rise would be 15.6 W × 62 °C/W = 967 °C, which is far above the 175 °C junction limit. The part is not usable at that current without a heatsink. That single calculation explains the datasheet's two thermal numbers, and it is why the headline current is a rating that assumes stated cooling.

Timing and switching

Switching times are the delays and transition times the device shows when its gate is driven hard and fast. The datasheet names four. The turn-on delay, td(on), is the time from the gate step until the drain current starts to rise. The rise time, tr, is how long the current takes to reach its final value. The turn-off delay, td(off), and the fall time, tf, are the same two steps in reverse. The IRLZ44N gives typical values of td(on) = 11 ns, tr = 84 ns, td(off) = 26 ns and tf = 15 ns, all at VDD = 28 V, ID = 25 A, VGS = 5.0 V, RG = 3.4 Ω and RD = 1.1 Ω (IRLZ44N datasheet, Electrical Characteristics, Switching Characteristics).

Those times belong to one set of conditions. Change the gate resistor, the load or the supply, and the times change. The figures in the datasheet are pictures of a single test, and you should read them as a record of that test only.

Try it in CircuitJS: check a model against the datasheet

The built-in MOSFET example uses a threshold of 1.5 V and a gain coefficient β of 0.02. The simulator defines its drain current in saturation as ½ × β × (VGS − VGS(th))², and in the linear region as β × ((VGS − VGS(th)) × VDS − VDS²/2). Those equations come from the simulator's MOSFET source (CircuitJS, MosfetElm.java, calculate). With the drain supply at 5 V and the gate at 5.0 V, the overdrive is 3.5 V, and the current should be ½ × 0.02 × 3.5² = 0.1225 A.

  1. Open the example: CircuitJS: nmosfet.txt.
  2. Select the MOSFET and open its properties to confirm the threshold is 1.5 V and β is 0.02. Compare the threshold with the datasheet range of 1.0 V to 2.0 V. The threshold is inside it.
  3. Set the Gate Voltage slider to 5.0 V, the datasheet's logic-level test voltage, and read ID from the MOSFET's info.
  4. Compare the measured value with the prediction. Then write one sentence saying what the datasheet does and does not tell you about this model.
Hint

The datasheet's "logic-level" rows are at 4 V and 5 V gate drive. The example's drain supply is 5 V, so the device is in saturation, where the current depends on the overdrive and not on the drain voltage. The test is saturation when VDS is at least the overdrive.

The measured current should be 0.1225 A. If it is not, check that the gate slider reads 5.0 V and not 3.5 V, which is the example's starting value.

What the model does not tell you

Now do the check the lesson is about. The model's β of 0.02 implies an on-resistance in the linear region of about 1 ÷ (β × Vov). With the 3.5 V overdrive, that is 1 ÷ (0.02 × 3.5) ≈ 14 Ω. The IRLZ44N datasheet gives 0.025 Ω at 5 V. The example is about five hundred times more resistive than the real part, and it is a small signal device. Its threshold is inside the IRLZ44N's range, but the threshold is the only number the two share. The model is useful for learning the square-law equations and for watching the saturation curve. It is not a prediction for this part, and the sentence you write in step 4 should say so.

You read a MOSFET datasheet in the right order: limits first, then the guaranteed numbers with their conditions, then the timing. You also checked a simulator model against its threshold range, and found where the model stops describing the part. That is the habit you need before you trust any part number in a design.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

The first mistake is to take the typical column for a worst-case design. The loss calculation then uses an on-resistance that the part only reaches on a good day. The symptom is a switch that runs hotter on the bench than the design predicted, and a heatsink that is too small. The fix is to use the maximum column for RDS(on) and the power loss, and to write the column you used beside each number in the calculation.

The second mistake is to drive the gate at a voltage whose row you have not read. A designer picks the 10 V on-resistance because it is the headline, then drives the gate from a 3.3 V pin. The symptom is a switch that is warm even at light load, because the channel is only partly on. The fix is to read the row at the gate voltage you actually have, or to choose a part specified at your voltage. If the datasheet has no row at 3.3 V, say so in the design review rather than extrapolating silently.

The third mistake is to treat the headline current as the design current. The first page says 47 A, and the designer sizes the traces and the heatsink for 47 A in a hot enclosure. The symptom is a part that fails at a fraction of its rating, when the case runs at 100 °C and the continuous current drops to 33 A. The fix is to read the condition row for the temperature you will reach, and to derate the current with it.

The fourth mistake is to read a stress limit as an operating value. A designer sees the ±16 V gate rating and decides a gate at 16 V is a safe design point, because it is inside the rating. The symptom is a gate oxide that fails after weeks, not on the bench. The fix is the one the datasheet implies: operate well inside the absolute maximum, and use the characteristic tables, not the rating, to choose the drive.

In real projects

A professional part selection is a short written table. It lists each candidate part with its key numbers, the condition under which each number applies, and the margin between the number and the design's worst case. The reviewer checks the margins, not the headline numbers. For a MOSFET the table has the threshold, the on-resistance at the real gate voltage, the maximum current at the real case temperature, the gate charge and the thermal resistance, each with its condition written beside it.

Summary

  • Absolute maximum ratings are stress limits, not operating values. Exceeding one can damage the part.
  • Electrical characteristics are guarantees only at their stated conditions, so every number needs its condition beside it.
  • The IRLZ44N's gate threshold is 1.0 V to 2.0 V, and its on-resistance is 0.022 Ω at 10 V, 0.025 Ω at 5 V and 0.035 Ω at 4 V.
  • Conduction loss is I² × RDS(on). Use the maximum column for worst-case loss.
  • Thermal resistance converts power to temperature rise. Junction to case is 1.4 °C/W and junction to ambient is 62 °C/W for this part.
  • The 47 A headline is at a case of 25 °C. At 100 °C the continuous current is 33 A.
  • A simulator model has its own parameters. Check them against the datasheet range, and say where the model does not apply.
