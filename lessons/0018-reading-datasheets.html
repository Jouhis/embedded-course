Reading datasheets: ratings, characteristics and timing

A datasheet is the contract between the part and your design. Read it in the right order, and you will know which numbers are limits, which are guarantees and which are just examples.

By the end of this lesson you can find the absolute maximum ratings, the electrical characteristics and the switching times in a MOSFET datasheet, state the test conditions that each number depends on, and check a simulator model against the datasheet.

The idea

A datasheet has three kinds of numbers, and they mean different things. Absolute maximum ratings are stress limits. Exceed one and the part may be damaged, even if only briefly, and the part is not guaranteed to work at that value. Electrical characteristics are measured values with test conditions, usually given as minimum, typical and maximum. They are guarantees only at those conditions. Timing values, such as rise and fall times, are measured under stated load and drive conditions, and they change when either changes. A number without its condition is not a specification. "RDS(on) = 0.022 Ω" means nothing until you read the VGS and ID next to it.

Absolute maximum ratings

The IRLZ44N, an Infineon logic-level N-channel MOSFET, lists a drain-source voltage of 55 V, a continuous drain current of 47 A at a case temperature of 25 °C, and a gate-source voltage of ±16 V (IRLZ44N datasheet, Absolute Maximum Ratings). The gate limit is the one people exceed by accident. A 5 V logic supply on the gate is fine, but a 12 V gate supply on a bench test is not. The 47 A is a case-temperature figure. At 100 °C the continuous current is lower, so read the condition row, not the headline number on the first page.

Thermal resistance tells you how much temperature rise a given power gives. The IRLZ44N lists 1.4 °C/W from junction to case and 62 °C/W from junction to ambient. Those are the two numbers you use to size a heatsink or check whether a MOSFET overheats on a bare PCB.

Electrical characteristics

The electrical characteristics table is where most design numbers come from. Two rows matter most for a switch. The gate threshold, VGS(th), is 1.0 V minimum and 2.0 V maximum, measured at VDS = VGS and ID = 250 µA. The on-resistance, RDS(on), is 0.022 Ω at VGS = 10 V and 25 A, 0.025 Ω at 5.0 V and 25 A, and 0.035 Ω at 4.0 V and 21 A (IRLZ44N datasheet, Electrical Characteristics). The datasheet does not give RDS(on) at 3.3 V, so a 3.3 V design needs an extrapolation, or a part specified at 3.3 V. Using the "typical" column for a worst-case design is a common mistake. Use the maximum for RDS(on) and the power loss, and the minimum or maximum for the threshold, depending on which way the error hurts you.

The total gate charge, Qg, is 48 nC at the stated conditions, and the input capacitance, Ciss, is 1700 pF at VGS = 0 V. Qg tells you how much current a gate driver needs to switch the device quickly. A 4 mA GPIO driver charges 48 nC in 48 nC ÷ 4 mA = 12 µs. That is slow for PWM, and lab 1 uses this number when it justifies the gate resistor.

Timing and switching

Switching times, such as turn-on delay td(on), rise time tr, turn-off delay td(off) and fall time tf, are given for stated drive and load conditions. The IRLZ44N lists tr = 84 ns and td(off) = 26 ns at VDD = 28 V, ID = 25 A, VGS = 5 V and RG = 3.4 Ω (IRLZ44N datasheet, Switching Characteristics). Change any of these, and the times change. Read the figure of the switching waveform as a picture of those conditions, not as a general law.

Try it in CircuitJS: check a model against the datasheet

The built-in MOSFET example uses a threshold of 1.5 V and a β of 0.02. The datasheet's threshold range is 1.0 V to 2.0 V, so 1.5 V is inside it. In saturation, the drain current is ½ × β × (VGS − VGS(th))². With the drain supply at 5 V and the gate at 5.0 V, the overdrive is 3.5 V, so the current should be ½ × 0.02 × 3.5² = 122.5 mA.

  2. Select the MOSFET and open its properties to confirm the threshold is 1.5 V and β is 0.02. Compare the threshold with the datasheet range.
  3. Set the Gate Voltage slider to 5.0 V, the datasheet's logic-level test voltage, and read ID from the MOSFET's info.
  4. Compare the measured value with the prediction. Then write one sentence saying what the datasheet does and does not tell you about this model.
Hint

The datasheet's "logic-level" rows are at 4 V and 5 V gate drive. The example's drain supply is 5 V, so the device is in saturation, where the current depends on the overdrive and not on the drain voltage.

The measured current should be 0.1225 A. If it is not, check that the gate slider reads 5.0 V and not 3.5 V, which is the example's starting value.

You read a MOSFET datasheet in the right order: limits first, then the guaranteed numbers with their conditions, then the timing. You also checked a simulator model against its threshold range. That is the habit you need before you trust any part number in a design.

"A datasheet gives RDS(on) at 10 V and your gate is 3.3 V. What do you do?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

I would not use the 10 V number, because the device is not guaranteed at my gate voltage. I would look for the RDS(on) row at a gate voltage close to 3.3 V, or a logic-level part specified there. If the datasheet does not give one, I would use the model: take the threshold maximum, which is the worst case, and estimate the overdrive, then use the maximum current for the loss, and I would confirm on the bench with the real device at the real temperature.
