Power, energy, and heat

How fast a circuit uses energy, and the heat that results

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the power a resistor dissipates from its voltage, current or resistance, and choose a power rating with margin.

Heat in resistors and other parts

Lesson 5 gave us voltage, current and resistance, with Ohm's law linking them, and we found that a 100 Ω resistor on a 5 V supply carries 50 mA. That number says how much charge moves through the resistor each second. It leaves open what happens to the resistor itself, and this lesson answers that.

Heat is the failure that catches most beginners, because a resistor that is too small for its job rarely breaks on the first power-up. It warms up, its value drifts, a nearby capacitor ages faster, and some weeks later the board fails in the field. A heater in a 3D printer shows the same physics on a larger scale, since the heater element is a resistor meant to get hot while the wiring and the MOSFET that switches it must not get hot by accident. Choosing parts that survive therefore means working out the heat before you build the board.

Power as the rate of energy use

Energy is the capacity to do work, measured in joules (J), and power is the rate at which energy is used, measured in watts (W), where one watt is one joule per second. The two relate the way speed and distance do, since power says how fast the energy goes and energy is what the power adds up to over time. An electricity bill, for example, counts energy in kilowatt-hours, which is power multiplied by time.

For an electrical component, the power is the voltage across it multiplied by the current through it:

P = V × I(1)

where P is the power in watts, V is the voltage across the component in volts and I is the current through it in amperes. The units confirm the formula, because a volt is one joule per coulomb and an ampere is one coulomb per second, so multiplying them cancels the coulombs and leaves joules per second, which is watts. You can check any power formula this way, and if the units do not come out as watts, the formula is wrong.

A resistor does no useful work in a circuit. It is there to limit a current or to set a voltage, so almost all of the power it takes from the supply leaves as heat. As that energy goes into the resistor its temperature rises, and the rise continues until the heat flowing out to the air and the board equals the power flowing in. At that point the temperature stops rising, and engineers call this condition steady state.

Power in terms of resistance

Ohm's law from lesson 5 lets us replace either the voltage or the current in equation (1) with a resistance, so we can work with whichever two quantities we know. Substituting V = I × R gives the first form, and substituting I = V ÷ R gives the second:

P = I² × R(2)

P = V² ÷ R(3)

where R is the resistance in ohms and the other symbols are as in equation (1). All three forms give the same answer for the same resistor, so the choice depends only on which numbers you have measured or been given.

Equation (2) suits a known current, for example one measured with an ammeter in series, and equation (3) suits a known voltage across a part of known resistance. When you have measured both the voltage and the current, equation (1) is the direct route. Picking the form that uses the quantities you actually have is worth the habit, because every extra conversion step is another chance for an arithmetic slip.

  1. 5 V across 100 Ω drives 50 mA. The resistor turns P = V × I = 5 × 0.050 = 0.25 W of electrical power into heat, all the time the current flows.
  2. At first the resistor is at room temperature and loses little heat, so most of the 0.25 W goes into warming it. The temperature climbs quickly.
  3. The hotter it is, the faster heat leaves to the air and the board. Less of the power is left over to warm it, so the rise slows down.
  4. Steady state: the heat leaving equals the 0.25 W going in, and the temperature stops rising. Where it stops depends on the part's size and its surroundings.
  5. Choosing the part: the lesson's rule is a rating of at least twice the dissipated power. A 1/4 W resistor at 0.25 W has no margin, so this load needs a 1/2 W part.

Energy over time

Since power is a rate, the total energy follows from how long the power lasts. For a resistor that dissipates a steady power for a fixed time, the energy it turns into heat is

E = P × t(4)

where E is the energy in joules, P is the power in watts and t is the time in seconds. The time has to be in seconds because a watt is a joule per second, so a 1 W resistor left on for an hour, which is 3600 s, turns 3600 J into heat.

Energy matters for batteries and for thermal mass. A small resistor heats up quickly because it holds little energy, whereas a large heater stores a lot and takes longer to cool. For the hands-on task in this lesson power is the quantity you need, and energy returns in lesson 15, where a battery's charge has to last through the losses of the regulator that feeds the board.

Power relates to energy as speed relates to distance. A car's speedometer shows km/h, the rate, while its trip counter adds up kilometres, the total, so watts correspond to the speedometer and joules or watt-hours to the trip counter. A battery's capacity is then the fuel tank, which says how far you can go, while the power says how fast you are using it up. The comparison breaks down for a resistor, which has no tank at all and simply turns whatever power reaches it into heat at once.

Worked examples of power dissipation

The first example takes the 100 Ω resistor from lesson 5, connected across the Pico's 5 V VBUS input, where the current is 0.050 A. Before you read the steps, estimate whether the power will be closer to a milliwatt, a quarter of a watt or a full watt.

  1. Known values. V = 5 V and I = 0.050 A, both from lesson 5.
  2. Choose the form. We know V and I, so equation (1) is the direct route: P = 5 V × 0.050 A.
  3. Multiply. 5 × 0.050 = 0.25, so P = 0.25 W.
  4. Check with a second form. Equation (2) gives P = I² × R = 0.050² × 100 = 0.0025 × 100 = 0.25 W. The two forms agree, as they must, because they are the same equation.

The second example is a linear regulator that takes 5 V in and gives 3.3 V out at 100 mA. The difference of 1.7 V is dropped across the regulator while the same current passes through it, so by equation (1) the regulator dissipates 1.7 V × 0.100 A = 0.17 W as heat. The load itself uses only 3.3 V × 0.100 A = 0.33 W, which means about a third of the power drawn from the supply is lost in the regulator. Lesson 15 explains why a switching regulator wastes much less power in the same job.

The third example is a printer heater, with illustrative numbers. A heater element is a resistor designed to get hot, so suppose a 24 V heater has a 5.8 Ω element, and equation (3) gives P = V² ÷ R = 576 ÷ 5.8 ≈ 99 W. These figures belong to no particular product. For a real heater, look up its voltage and resistance in its documentation and run the same calculation before you trust any number from a forum.

When you have a choice of formula, pick the one whose inputs you have measured or been given. Converting a current to a voltage first, then using P = V × I, is a common source of arithmetic slips.

Power ratings and derating

Knowing the power, we can now choose a part that tolerates it. A resistor's power rating is the continuous power it can dissipate without overheating. Common through-hole and chip resistors are sold with ratings such as 1/8 W, 1/4 W and 1/2 W, but the value that counts is the one in the datasheet for your exact part. That rating applies at a stated ambient temperature, the temperature of the air or board around the part, and above it the allowed power falls, which is called derating.

A board takes 12 V and needs 5 V at 0.5 A for its logic. A linear regulator does this by turning the difference into heat, (12 − 5) V × 0.5 A = 3.5 W, in a part the size of a fingernail. Without a heatsink it gets far too hot to touch, and a regulator with thermal protection will then shut itself down. Such boards therefore use a switching regulator for the large step and keep linear regulators for small drops at small currents (lesson 15).

Derating exists because a resistor's temperature depends on where it sits. A part on an open bench in cool air runs cooler than the same part in a sealed enclosure next to a heater, and a resistor on a dense board, surrounded by other parts that also produce heat, runs hotter than its own power alone would suggest. The datasheet gives a derating curve or table, and you need to read it for your own part instead of relying on a generic table found online.

A 1/4 W resistor carrying exactly 0.25 W is at its limit with no margin. It will run hot, its value can drift, and in a sealed printer enclosure it may fail. The usual practice is to choose a rating with at least a factor of two margin, and to check the derating curve if the part sits in a warm place.

Power and margin checks in C

Power is easy to compute in firmware, and real boards sometimes check it at run time. The example below computes the power in a resistor and then tests whether a chosen part has a margin of at least a factor of two. 

#include <stdio.h>

/* Power dissipated in a resistor, in watts: P = V x I. */
static float power_watts(float volts, float amps)
{
    return volts * amps;
}

/* Returns 1 if the rating is at least twice the dissipated power. */
static int rating_has_margin(float dissipated_w, float rated_w)
{
    return rated_w >= 2.0f * dissipated_w;
}

int main(void)
{
    float p = power_watts(5.0f, 0.050f);   /* 5 V across 100 ohm, from lesson 5 */
    printf("%.2f W\n", (double)p);
    printf("1/2 W rating ok: %d\n", rating_has_margin(p, 0.5f));
    printf("1/4 W rating ok: %d\n", rating_has_margin(p, 0.25f));
    return 0;
}

power_watts is equation (1) written as one line. It takes the voltage and the current as float values and returns their product in watts, and its name carries the unit, following the naming habit from lesson 5. The function is static because it is a helper used only in this file.

rating_has_margin compares the rated power with twice the dissipated power. The doubling is the margin, since a part rated at exactly the load has none, so the test asks for at least twice the load. The function returns 1 for true and 0 for false, the usual C convention for a yes-or-no answer when the code does not include stdbool.h.

In main, the first call computes 5 V times 0.050 A, which is 0.25 W, and printf prints it with two decimals. The next two calls test that 0.25 W load against a 1/2 W part and a 1/4 W part. The first passes because 0.5 W is at least twice 0.25 W, and the second fails because 0.25 W is not, so the program prints 1 and then 0, the same verdict a reviewer would reach.

Common mistakes in power calculations

The wrong form of the power formula

You know the voltage across a 1.5 kΩ resistor is 9 V, but you compute P = I² × R with a guessed current, and the power does not match a measurement. The cause is a formula chosen before checking which quantities you actually have. The fix is to write down the two known quantities first and pick the one form that uses them, which here is equation (3).

A missing kilo prefix

You compute 81 V² ÷ 1.5 and get 54 W, when the resistance is 1500 Ω and the power is 0.054 W. A power a thousand times too large should make you suspicious straight away, because a small resistor dissipating 54 W would glow. The fix is to convert to base units before you divide, as lesson 5 showed.

A nominal rating trusted over the real load

You see that a resistor is a 1/4 W part and assume that 0.2 W is fine, and then the part is warm to the touch on the bench and changes value after a long run. The rating has almost no margin at that load, and it may also be derated in a warm enclosure. The fix is to calculate the power from the real measured current, then apply the factor of two margin and the derating from the datasheet.

Energy written where power was meant

You write that a 0.25 W resistor "uses 0.25 J" without a time. An answer in joules with no duration attached means the question was about a rate and the answer is about a total. The fix is to keep power in watts and to multiply by a time, using equation (4), only when the question asks for energy.

Power budgets in real projects

Power is among the first calculations a hardware review asks for, covering every resistor in the current path, every regulator and the temperature of the hottest part. Teams that ship products keep a power budget for each rail, so that a change to one part cannot quietly overload another. When a board runs hot in the field, the first check is whether the measured power matches the calculated power, and that comparison usually finds the fault.

Heaters, motor drivers and LED strings cause most real heat problems. A 3D printer is a good example, since its hotend heater and bed heater are resistive loads that draw a large share of the printer's power, and the MOSFETs that switch them dissipate heat of their own while they conduct. Lesson 12 covers the MOSFET's on-resistance, which sets how much of that heat the switch produces, and the same accounting of power in and heat out applies to all of these parts.

Summary

  • Power is voltage times current, P = V × I, in watts. One watt is one joule per second.
  • Energy is power times time, E = P × t, in joules. The time must be in seconds.
  • With Ohm's law, the power in a resistor is also I² × R or V² ÷ R. Pick the form that uses the numbers you have.
  • Almost all the power in a resistor leaves as heat, and its temperature rises until heat out equals power in.
  • A power rating is given at a stated temperature, and the allowed power falls above it (derating).
  • Choose a rating with at least a factor of two margin over the calculated power, and read the datasheet for your exact part.
