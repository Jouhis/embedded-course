Power, energy, and heat

Every resistor and chip that carries current also warms up. Working out how much heat it makes is how you choose a part that survives.

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the power a resistor dissipates from its voltage, current or resistance, and choose a power rating with margin.

The idea

Power is the rate at which energy is used, measured in watts (W). For a component, P = V × I: volts times amperes. A resistor has no moving parts and does no useful work, so almost all of that power leaves as heat. The heat raises the resistor's temperature until the heat flowing out to the air and the board equals the power going in. Energy is power times time, measured in joules (J). One watt for one second is one joule. Your electricity bill uses watt-hours, which is the same idea over a longer time.

Ohm's law gives two more forms, so you can use whichever two quantities you know: P = I² × R and P = V² ÷ R. Work out which two you have, then pick the form that uses them.

Worked examples with real numbers

Example A, a 5 V USB load. The Pico's USB input is nominally 5 V. Put 100 Ω across it. The current is 0.050 A (lesson 5), so P = 5 V × 0.050 A = 0.25 W. Check it another way: P = I² × R = 0.050² × 100 = 0.25 W. The two forms agree, and they should.

Example B, a linear regulator. This is arithmetic, not a specific part. A regulator takes 5 V in and gives 3.3 V out at 100 mA. The difference, 1.7 V, is dropped across the regulator, so it dissipates 1.7 V × 0.100 A = 0.17 W as heat. Lesson 15 returns to this, because it is why a switching regulator runs cooler.

Example C, a printer heater (illustrative). Heater elements are resistors. A 24 V heater with a 5.8 Ω element dissipates P = V² ÷ R = 576 ÷ 5.8 ≈ 99 W. These are not the QIDI Q2's figures; look up your own heater's voltage and resistance in its documentation, and run the same sum.

Power ratings

A resistor's power rating is the heat it can dissipate continuously without overheating. Common through-hole and chip sizes are rated at 1/8 W, 1/4 W and 1/2 W, and the rating assumes an ambient temperature, commonly 70 °C for many chip resistors. Above that temperature the allowed power falls, which is called derating. Vishay's chip resistor family is a typical example, and its product pages give the derating rules. Vishay CRCW product information Use the rating from the datasheet of the exact part, not from a generic table. A part in a sealed enclosure, or next to a heater, runs hotter than one in open air.

A 1/4 W resistor carrying exactly 0.25 W is at its limit with no margin. It will run hot, its value can drift, and in a sealed printer enclosure it may fail. The usual practice is to choose a rating with at least a factor of two margin.

Model answer

Calculate the power from the measured voltage and current, or from V² ÷ R. Compare it with the rating from the datasheet, at the ambient temperature in the enclosure, and apply the derating. If it is close to or above the derated rating, increase the rating (for example from 1/4 W to 1/2 W or a larger case), or reduce the current or the voltage across it.
