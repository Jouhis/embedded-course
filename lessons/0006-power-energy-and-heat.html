Power, energy, and heat

Every resistor and chip that carries current also warms up. Working out how much heat it makes is how you choose a part that survives.

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the power a resistor dissipates from its voltage, current or resistance, and choose a power rating with margin.

Where we are and why it matters

Lesson 5 gave you voltage, current and resistance, and Ohm's law to link them. You worked out the current through a 100 Ω resistor on a 5 V supply and found 50 mA. That number tells you how much charge moves. This lesson answers the question that number leaves open: what happens to the resistor.

Heat is the failure mode that catches most beginners. A resistor that is too small for its job does not usually break on the first power-up. It warms up, its value drifts, a nearby capacitor ages faster, and after some weeks the board fails in the field. A heater in a 3D printer is the same story on a larger scale: the heater element is a resistor that is meant to get hot, and the wiring and the MOSFET that switches it must not get hot by accident. Choosing parts that survive means working out the heat before you build the board.

Power is the rate of using energy

Energy is the capacity to do work, measured in joules (J). Power is the rate at which energy is used, measured in watts (W), and one watt is one joule per second. The two are linked the way speed and distance are. Power tells you how fast the energy goes, and energy is what the power adds up to over time. Your electricity bill counts energy in kilowatt-hours, which is power multiplied by hours, not power on its own.

For an electrical component, the power is the voltage across it times the current through it:

P = V × I

The units make sense when you check them. A volt is one joule per coulomb, and an amp is one coulomb per second. Multiply them and the coulombs cancel, which leaves joules per second, or watts. You can check any power formula this way. If the units do not come out to watts, the formula is wrong.

A resistor does no useful work in a circuit. It exists to limit current or to make a voltage, and almost all of the power it takes from the supply leaves as heat. Some of the energy goes into the resistor, and the resistor's temperature rises until the heat flowing out to the air and the board equals the power coming in. At that point the temperature stops rising, and engineers call that state steady state.

Two more forms of the same power

Ohm's law from lesson 5 lets you swap one of the voltage or current terms for a resistance, so you can use whichever two quantities you know. Substitute V = I × R into P = V × I and you get P = I² × R. Substitute I = V ÷ R and you get P = V² ÷ R. All three forms give the same answer for the same resistor, so the choice depends only on which numbers you have measured or been given.

Use P = I² × R when you know the current, for example from a measurement with an ammeter in series. Use P = V² ÷ R when you know the voltage across the part and its resistance. Use P = V × I when you have measured both. The formula that uses the quantities you actually have is the one to pick, because every extra step is a chance to make an arithmetic error.

Energy: power over time

Power is a rate, and energy is the total. If a resistor dissipates a steady power P for a time t, the energy it turns into heat is E = P × t. The time must be in seconds, because a watt is a joule per second. One hour is 3600 seconds, so a 1 W resistor left on for an hour turns 3600 J into heat.

Energy matters for batteries and for thermal mass. A small resistor heats up quickly because it holds little energy, while a large heater holds a lot and takes longer to cool. For the hands-on task in this lesson, power is what you need. Energy becomes important in the battery lesson, lesson 15, where a regulator's losses are added up over a day.

Worked examples with real numbers

Example A: a 5 V USB load. Take the 100 Ω resistor from lesson 5 across the Pico's 5 V VBUS input. The current is 0.050 A, so the power is:

  1. Known values: V = 5 V and I = 0.050 A, from lesson 5.
  2. Apply P = V × I: P = 5 V × 0.050 A.
  3. Multiply: 5 × 0.050 = 0.25, so P = 0.25 W.
  4. Check with a second form: P = I² × R = 0.050² × 100 = 0.0025 × 100 = 0.25 W. The two forms agree, and they must, because they are the same equation.

Example B: a linear regulator. A linear regulator takes 5 V in and gives 3.3 V out at 100 mA. The difference, 1.7 V, is dropped across the regulator, and the current passes through it unchanged. The regulator therefore dissipates 1.7 V × 0.100 A = 0.17 W as heat. The load itself uses only 3.3 V × 0.100 A = 0.33 W. Lesson 15 explains why a switching regulator wastes much less power in the same job.

Example C: a printer heater (illustrative). A heater element is a resistor that is designed to get hot. Suppose a 24 V heater has a 5.8 Ω element. The power is P = V² ÷ R = 576 ÷ 5.8 ≈ 99 W. These are example figures, not any one product's. For a real heater, look up its voltage and resistance in its documentation, and run the same sum before you trust any number a forum gives you.

When you have a choice of formula, pick the one whose inputs you have measured or been given. Converting a current to a voltage first, then using P = V × I, is a common source of arithmetic slips.

The code: power and a margin check

Power is easy to compute in firmware, and a real board often checks it. The example below computes the power in a resistor and then asks whether a chosen part has a margin of at least a factor of two. 

#include <stdio.h>

/* Power dissipated in a resistor, in watts: P = V x I. */
static float power_watts(float volts, float amps)
{
    return volts * amps;
}

/* Returns 1 if the rating is at least twice the dissipated power. */
static int rating_has_margin(float dissipated_w, float rated_w)
{
    return rated_w >= 2.0f * dissipated_w;
}

int main(void)
{
    float p = power_watts(5.0f, 0.050f);   /* 5 V across 100 ohm, from lesson 5 */
    printf("%.2f W\n", (double)p);
    printf("1/2 W rating ok: %d\n", rating_has_margin(p, 0.5f));
    printf("1/4 W rating ok: %d\n", rating_has_margin(p, 0.25f));
    return 0;
}

power_watts is the formula P = V × I, written as one line. It takes the voltage and the current as float values and returns the product in watts. Its name carries the unit, which is the naming habit from lesson 5. The function is static because it is a helper used only in this file.

rating_has_margin compares the rated power with the dissipated power, doubled. Doubling the dissipated power is the margin: a part rated at exactly the load has no margin, so the test asks for at least twice the load. The function returns 1 for true and 0 for false, which is the usual C convention for a yes-or-no answer when the code does not include stdbool.h.

In main, the first call computes 5 V times 0.050 A, which is 0.25 W, and printf prints it with two decimals. The next two calls test the same 0.25 W load against a 1/2 W part and a 1/4 W part. The first passes, because 0.5 W is at least twice 0.25 W. The second fails, because 0.25 W is not at least twice 0.25 W. The program prints 1 and then 0, which is the verdict a reviewer would want.

Power ratings and derating

A resistor's power rating is the continuous power it can dissipate without overheating. Common through-hole and chip resistors are sold in ratings such as 1/8 W, 1/4 W and 1/2 W, but the value that matters is the one in the datasheet for your exact part. The rating is given at a stated ambient temperature, which is the temperature of the air or board around the part. Above that temperature the allowed power falls, and the fall is called derating.

The reason for derating is that a resistor's temperature is set by where it sits. A part on an open bench, in cool air, runs cooler than the same part in a sealed enclosure next to a heater. The datasheet gives a curve or a table, and you must read it for your part, not for a generic table you found online. A resistor on a dense board, with other parts that also produce heat, runs hotter than its own power alone would suggest.

A 1/4 W resistor carrying exactly 0.25 W is at its limit with no margin. It will run hot, its value can drift, and in a sealed printer enclosure it may fail. The usual practice is to choose a rating with at least a factor of two margin, and to check the derating curve if the part sits in a warm place.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

  1. Using the wrong form of Ohm's law. You know the voltage across a 1.5 kΩ resistor is 9 V and compute P = I² × R with a made-up current. The symptom is a power that does not match a measurement. The fix is to write down which two quantities you have, and pick the one form that uses them.
  2. Forgetting the kilo prefix. You compute 81 V² ÷ 1.5 and get 54 W, when the resistance is 1500 Ω and the power is 0.054 W. The symptom is a power a thousand times too large, which should make you suspicious straight away. The fix is to convert to base units before you divide, as lesson 5 showed.
  3. Trusting the nominal rating over the real load. You see that a resistor is a 1/4 W part and assume 0.2 W is fine. The symptom is a part that is warm to the touch on the bench and changes value after a long run. The fix is to calculate the power from the real measured current, then apply the factor of two margin and the derating from the datasheet.
  4. Using the energy when you meant the power. You write that a 0.25 W resistor "uses 0.25 J" without a time. The symptom is an answer in joules with no duration attached, which means the question was about the rate and the answer is about the total. The fix is to keep power in watts and multiply by the time only when the question asks for energy.

In real projects

Power is the first calculation most hardware reviews ask for. A design review asks for the power of every resistor in the current path, the power of every regulator, and the temperature of the hottest part. Teams that ship products keep a power budget for each rail, so a change to one part cannot silently overload another. When a board runs hot in the field, the first question is whether the measured power matches the calculated power, and that comparison usually finds the fault.

Heaters, motor drivers and LED strings are the parts that cause real heat problems. The 3D printer you own is an example: its hotend heater and its bed heater are resistive loads that draw a large share of the printer's power, and the MOSFETs that switch them dissipate their own heat while they conduct. Lesson 12 covers the MOSFET's on-resistance, which sets how much of that heat it makes. The same thinking, power in, heat out, applies to all of them.

In interviews, a heat question is a test of method. The interviewer wants you to say "measure the voltage and the current, compute the power, compare with the rating at the operating temperature, then add margin", in that order. A candidate who starts with the rating and never measures the load loses the point.

Summary

  • Power is voltage times current, P = V × I, in watts. One watt is one joule per second.
  • Energy is power times time, E = P × t, in joules. The time must be in seconds.
  • With Ohm's law, the power in a resistor is also I² × R or V² ÷ R. Pick the form that uses the numbers you have.
  • Almost all the power in a resistor leaves as heat, and its temperature rises until heat out equals power in.
  • A power rating is given at a stated temperature, and the allowed power falls above it (derating).
  • Choose a rating with at least a factor of two margin over the calculated power, and read the datasheet for your exact part.

Model answer

Calculate the power from the measured voltage and current, or from V² ÷ R. Compare it with the rating from the datasheet, at the ambient temperature in the enclosure, and apply the derating. If it is close to or above the derated rating, increase the rating (for example from 1/4 W to 1/2 W or a larger case), or reduce the current or the voltage across it.
