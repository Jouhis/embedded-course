Bench lab 18: The capstone on the bench

The filament-dryer controller ran on a simulated Blue Pill. Write a Pico 2 board file under the same HAL, then drive a real NTC and a low-voltage power resistor standing in for the heater, and watch the safety checks catch real faults.

This bench lab is optional, and a stretch. It needs a Pico 2, the OLED, two pushbuttons, a 10 kΩ NTC, a 10 kΩ resistor, a 22 Ω 5 W power resistor, a logic-level N-channel MOSFET (for example an IRLZ44N or an AO3400 module), a 100 Ω and a 10 kΩ resistor, an LED with 330 Ω for the fan, and heat-resistant tape. It doesn't count towards your progress. It's split into three parts of under an hour each.
By the end of this lab you can port a layered application to a new board by writing only its board file, and test its safety logic against physical faults on real hardware.
Low voltage only. The heater in this lab is a 22 Ω resistor on the USB's 5 V: about 230 mA and 1.1 W. It gets hot enough to burn a finger at the ABS setting, so don't touch it while it runs, and keep it off plastic surfaces. Never connect this circuit, or any heater, to mains voltage. A real dryer's mains heater needs a certified design, a thermal fuse and an enclosure, which this course doesn't cover.

Only one file changes

Lesson 113 split the dryer into layers: everything in app/ is portable C that talks to hardware only through hal.h, and lesson 115's hw/hal_bluepill.c implemented that header for the STM32F103. The point of that design was this moment. In projects/bench18-capstone-on-the-bench, app/ and main.c are lesson 115's, copied unchanged, and the build lists hw/hal_pico2.c instead of the Blue Pill file. That file is your job.

The HAL's contract matters more than its code. hal_adc_read_ntc() returns 0 to 4095 because ntc.c assumes a 12-bit full scale, and the Pico 2's ADC is also 12-bit, so the conversion is unchanged. hal_heater_set_permille(1000) must mean full, continuous power, as CCR above ARR did on the STM32; on the RP2350, a PWM level above the wrap value does the same. And hal_i2c_write() must return −1 on a NACK or a timeout, never hang, because the safety logic depends on the loop running.

Part 1: log, time, buttons and fan (about 40 minutes)

  1. Open the starter in Wokwi. It builds and runs: the stub board file returns 0 from the ADC, which ntc.c reads as a shorted sensor, so the dryer goes straight to a sensor fault. The log works because hal_log() and hal_millis() are already filled in.
  2. Implement hal_init()'s GPIO part, hal_button_pressed() and hal_fan_set(). Buttons are active low with pull-ups (pull-up, not pull-down: bench lab 2).
  3. Check them in Wokwi. The display only works after Part 3, so watch the fan LED and the log instead: the start button can't start a cycle while the sensor reads as faulty, but the fan follows the state machine.

Part 2: the NTC and the heater, in Wokwi and on the bench (about 50 minutes)

  1. Implement hal_adc_read_ntc(): adc_init() and adc_gpio_init(26) in hal_init(), then select input 0 and read.
  2. Implement the heater PWM: gpio_set_function(15, GPIO_FUNC_PWM), wrap 999, and a clock divider of clk_sys / (1000 × 1000), which is 150 at 150 MHz. Set the level to 0 before enabling the slice, so the heater never pulses at start-up.
  3. Run in Wokwi: start a PLA cycle, and the heater LED lights; drag the NTC's temperature above 46 °C and it goes out; below 44 °C it comes back (the 1 °C hysteresis of lesson 115's bang-bang control).
  4. On the bench: wire the NTC divider (10 kΩ from 3V3 to GP26, NTC from GP26 to AGND), and the heater: VBUS → 22 Ω → MOSFET drain; source → GND; gate ← 100 Ω ← GP15, with 10 kΩ from gate to GND so the heater stays off while the Pico boots. Tape the NTC to the power resistor's body. Fan LED: GP16 → 330 Ω → LED → GND.
  5. Flash, start a PLA cycle, and watch the log: the temperature climbs and settles in a band around 45 °C. Note how far above the band it overshoots: the resistor keeps heating the NTC after the PWM goes off, which a simulated NTC slider never does.

Part 3: the display and the safety checks (about 50 minutes)

  1. Implement hal_i2c_write() and hal_i2c_read() with i2c_write_timeout_us() and i2c_read_timeout_us(), and set up I2C0 at 100 kHz in hal_init(). Wire the OLED as in bench lab 6. The screen shows the state, the temperature and the preset.
  2. Sensor fault. During a cycle, pull the NTC's wire out of GP26. Within one control period, the heater must go off and the screen show the fault. Plug it back in: the fault stays latched. A start press clears it only once the sensor reads normally again and the part has cooled, as lesson 115's state machine does.
  3. Thermal runaway. Start a PETG cycle (65 °C), and once it's heating at full power, peel the NTC off the resistor and let it hang in air. The temperature stops rising, so the safety check sees full power without a 2 °C rise in 60 s and declares a runaway. In Wokwi you'd have to fake this by holding the slider still; here it's a real detached sensor, the exact field failure the check exists for.
  4. Leave a PLA cycle running for 10 minutes and note the band the temperature holds. Compare it with what the simulation showed.

The solution's board file is in solution/hw/hal_pico2.c; compare yours after each part, not before.

Common mistakes

  1. Changing app/ to fit the board. If the board file can't meet the HAL contract, that's a finding about the HAL. Write it down; don't patch around it in the application.
  2. No gate pull-down on the MOSFET. While the Pico boots, GP15 floats, and the heater may switch on. The 10 kΩ keeps it off until the firmware takes over.
  3. A MOSFET that isn't logic-level. A standard MOSFET needs about 10 V on its gate to switch fully; at 3.3 V it stays partly on and gets hot. Use one rated for low gate voltage.
  4. A blocking I2C call. i2c_write_blocking() can wait for ever on a stuck bus, which stops the control loop with the heater on. Use the timeout versions.
  5. The ADC channel and GPIO mixed up. adc_select_input(0) selects GP26; passing 26 selects nothing useful.

Summary

  • A clean HAL let the capstone move from an STM32 to an RP2350 by rewriting one file.
  • The HAL contract includes ranges and failure behaviour: 12-bit codes, 1000 per mille as full power, I2C that times out instead of hanging.
  • Real thermal systems overshoot: the heater keeps warming the sensor after the power goes off.
  • Physical fault tests (a pulled sensor, a detached sensor) exercise the safety logic in ways a slider can't.
  • Gate pull-downs and logic-level MOSFETs keep the heater off while the firmware isn't in control.
Your capstone runs on a second chip family with real heat, and its safety checks have caught real faults. That's a product prototype, built on an architecture that already proved it can move.
