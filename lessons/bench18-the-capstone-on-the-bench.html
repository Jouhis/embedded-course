Bench lab 18: The capstone on the bench

The filament-dryer controller on a real Pico 2

By the end of this lab you can port a layered application to a new board by writing only its board file, and test its safety logic against physical faults on real hardware.

The board file under the dryer's HAL

The filament-dryer controller ran on a simulated Blue Pill, and this lab writes a Pico 2 board file under the same HAL, then drives a real NTC and a low-voltage power resistor standing in for the heater and watches the safety checks catch real faults. The lab is optional and a stretch, needing a Pico 2, the OLED, two pushbuttons, a 10 kΩ NTC, a 10 kΩ resistor, a 22 Ω 5 W power resistor, a logic-level N-channel MOSFET (for example an IRLZ44N or an AO3400 module), a 100 Ω and a 10 kΩ resistor, an LED with 330 Ω for the fan, and heat-resistant tape. It does not count towards your progress, and it is split into three parts of under an hour each.

Low voltage only. The heater in this lab is a 22 Ω resistor on the USB's 5 V: about 230 mA and 1.1 W. It gets hot enough to burn a finger at the ABS setting, so don't touch it while it runs, and keep it off plastic surfaces. Never connect this circuit, or any heater, to mains voltage. A real dryer's mains heater needs a certified design, a thermal fuse and an enclosure, which this course doesn't cover.

Lesson 114 split the dryer into layers, everything in app/ being portable C that talks to hardware only through hal.h, and lesson 116's hw/hal_bluepill.c implemented that header for the STM32F103. That design was made for this moment. In projects/bench18-capstone-on-the-bench, app/ and main.c are lesson 116's, copied unchanged, and the build lists hw/hal_pico2.c instead of the Blue Pill file, which is the file you write.

The HAL's contract matters more than its code. hal_adc_read_ntc() returns 0 to 4095, because ntc.c assumes a 12-bit full scale, and since the Pico 2's ADC is also 12-bit, the conversion is unchanged. hal_heater_set_permille(1000) must mean full, continuous power, as CCR above ARR did on the STM32, which on the RP2350 a PWM level above the wrap value also does. hal_i2c_write() must return −1 on a NACK or a timeout and never hang, because the safety logic depends on the loop running.

Part 1: log, time, buttons and fan (about 40 minutes)

  1. Open the starter in Wokwi, which builds and runs. The stub board file returns 0 from the ADC, which ntc.c reads as a shorted sensor, so the dryer goes straight to a sensor fault, while the log works, because hal_log() and hal_millis() are already filled in.
  2. Implement hal_init()'s GPIO part, hal_button_pressed() and hal_fan_set(). The buttons are active low with pull-ups rather than pull-downs (bench lab 2).
  3. Check them in Wokwi. The display works only after Part 3, so watch the fan LED and the log instead, where the start button cannot start a cycle while the sensor reads as faulty, but the fan follows the state machine.

Part 2: the NTC and the heater, in Wokwi and on the bench (about 50 minutes)

  1. Implement hal_adc_read_ntc() with adc_init() and adc_gpio_init(26) in hal_init(), then select input 0 and read.
  2. Implement the heater PWM with gpio_set_function(15, GPIO_FUNC_PWM), wrap 999 and a clock divider of clk_sys ÷ (1000 × 1000), which is 150 at 150 MHz. Set the level to 0 before enabling the slice, so that the heater never pulses at start-up.
  3. Run in Wokwi and start a PLA cycle, and the heater LED lights. Dragging the NTC's temperature above 46 °C puts it out, and below 44 °C it comes back, the 1 °C hysteresis of lesson 116's bang-bang control.
  4. On the bench: wire the NTC divider (10 kΩ from 3V3 to GP26, NTC from GP26 to AGND) and the heater, VBUS → 22 Ω → MOSFET drain, source → GND and gate ← 100 Ω ← GP15, with 10 kΩ from gate to GND so that the heater stays off while the Pico boots. Tape the NTC to the power resistor's body, and wire the fan LED as GP16 → 330 Ω → LED → GND.
  5. Flash, start a PLA cycle and watch the log, in which the temperature climbs and settles in a band around 45 °C. Note how far above the band it overshoots, because the resistor keeps heating the NTC after the PWM goes off, which a simulated NTC slider never does.

Part 3: the display and the safety checks (about 50 minutes)

  1. Implement hal_i2c_write() and hal_i2c_read() with i2c_write_timeout_us() and i2c_read_timeout_us(), and set up I2C0 at 100 kHz in hal_init(). Wire the OLED as in bench lab 6, and the screen shows the state, the temperature and the preset.
  2. Sensor fault. During a cycle, pull the NTC's wire out of GP26, and within one control period the heater must go off and the screen show the fault. Plugging it back in leaves the fault latched, a start press clearing it only once the sensor reads normally again and the part has cooled, as lesson 116's state machine does.
  3. Thermal runaway. Start a PETG cycle (65 °C), and once it is heating at full power, peel the NTC off the resistor and let it hang in air. The temperature stops rising, so the safety check sees full power without a 2 °C rise in 60 s and declares a runaway. In Wokwi you would have to fake this by holding the slider still, whereas here it is a real detached sensor, the exact field failure the check exists for.
  4. Leave a PLA cycle running for 10 minutes, note the band the temperature holds and compare it with what the simulation showed.

The solution's board file is in solution/hw/hal_pico2.c, to compare with yours after each part rather than before.

Your capstone runs on a second chip family with real heat, and its safety checks have caught real faults, a product prototype built on an architecture that has already proved it can move.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

app/ changed to fit the board

The port works but the application now differs between boards, because the board file could not meet the HAL contract and the gap was patched in the application. Write the gap down as a finding about the HAL rather than patching around it.

No gate pull-down on the MOSFET

The heater switches on briefly at power-up, because GP15 floats while the Pico boots. The 10 kΩ from gate to GND keeps it off until the firmware takes over.

A MOSFET that is not logic-level

The MOSFET gets hot and the heater runs weakly, because a standard MOSFET needs about 10 V on its gate to switch fully, so at 3.3 V it stays partly on. Use one rated for low gate voltage.

A blocking I2C call

The control loop stops with the heater on, because i2c_write_blocking() can wait for ever on a stuck bus. Use the timeout versions.

The ADC channel and GPIO mixed up

The NTC reading makes no sense, because adc_select_input(0) selects GP26, whereas passing 26 selects nothing useful. Pass the channel number to the input selection.

Summary

  • A clean HAL let the capstone move from an STM32 to an RP2350 by rewriting one file.
  • The HAL contract includes ranges and failure behaviour, 12-bit codes, 1000 per mille as full power and I2C that times out instead of hanging.
  • Real thermal systems overshoot, because the heater keeps warming the sensor after the power goes off.
  • Physical fault tests, a pulled sensor and a detached sensor, exercise the safety logic in ways a slider cannot.
  • Gate pull-downs and logic-level MOSFETs keep the heater off while the firmware is not in control.
