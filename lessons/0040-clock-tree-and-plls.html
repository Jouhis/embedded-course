Clock tree and PLLs

The oscillators, multipliers and dividers that clock a chip

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the system clock of an RP2040 or an STM32F103 from its oscillator and PLL settings, and name the clock source each chip uses straight after reset.

One clock behind every timing number

Lesson 36 introduced wait states, the cycles a core spends waiting for flash that cannot answer as fast as it asks, and lesson 38 showed that a peripheral is a block of registers at a base address. Neither said where the clock comes from, although every peripheral needs one: the UART that printed values in lesson 38 divides the clock to obtain its baud rate, and the timers of later lessons count clock ticks, so a change of clock changes every one of those numbers.

A firmware engineer meets this on the first day with a new board. The datasheet offers a 12 MHz crystal, a 72 MHz target and registers with names such as PLLMUL and SWS, and these have to be turned into one number, the system clock, which is then checked on the chip. A team that cannot do this ends up with slow timing, wrong baud rates, and motors or displays that behave differently from build to build.

The structure of a clock tree

A clock tree is the set of oscillators, multipliers, dividers and selectors between a crystal and the logic that needs a clock, with each block doing one job. An oscillator produces a reference frequency, a PLL, or phase-locked loop, multiplies that reference up to a higher frequency, and dividers and selectors then choose what each block receives. The core runs from the system clock, while the peripherals run from their own clocks, which may be divided from the same source.

A clock tree works much like a car's drivetrain. The crystal is the engine turning at one steady speed, the PLL a gear that multiplies that speed for the core, and the dividers smaller gears that slow it down for each wheel, the peripherals. As in a car, top gear cannot be selected at any moment, because the flash needs its wait states set first or the core reads it faster than it can answer. The comparison fails in one respect, though: a gear change takes effect at once, whereas a PLL must lock onto its new frequency before its output can be used.

The reason this matters lies in how the core measures time, in clock cycles, so that a duration such as one microsecond is a number of cycles at a known frequency, and a wrong frequency makes every delay wrong by the same ratio. A delay loop sized for 500 ms at 8 MHz runs for 4 000 000 cycles, and if the clock becomes 72 MHz while the loop is unchanged, it finishes in 4 000 000 ÷ 72 000 000 s, about 56 ms.

Oscillators, PLL dividers and the STM32 names

Both chips use the same few terms. A crystal oscillator is a quartz crystal driven by a circuit that makes it vibrate at a fixed frequency; it is accurate, but it must be started and takes time to settle. A ring oscillator is a loop of logic gates that oscillates on its own, needing no external part, but its frequency moves with voltage and temperature and is only roughly known.

A PLL is described by three numbers. The reference frequency goes in, a feedback divider sets the multiplication by making the voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) run at the reference times the feedback value, which the PLL keeps locked, and one or more post-dividers divide the VCO output down to the frequency the chip uses. The VCO must stay within a fixed range, which the RP2040 datasheet gives as 750 to 1600 MHz (section 2.18.1), and it is an internal stage rather than the clock that drives the core.

The STM32 names the same pieces differently. Its internal 8 MHz RC oscillator is HSI, for high-speed internal, and its external crystal input HSE, for high-speed external. Its PLL takes either HSI divided by two or HSE, optionally divided by two, and multiplies it by a factor set in a 4-bit register field.

RP2040 clocks from the 12 MHz crystal

The RP2040 reference design uses a 12 MHz crystal, as the datasheet's crystal section states (section 2.15.2.2). The crystal oscillator (XOSC) is inactive at power-up, and software must enable it and wait for the XOSC_STABLE flag before using the crystal or starting the PLLs, for a physical reason given in the datasheet: before the flag is set, the output "may be non-existent or may have very short pulse widths which will corrupt logic if used" (section 2.15.2.2), leaving the logic nothing reliable to read.

Until then the ring oscillator runs the chip. The datasheet's boot sequence says the ROSC starts at power-up and that clk_sys and clk_ref run at a relatively low frequency, typically 6.5 MHz (section 2.7), while the ROSC section gives a typical startup frequency of 6 MHz within a range of 4 to 8 MHz, guaranteed to lie between 1.8 and 12 MHz (section 2.15.2.1). Both figures are typical values and do not contradict each other, but neither is a number to build a delay on.

Of the RP2040's two PLLs, PLL_SYS feeds the system clock, and section 2.18.2 of the datasheet gives its arithmetic. The output frequency is

fout = fref × FBDIV ÷ (POSTDIV1 × POSTDIV2)(1)

where fref is the reference, FBDIV the feedback divider and POSTDIV1 and POSTDIV2 the two post-dividers, with fref × FBDIV being the VCO frequency. With a 12 MHz reference FBDIV must stay between 63 and 133, giving a VCO between 756 and 1596 MHz. The datasheet's worked example sets FBDIV to 100 for a VCO of 12 × 100 = 1200 MHz and post-dividers of 6 and 2, so the output is 1200 ÷ 6 ÷ 2 = 100 MHz, while its jitter section (2.18.2.1) gives the 125 MHz case, a 1500 MHz VCO from FBDIV 125, divided by 6 and then by 2. The SDK's default system clock is also 125 MHz, since its platform definition sets SYS_CLK_HZ to 125000000 unless a project overrides it (src/rp2040/hardware_regs/include/hardware/platform_defs.h, pico-sdk 2.3.1).

The VCO runs at 1500 MHz, the largest number in the calculation, but the system clock is the output after the post-dividers, and beginners often stop at the VCO.

The clock table in section 2.15.3.1 gives the nominal clk_sys frequency as 125 MHz and notes that clk_sys and clk_peri have a maximum of 133 MHz across all process, voltage and temperature variations, adding that 200 MHz can be reached by running the core supply at an elevated voltage with the regulator set to 1.15 V. So 125 MHz is the normal operating point, and the datasheet limits mark its outer edges.

STM32F103 clocks from HSI and HSE

After every reset the STM32F1 runs from its internal 8 MHz oscillator, HSI, as the ST system file states in its header: "After each device reset the HSI (8 MHz) is used as system clock source" (third_party/cmsis-device-f1/Source/Templates/system_stm32f1xx.c). The same file initialises the variable SystemCoreClock to 8000000, so the value is correct before any code runs.

The system file's header says that SystemInit configures the clock before main() runs, but in this version of the file the code does not: SystemInit only writes the vector table offset register, and only when USER_VECT_TAB_ADDRESS is defined. Your own code must switch the clock, so read the function body as well as the comment above it.

Switching the clock is a sequence of register writes, and three fields of RCC_CFGR control the PLL, each defined in the header (third_party/cmsis-device-f1/Include/stm32f103xb.h):

  • PLLSRC, bit 16, chooses the PLL input, 0 for HSI divided by two and 1 for HSE.
  • PLLXTPRE, bit 17, divides HSE by two before the PLL when set.
  • PLLMUL, bits 21 to 18, is the multiplier field, whose factor the system file computes as the field value plus 2, so a field of 7 gives ×9, the value the header names RCC_CFGR_PLLMULL9 with the comment "PLL input clock*9".

The system file's SystemCoreClockUpdate() reads these bits back and gives the formulas for this chip: (HSI_VALUE >> 1U) * pllmull for the PLL with HSI, and HSE_VALUE * pllmull, or (HSE_VALUE >> 1U) * pllmull when PLLXTPRE is set, for the PLL with HSE. In general form,

SYSCLK = fin × (PLLMUL + 2)(2)

where fin is the PLL input after any divide by two and PLLMUL the field value. The F100 variant uses a different formula with a PREDIV1 divider, which is not the F103 path and must not be copied.

The header also states the frequency limits for this part, defining RCC_HSE_MIN as 4000000, RCC_HSE_MAX as 16000000 and RCC_MAX_FREQUENCY as 72000000 (stm32f103xb.h, lines 10168 to 10173). The highest system clock the header allows is therefore 72 MHz, and the Blue Pill's 8 MHz crystal lies within the HSE range.

Worked example: 8 MHz to 72 MHz on the Blue Pill

With an 8 MHz crystal on HSE, we work out what to write for a 72 MHz system clock, step by step because the order matters. Before reading on, work out the multiplier field value yourself.

  1. Choose the input. Use HSE with no pre-divide, so PLLSRC is 1, PLLXTPRE is 0 and the PLL input is 8 MHz.
  2. Choose the multiplier. 72 MHz out needs a factor of 72 ÷ 8 = 9, and since Equation 2 adds 2 to the field, the field must be 7. Binary 0111 in bits 21 to 18 is 0x7 << 18, or 0x001C0000, which matches the header's RCC_CFGR_PLLMULL9 mask.
  3. Check the result. The system file's HSE_VALUE * pllmull gives 8 000 000 × 9 = 72 000 000 Hz, exactly RCC_MAX_FREQUENCY, so no margin is left.
  4. Raise the flash wait states first. At 72 MHz the flash needs more wait states than at 8 MHz, set in the LATENCY field of FLASH_ACR, bits 2 to 0 in the header, which lesson 36 introduced. The HAL writes the new latency before raising the clock, with the comment "Increasing the number of wait states because of higher CPU frequency" (third_party/stm32f1xx-hal-driver/Src/stm32f1xx_hal_rcc.c, around line 830).
  5. Switch the system clock and wait. Turn on the PLL, wait until it is ready, then select it. The SWS field in RCC_CFGR reports the source in use, and since the header gives the PLL value as 0x00000008, wait until SWS reads that value.
  6. Update the variable. Call SystemCoreClockUpdate(), so that SystemCoreClock holds 72000000 and the baud-rate divisor and delay code use the new frequency.

The RP2040 case is simpler to check. By Equation 1, a 12 MHz crystal with FBDIV 125, POSTDIV1 6 and POSTDIV2 2 gives a VCO of 12 × 125 = 1500 MHz and an output of 1500 ÷ 6 ÷ 2 = 125 MHz, the SDK's default system clock.

The demo code line by line

The project projects/0040-clock-tree reads the clock state, and its key lines are these. 

USART1->BRR = SystemCoreClock / 115200U;           /* baud divisor from the clock */
print_line("SystemCoreClock (Hz): ", SystemCoreClock);
print_line("SWS (clock source):   ", (RCC->CFGR >> 2) & 0x3U);   /* 0 = HSI */
print_line("PLLMUL field:         ", (RCC->CFGR >> 18) & 0xFU);

The first line sets the UART baud divisor: BRR is the baud-rate register and the divisor is the system clock divided by the baud rate, so the clock value must be right before the UART starts. The second line prints the variable the timing code uses. The third reads RCC->CFGR, the clock configuration register, shifts it right by 2 and masks with 0x3U to keep the two-bit SWS field at bits 3 and 2, and the fourth shifts by 18 and masks four bits to extract the PLLMUL field. Each mask is as wide as its field in the header.

Every value passes through print_hex, so the serial monitor shows hex, and a line reading 0x007A1200 is 8000000 in decimal, which is 8 MHz.

Try it in Wokwi

The project projects/0040-clock-tree is a single demo that prints SystemCoreClock, the value of RCC_CFGR, the clock source bits and the PLL multiplier field. It does not change the clock, so what you check is the reset state.

  1. Open projects/0040-clock-tree in VS Code, build with Ctrl+Shift+B, and start the simulator with F1 → Wokwi: Start Simulator.
  2. Read the first line, SystemCoreClock (Hz): 0x007A1200, which is 8000000 in decimal, the HSI value. It should be followed by PLLMUL field: 0x00000000, meaning that no multiplier is selected.
  3. Read the SWS (clock source): 0x00000000 line, where zero means HSI, as the header defines RCC_CFGR_SWS_HSI, and explain why the PLL field reads zero although the chip has a PLL.
  4. Press F5 to debug, stop at main, and type print/x RCC->CFGR in the debug console, checking that bits 3 and 2 are zero, the SWS value above.
Hint

The SWS field is bits 3 and 2 of RCC_CFGR, so shift right by two and mask two bits; a value of 0 means HSI, 4 HSE and 8 PLL. The PLLMUL field is bits 21 to 18, which is zero while no multiplier is set.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

The VCO taken as the clock

On the RP2040 an LED blinks far too slowly, because the delay loops were sized for the 1500 MHz VCO while the real clock is 125 MHz, so each loop takes 12 times longer than intended. Divide by the post-dividers, since the system clock is the output after POSTDIV1 and POSTDIV2, 125 MHz in the example.

A faster clock before more flash wait states

The chip runs for a while, then executes wrong instructions or reads wrong data, and the fault changes with the build, which makes it hard to reproduce. Set LATENCY in FLASH_ACR first and switch the clock afterwards, in the order the HAL uses and following the one-way rule of lesson 36.

The clock changed without SystemCoreClock

The LED blinks at the right rate but the serial monitor shows garbage, because the baud divisor was computed from the old clock and the UART runs at the wrong speed. Call SystemCoreClockUpdate() after every clock change and compute the baud divisor afterwards, so that the divisor and the delays use the same number.

A clock source selected before it is ready

The core keeps running at 8 MHz after the switch is written, or it switches to a source still settling and the timing is wrong for a few milliseconds. Wait for each oscillator and the PLL to report ready, then check SWS to see which source is in use; the RP2040 needs the same wait for XOSC_STABLE, as section 2.15.2.2 says.

Clock setup in real projects

In a real project the clock setup is one of the first things main() does, and a code review checks it line by line. The reviewer asks whether the flash latency is raised before the clock, whether the source is checked after the switch, and whether SystemCoreClock is updated with every clock-dependent peripheral configured after that. Vendor code generators such as STM32CubeMX produce this sequence automatically, but a good engineer can still check their output against the register fields.

Summary

  • A clock tree consists of oscillators, PLLs, dividers and selectors, with the core running from the system clock and peripherals from their own clocks.
  • The RP2040 crystal is inactive at power-up and must not be used before XOSC_STABLE, with the ROSC running the chip until then.
  • The RP2040 output frequency is 12 MHz × FBDIV ÷ POSTDIV1 ÷ POSTDIV2, so the example 1500 ÷ 6 ÷ 2 gives 125 MHz.
  • The STM32F103 runs from HSI at 8 MHz after reset, and its PLL output is the input times the field plus 2, with HSE halved when PLLXTPRE is set.
  • The F103 header limits SYSCLK to 72 MHz (RCC_MAX_FREQUENCY).
  • Raise the flash wait states before raising the clock, and update SystemCoreClock after changing it.
  • Check the SWS field after each switch to confirm which source the core is really using.
