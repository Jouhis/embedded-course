Clock tree and PLLs

Every timing number in your firmware, from a 500 ms delay to a UART baud rate, is a division of one clock. Learn the tree once and you can check any of them.

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the system clock of an RP2040 or an STM32F103 from its oscillator and PLL settings, and name the clock source each chip uses straight after reset.

Where we are and why it matters

Lesson 36 introduced wait states: flash cannot always answer as fast as the core asks, so the core waits. Lesson 38 showed that a peripheral is a block of registers at a base address. Neither lesson said where the clock comes from, and every peripheral needs one. The UART you used to print values in lesson 38 divides the clock to get its baud rate, and the timers you will meet in later lessons count clock ticks. Change the clock, and every one of those numbers changes with it.

The problem this lesson solves is the one a firmware engineer meets on the first day with a new board. The datasheet offers a 12 MHz crystal, a 72 MHz target, and a list of registers with names like PLLMUL and SWS. You need to turn those into one number, the system clock, and check it on the chip. A firmware team that cannot do this has bugs that show up as slow timing, wrong baud rates, and motors or displays that behave differently at each build.

The idea: a tree of clocks

A clock tree is the set of oscillators, multipliers, dividers and selectors between a crystal and the logic that needs a clock. Each block has one job. An oscillator makes a reference frequency. A PLL, the phase-locked loop, multiplies that reference up to a higher frequency. Dividers and selectors then choose what each block receives. The core runs from the system clock, and the peripherals run from their own clocks, which may be divided from the same source.

A clock tree works like a car's drivetrain. The crystal is the engine, turning at one steady speed. The PLL is a gear that multiplies that speed for the core, and the dividers are smaller gears that slow it down for each wheel, the peripherals. Like a car, you cannot change to top gear whenever you like: the flash needs its wait states set first, or the core reads it faster than it can answer.

The architecture course gave you the reason this matters. The core counts time in clock cycles, so a time such as one microsecond is a number of cycles at a known frequency. If the frequency is wrong, every delay is wrong by the same ratio. Take a delay loop that was sized for 500 ms at 8 MHz, which is 4,000,000 cycles. If the clock becomes 72 MHz and the loop is unchanged, the same loop finishes in 4,000,000 ÷ 72,000,000 seconds, about 56 ms.

Four terms you need before the numbers

The two chips use the same few terms, so define them once. A crystal oscillator is a quartz crystal driven by a circuit that makes it vibrate at a fixed frequency. It is accurate, but it must be started and it takes time to settle. A ring oscillator is a loop of logic gates that oscillates on its own. It needs no external part, but its frequency moves with voltage and temperature, so it is only roughly known.

A PLL has three numbers. The reference frequency goes in. A feedback divider sets the multiplication: the voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) runs at the reference times the feedback value, and the PLL keeps that locked. Then one or more post-dividers divide the VCO output down to the frequency the chip uses. The VCO must stay within a fixed range. The RP2040 datasheet gives that range as 750 to 1600 MHz (section 2.18.1). The VCO is an internal stage, not the clock that drives the core.

The STM32 names the same pieces differently. Its internal 8 MHz RC oscillator is called HSI, for high-speed internal, and its external crystal input is HSE, for high-speed external. The PLL takes HSI divided by two, or HSE, optionally divided by two, and multiplies it by a factor that is set in a 4-bit register field.

RP2040: 12 MHz crystal, PLL to 125 MHz

The RP2040 reference design uses a 12 MHz crystal, and the datasheet states this in its crystal section (section 2.15.2.2). The crystal oscillator (XOSC) is inactive at power-up. Software must enable it and wait for the XOSC_STABLE flag before it uses the crystal or starts the PLLs. The datasheet's reason is physical: before the flag is set, the output "may be non-existent or may have very short pulse widths which will corrupt logic if used" (section 2.15.2.2). A short or missing pulse gives the logic nothing reliable to read.

What runs in the meantime? The ring oscillator. The datasheet's boot sequence says the ROSC is started at power-up and that clk_sys and clk_ref run at a relatively low frequency, typically 6.5 MHz (section 2.7). The ROSC section says the typical startup frequency is 6 MHz, in a range of 4 to 8 MHz, and that it is guaranteed to be between 1.8 and 12 MHz (section 2.15.2.1). The two numbers are both typical values, and they do not contradict each other. Neither one is a number you should build a delay on.

The RP2040 has two PLLs, and PLL_SYS feeds the system clock. The datasheet gives the arithmetic in section 2.18.2. With a 12 MHz reference, the feedback value (FBDIV) must stay between 63 and 133, which gives a VCO between 756 and 1596 MHz. The worked example there sets FBDIV to 100, so the VCO is 12 × 100 = 1200 MHz. Then POSTDIV1 is 6 and POSTDIV2 is 2, so the output is 1200 ÷ 6 ÷ 2 = 100 MHz. The jitter section (2.18.2.1) gives the 125 MHz case: a 1500 MHz VCO, which comes from FBDIV 125, divided by 6 and then by 2. The SDK's default system clock is 125 MHz as well. Its platform definition sets SYS_CLK_HZ to 125000000 unless a project overrides it (src/rp2040/hardware_regs/include/hardware/platform_defs.h, pico-sdk 2.3.1).

Notice the VCO. It runs at 1500 MHz, the largest number in the calculation, but the system clock is the output after the post-dividers. Beginners often stop at the VCO.

The clock table in section 2.15.3.1 gives the nominal frequency of clk_sys as 125 MHz. The same table notes that clk_sys and clk_peri have a maximum of 133 MHz across all process, voltage and temperature variations. It also says that 200 MHz can be reached by running the core supply at an elevated voltage and setting the regulator to 1.15 V. So 125 MHz is the normal operating point, and the datasheet limits are the outer edges of it.

STM32F103: HSI at reset, HSE through the PLL

After every reset the STM32F1 uses its internal 8 MHz oscillator, HSI, as the system clock. The ST system file says so in its header: "After each device reset the HSI (8 MHz) is used as system clock source" (third_party/cmsis-device-f1/Source/Templates/system_stm32f1xx.c). The same file starts the variable SystemCoreClock at 8000000, so the value is correct before any code runs.

The system file's header says that SystemInit configures the clock before main() runs. In this version of the file, the code does not. SystemInit only writes the vector table offset register, and only when USER_VECT_TAB_ADDRESS is defined. Your own code must switch the clock. Read the function body as well as the comment above it.

The clock is a sequence of register writes. Three fields of RCC_CFGR control the PLL, and the header defines each one (third_party/cmsis-device-f1/Include/stm32f103xb.h):

  • PLLSRC, bit 16, chooses the PLL input: 0 for HSI divided by two, 1 for HSE.
  • PLLXTPRE, bit 17, divides HSE by two before the PLL when set.
  • PLLMUL, bits 21 to 18, is the multiplier field. The system file computes the factor as the field value plus 2, so a field of 7 gives ×9. The header names that value RCC_CFGR_PLLMULL9, with the comment "PLL input clock*9".

The system file's SystemCoreClockUpdate() reads these bits back, and it gives the formulas for this chip. For the PLL with HSI, it uses (HSI_VALUE >> 1U) * pllmull. For the PLL with HSE, it uses HSE_VALUE * pllmull, or (HSE_VALUE >> 1U) * pllmull when PLLXTPRE is set. The F100 variant has a different formula with a PREDIV1 divider, and that is not the F103 path, so do not copy it.

The header also gives the frequency limits for this part. It defines RCC_HSE_MIN as 4000000 and RCC_HSE_MAX as 16000000, and RCC_MAX_FREQUENCY as 72000000 (stm32f103xb.h, lines 10168 to 10173). So 72 MHz is the highest system clock the header allows, and the Blue Pill's 8 MHz crystal fits the HSE range.

A worked example: 8 MHz to 72 MHz

Take the Blue Pill with an 8 MHz crystal on HSE, and work out what to write for a 72 MHz system clock. Show each step, because the order matters.

  1. Choose the input. Use HSE with no pre-divide, so PLLSRC is 1 and PLLXTPRE is 0. The PLL input is 8 MHz.
  2. Choose the multiplier. You need 72 MHz out, so the factor is 72 ÷ 8 = 9. The system file's rule is factor = field + 2, so the field must be 7. Binary 0111 in bits 21 to 18 is 0x7 << 18, which is 0x001C0000. That matches the header's RCC_CFGR_PLLMULL9 mask.
  3. Check the result with the formula. The system file gives HSE_VALUE * pllmull, which is 8,000,000 × 9 = 72,000,000 Hz. That equals RCC_MAX_FREQUENCY, so there is no margin left.
  4. Raise the flash wait states first. At 72 MHz the flash needs more wait states than at 8 MHz. Lesson 36 showed the LATENCY field of FLASH_ACR, which is bits 2 to 0 in the header. The HAL writes the new latency before it raises the clock, with the comment "Increasing the number of wait states because of higher CPU frequency" (third_party/stm32f1xx-hal-driver/Src/stm32f1xx_hal_rcc.c, around line 830).
  5. Switch the system clock and wait. Turn on the PLL, wait until it is ready, then select it. The SWS field in RCC_CFGR reports which source the core is using. The header gives the value for PLL as 0x00000008, so you wait until SWS reads that value.
  6. Update the variable. Call SystemCoreClockUpdate(), so that SystemCoreClock holds 72000000 and the baud-rate divisor and delay code use the new frequency.

The reverse for the RP2040 is simpler to check. A 12 MHz crystal, FBDIV 125, POSTDIV1 6 and POSTDIV2 2 gives 12 × 125 = 1500 MHz, then 1500 ÷ 6 ÷ 2 = 125 MHz, which is the system clock the SDK uses by default.

Then fix every red check: the APB1 limit and the flash wait states. The box at the bottom shows the register values that the code in the next section writes. Switch the chip to the RP2040 and make 125 MHz from the 12 MHz crystal, then try a VCO outside its range.

The code, line by line

The project projects/0040-clock-tree reads the clock state. Its key lines are these: 

USART1->BRR = SystemCoreClock / 115200U;           /* baud divisor from the clock */
print_line("SystemCoreClock (Hz): ", SystemCoreClock);
print_line("SWS (clock source):   ", (RCC->CFGR >> 2) & 0x3U);   /* 0 = HSI */
print_line("PLLMUL field:         ", (RCC->CFGR >> 18) & 0xFU);

The first line sets the UART baud divisor. BRR is the baud-rate register, and the divisor is the system clock divided by the baud rate, so the clock value has to be right before the UART starts. The second line prints the variable that the timing code uses. The third line reads RCC->CFGR, the clock configuration register, and shifts right by 2. Masking with 0x3U keeps two bits, which is the SWS field at bits 3 and 2. The fourth line shifts by 18 and masks four bits, which is the PLLMUL field. Each mask has a width that matches its field in the header.

Every value passes through print_hex, so the serial monitor shows hex. A line that reads 0x007A1200 is the number 8000000 in decimal, which is 8 MHz.

In real projects

In a real project the clock setup is one of the first things main() does, and a code review checks it line by line. The reviewer asks three questions. Is the flash latency raised before the clock? Is the source checked after the switch? Is SystemCoreClock updated, and is every peripheral that depends on the clock set up after that? Vendor code generators, such as STM32CubeMX, produce this sequence for you, and a good engineer can still check the output against the register fields.

Try it in Wokwi

The project projects/0040-clock-tree is a single demo. It prints SystemCoreClock, the value of RCC_CFGR, the clock source bits and the PLL multiplier field. The demo does not change the clock, so you are checking the reset state.

  1. Open projects/0040-clock-tree in VS Code, build with Ctrl+Shift+B, and start the simulator with F1 → Wokwi: Start Simulator.
  2. Read the first line, SystemCoreClock (Hz): 0x007A1200. That is 8000000 in decimal, the HSI value. The line PLLMUL field: 0x00000000 should follow, which means no multiplier is selected.
  3. Read the SWS (clock source): 0x00000000 line. Zero means HSI, as the header defines RCC_CFGR_SWS_HSI. Explain why the PLL field reads zero even though the chip has a PLL.
  4. Press F5 to debug, stop at main, and type print/x RCC->CFGR in the debug console. Check that bits 3 and 2 are zero, which is the SWS value in the line above.
Hint

The SWS field is bits 3 and 2 of RCC_CFGR, so shift right by two and mask two bits. A value of 0 means HSI, 4 means HSE and 8 means PLL. The PLLMUL field is bits 21 to 18, so it is zero when no multiplier is set.

Common mistakes

Mistake 1: using the VCO as the clock

Symptom: on the RP2040, you use the 1500 MHz VCO as the clock, so your delay loops assume 1500 MHz cycles per second. The real clock is 125 MHz, so each loop takes 12 times longer than intended, and the LED blinks far too slowly. Fix: divide by the post-dividers. The answer is the output after POSTDIV1 and POSTDIV2, which is 125 MHz in the example.

Mistake 2: switching to a faster clock before the flash waits go up

Symptom: the chip runs for a while, then executes wrong instructions or reads wrong data. The fault changes with the build, so it is hard to reproduce. Fix: set LATENCY in FLASH_ACR first, then switch the clock. The HAL does this in that order, and the lesson 36 rule is one-way: more clock means more wait states, set first.

Mistake 3: changing the clock but not SystemCoreClock

Symptom: the LED blinks at the right rate, but the serial monitor shows garbage. The baud divisor was computed from the old clock, so the UART runs at the wrong speed. Fix: call SystemCoreClockUpdate() after every clock change, and compute the baud divisor after that call. Then the divisor and the delays use the same number.

Mistake 4: selecting a clock source that is not ready

Symptom: you write the new source to the switch, and the core keeps running at 8 MHz. Or the core switches to a source that is still settling, and the timing is wrong for a few milliseconds. Fix: wait for each oscillator and the PLL to report ready, and then check SWS to see which source is in use. The RP2040 needs the same wait for XOSC_STABLE, as section 2.15.2.2 says.

Summary

  • A clock tree is oscillators, PLLs, dividers and selectors. The core runs from the system clock, and peripherals run from their own clocks.
  • On the RP2040, the crystal is inactive at power-up. Wait for XOSC_STABLE before you use it, and the ROSC runs until then.
  • On the RP2040, the output frequency is 12 MHz × FBDIV ÷ POSTDIV1 ÷ POSTDIV2. The example 1500 ÷ 6 ÷ 2 gives 125 MHz.
  • On the STM32F103, the system clock is HSI at 8 MHz after reset. The PLL output is the input times the field plus 2, with HSE divided by two if PLLXTPRE is set.
  • The F103 header limits SYSCLK to 72 MHz (RCC_MAX_FREQUENCY).
  • Raise the flash wait states before you raise the clock, and update SystemCoreClock after you change it.
  • Check the SWS field after each switch, so you know which source the core is really using.
