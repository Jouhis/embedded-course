Bench lab 1: Measure what you calculated

An LED circuit measured against its calculation

By the end of this lab you can measure a GPIO's output voltage, the LED's forward voltage and the current in the loop, and account for each gap between them and the lesson 9 calculation.

The calculation under test

Lesson 9 sized an LED resistor on paper, and this lab builds that circuit on a Pico 2, measures it with a multimeter and explains why the numbers come out different. It is optional, needing a Pico 2, a red LED, a 330 Ω resistor and a multimeter (see bench lab 0 for the kit), it does not count towards your progress, and it takes about 40 minutes.

Lesson 9 worked a red LED on a 3.3 V pin with a forward voltage of 2.0 V and a 4 mA target, so

R = (Vpin − Vf) ÷ I = (3.3 − 2.0) ÷ 0.004 = 325 Ω(1)

where Vpin is the pin voltage, Vf the LED's forward voltage and I the target current, rounded up to 330 Ω, with which the calculation predicts 1.3 V ÷ 330 Ω ≈ 3.9 mA.

That number rests on three assumptions, that the pin delivers exactly 3.3 V, that the LED drops exactly 2.0 V and that the resistor is exactly 330 Ω, all of which hold in Wokwi, because the simulator uses ideal parts, whereas on the bench none of them holds exactly, so this lab measures each one to show which assumption moves the answer most.

What the datasheet promises about the pin

A GPIO driving high is not a perfect 3.3 V source, because its output transistor has resistance, so the more current you draw, the lower the pin voltage, and the RP2350 datasheet states the floor, the output high voltage VOH being at least 2.62 V with IOVDD at 3.3 V while the pin sources the current of its drive-strength setting, 2, 4, 8 or 12 mA (RP2350 datasheet, section 14.9.4, Table 1683).

The drive strength is a pad setting rather than a current limit, choosing how strong the output transistor is, as the datasheet says directly, a higher drive strength keeping the pin closer to IOVDD for a given current, while you can draw more than the setting, although VOH is then no longer guaranteed (RP2350 datasheet, section 14.9.4). The SDK's default is 4 mA, and the same section caps the total current sourced by all GPIOs together at 100 mA.

The pin voltage therefore depends on the drive setting, so this lab's program cycles through all four, holding each for 10 s, so that you can read the meter.

On the board

Wire GP15 → 330 Ω → LED anode and the LED cathode → any GND pin, the cathode being the shorter leg, on the flat side of the rim, then flash the UF2 as in bench lab 0 and open the serial port, so that you know which drive setting is active.

There is no simulator stage in this lab, because Wokwi uses ideal parts, so the drive setting changes nothing there and every voltage is the nominal one. Before measuring, write down your three predictions from lesson 9, the pin voltage, the LED voltage and the current.

  1. Measure the resistor first, out of the circuit. Use the ohms range with the probes across the resistor before you plug it in, a 5 % resistor possibly reading anywhere from about 314 Ω to 347 Ω, and write the value down as Rmeas.
  2. Pin voltage. Use DC volts with the black probe on GND and the red probe on GP15 (touch the resistor leg next to it), recording the value for each drive setting as the log steps through them.
  3. LED voltage. Put the red probe on the anode and the black on the cathode, recording the value at the 4 mA setting.
  4. Resistor voltage. Put the probes across the resistor, recording the value at the 4 mA setting.
  5. Current, computed. Compute I = VR ÷ Rmeas, which avoids breaking the circuit to insert the meter.
  6. Current, measured (optional). Move the red lead to the meter's mA socket, unplug the LED's cathode from GND and put the meter in the gap, as lesson 10 showed, compare with the computed value and then put the red lead back in the V socket.
Never put the meter's probes across a supply while the red lead is in the mA or A socket: in that socket the meter is nearly a short circuit, and it blows its fuse (or worse). Move the lead back to V as soon as you finish the current measurement.

Reading your numbers

A typical bench result looks like the table below, yours differing in the second decimal, which is the point of measuring.

Pin voltage3.30 V3.2 V or soThe 3V3 rail itself is a little off nominal, and the output transistor drops some voltage under load
LED forward voltage2.0 V1.8 to 2.0 VVf varies with the LED, its colour, its temperature and the current
Resistor330 Ωwithin ±5 %The tolerance band on the body
Current3.9 mA4 to 4.5 mAA lower Vf leaves more voltage across the resistor

Kirchhoff's voltage law from lesson 7 checks the loop, Vpin having to equal VR + VLED within the meter's last digit, so if it does not, one of the readings was taken at a different drive setting than the others.

Across the four drive settings, the pin voltage changes only a little at about 4 mA, a few tens of millivolts at most, because every setting is comfortably strong enough for that load, the drive setting mattering when the load approaches or exceeds the setting's current and when edges must be fast, so for an LED on a resistor the default is fine.

The big lesson is the forward voltage, because a 0.2 V change in Vf is a 15 % change in the voltage across the resistor and so in the current, which makes the LED's assumption the least reliable of the three, why LED datasheets give Vf as a range and why designs needing an exact brightness drive LEDs from a constant-current source rather than a resistor.

You calculated a circuit, built it, measured it and explained every difference, a loop of predicting, measuring and explaining that is how hardware gets debugged.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

The resistor measured in the circuit

The resistance reading is wrong, because the meter's own test current flows through the LED and the pin too. Measure the resistor before you plug it in.

Readings from different drive settings

The loop does not add up under Kirchhoff's voltage law, because the setting changes every 10 s and the readings straddled a change. Take VR and VLED while the log shows the same line.

The red lead left in the mA socket

The next voltage measurement shorts what it touches, because in the mA socket the meter is nearly a short circuit. Move the lead back right after the current reading.

The LED in backwards

The LED stays dark, the pin reads near 3.3 V and the resistor 0 V, because almost no current flows through a reversed LED. Turn it round.

Summary

  • A calculation is only as good as its assumptions, and the pin voltage, forward voltage and resistance all differ from the nominal values on a real board.
  • The RP2350 guarantees VOH ≥ 2.62 V at 3.3 V IOVDD while sourcing the drive-strength current, typical pins doing much better at light loads.
  • Drive strength sets the output transistor's strength rather than a current limit.
  • The LED's forward voltage is the largest source of error for a resistor-limited LED.
  • Current measured as VR ÷ Rmeas avoids breaking the circuit, and KVL checks the loop.
RP2350 datasheet, section 14.9.4: Table 1683 (the GPIO DC characteristics) and the paragraphs after it on drive strength, VOH and the total IOVDD current limit.
