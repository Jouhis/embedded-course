Bench lab 1: Measure what you calculated

Lesson 9 sized an LED resistor on paper. Now build that circuit on a Pico 2, measure it with a multimeter, and explain why the numbers come out different.

This bench lab is optional. It needs a Pico 2, a red LED, a 330 Ω resistor and a multimeter (see bench lab 0 for the kit). It doesn't count towards your progress. Time: about 40 minutes.
By the end of this lab you can measure a GPIO's output voltage, the LED's forward voltage and the current in the loop, and account for each gap between them and the lesson 9 calculation.

The calculation you're checking

Lesson 9 worked a red LED on a 3.3 V pin: forward voltage 2.0 V, a 4 mA target, so R = (3.3 − 2.0) / 0.004 = 325 Ω, rounded up to 330 Ω. With 330 Ω the calculation predicts 1.3 V / 330 Ω ≈ 3.9 mA.

That number rests on three assumptions: the pin delivers exactly 3.3 V, the LED drops exactly 2.0 V, and the resistor is exactly 330 Ω. In Wokwi all three hold, because the simulator uses ideal parts. On the bench none of them holds exactly. This lab measures each one, so you can see which assumption moves the answer most.

What the datasheet promises about the pin

A GPIO driving high is not a perfect 3.3 V source. Its output transistor has resistance, so the more current you draw, the lower the pin voltage. The RP2350 datasheet states the floor: with IOVDD at 3.3 V, the output high voltage VOH is at least 2.62 V while the pin sources the current of its drive-strength setting, 2, 4, 8 or 12 mA (RP2350 datasheet, section 14.9.4, Table 1683).

The drive strength is a pad setting, not a current limit. It chooses how strong the output transistor is. The datasheet says so directly: a higher drive strength keeps the pin closer to IOVDD for a given current, and you can draw more than the setting, but then VOH is no longer guaranteed (RP2350 datasheet, section 14.9.4). The SDK's default is 4 mA. The same section caps the total current sourced by all GPIOs together at 100 mA.

So the pin voltage depends on the drive setting, and this lab's program cycles through all four, holding each for 10 s so you can read the meter.

On the board

Wire GP15 → 330 Ω → LED anode, LED cathode → any GND pin. The LED's cathode is the shorter leg, on the flat side of the rim. Flash the UF2 as in bench lab 0 and open the serial port so you know which drive setting is active.

There's no simulator stage in this lab: Wokwi uses ideal parts, so the drive setting changes nothing there and every voltage is the nominal one. Before you measure, write down your three predictions from lesson 9: pin voltage, LED voltage and current.

  1. Measure the resistor first, out of the circuit. Ohms range, probes across the resistor before you plug it in. A 5 % resistor may read anywhere from about 314 Ω to 347 Ω. Write it down as Rmeas.
  2. Pin voltage. DC volts, black probe on GND, red probe on GP15 (touch the resistor leg next to it). Record it for each drive setting as the log steps through them.
  3. LED voltage. Red probe on the anode, black on the cathode. Record it at the 4 mA setting.
  4. Resistor voltage. Probes across the resistor. Record it at the 4 mA setting.
  5. Current, computed: I = VR / Rmeas. This avoids breaking the circuit to insert the meter.
  6. Current, measured (optional): move the red lead to the meter's mA socket, unplug the LED's cathode from GND, and put the meter in the gap, as lesson 10 showed. Compare with the computed value, then put the red lead back in the V socket.
Never put the meter's probes across a supply while the red lead is in the mA or A socket: in that socket the meter is nearly a short circuit, and it blows its fuse (or worse). Move the lead back to V as soon as you finish the current measurement.

Reading your numbers

A typical bench result looks like this; yours will differ in the second decimal, which is the point of measuring.

QuantityLesson 9 assumedTypical measurementWhy it differs
Pin voltage3.30 V3.2 V or soThe 3V3 rail itself is a little off nominal, and the output transistor drops some voltage under load
LED forward voltage2.0 V1.8 to 2.0 VVf varies with the LED, its colour, its temperature and the current
Resistor330 Ωwithin ±5 %The tolerance band on the body
Current3.9 mA4 to 4.5 mAA lower Vf leaves more voltage across the resistor

Check the loop with Kirchhoff's voltage law from lesson 7: Vpin should equal VR + VLED within the meter's last digit. If it doesn't, one of the readings was taken at a different drive setting than the others.

Then look at the pin voltage across the four drive settings. At about 4 mA the change is small, a few tens of millivolts at most, because every setting is comfortably strong enough for that load. The drive setting matters when the load approaches or exceeds the setting's current, and when edges must be fast. For an LED on a resistor, the default is fine.

The big lesson is the forward voltage. A 0.2 V change in Vf is a 15 % change in the voltage across the resistor, and so in the current. Of the three assumptions, the LED's is the least reliable, which is why LED datasheets give Vf as a range, and why designs that need an exact brightness drive LEDs from a constant-current source rather than a resistor.

You calculated a circuit, built it, measured it, and explained every difference. That loop, predict, measure, explain, is how hardware gets debugged.

Common mistakes
  1. Measuring the resistor in the circuit. The meter's own test current flows through the LED and the pin too, and the reading is wrong. Measure it before you plug it in.
  2. Reading values at different drive settings. The setting changes every 10 s; take VR and VLED while the log shows the same line.
  3. Leaving the red lead in the mA socket. The next voltage measurement shorts what it touches. Move it back right after the current reading.
  4. LED in backwards. It stays dark, the pin reads near 3.3 V and the resistor reads 0 V, since almost no current flows. Turn it round.

Summary

  • A calculation is only as good as its assumptions: pin voltage, forward voltage and resistance all differ from the nominal values on a real board.
  • The RP2350 guarantees VOH ≥ 2.62 V at 3.3 V IOVDD while sourcing the drive-strength current; typical pins do much better at light loads.
  • Drive strength sets the output transistor's strength, not a current limit.
  • The LED's forward voltage is the largest source of error for a resistor-limited LED.
  • Measure current as VR / Rmeas to avoid breaking the circuit; check the loop with KVL.
RP2350 datasheet, section 14.9.4: Table 1683 (the GPIO DC characteristics) and the paragraphs after it on drive strength, VOH and the total IOVDD current limit.
