How this course works

The study method and the structure of the course

By the end of this lesson you can plan a study session that tries a task before reading its solution, retrieves from memory, and returns to earlier lessons.

A study method for a long course

Lessons 1 to 3 gave you a toolchain, a first program running on a simulated chip and a picture of the work the course prepares you for. This lesson sets out the method for everything that follows, including the labs and the checkpoints. The material becomes harder quickly: binary masks arrive by lesson 20, interrupts by lesson 57 and a real-time operating system by lesson 86. Each of these is easy to read and hard to remember unless it is studied the right way.

Most people study by rereading. They open the lesson again, recognise the familiar parts and move on, which feels productive but is one of the least effective habits in learning. The method described here is slower in the moment and much faster over months, because you meet the same ideas several times with gaps in between and are asked to produce answers rather than recognise them. Each habit below supports that.

Retrieval practice

Retrieval practice means bringing an answer out of your memory instead of reading it again. Every quiz in this course is a retrieval task: you see a question, produce an answer, and only then read the reasoning. The research summary on retrievalpractice.org states the problem plainly: "Easy learning leads to easy forgetting." Rereading feels productive because the material is familiar, but familiar is not the same as remembered.

Retrieval works because producing an answer takes effort, and that effort changes the memory. When you struggle to recall a fact and then check it, the corrected version is stored more firmly than a fact you simply read. Rereading skips the struggle, so the page does the remembering and your memory is never asked to do anything.

Fluency and storage strength

Two different things can pass for learning, and telling them apart matters for the rest of this lesson. Fluency is how easily you perform or recall something right now, while storage strength is how well it is learned, and it is the part that lasts. Easy practice raises fluency quickly, often without much storage behind it, which is why you can answer a question with the page open and fail the same question a week later.

Robert and Elizabeth Bjork describe this split in their "new theory of disuse", in which fluency and storage are separate measures. Their 2020 paper, Desirable difficulties in theory and practice, is the primary reference if you want the theory in the authors' own words.

The practical rule follows directly. An answer you have to struggle for, and then check, builds more storage than one read straight off the page. A quiz answered with the explainer open is therefore only a fluency check, which is why the course asks you to answer each quiz before rereading the explainer.

Desirable difficulties

A desirable difficulty, a term the Bjorks coined, is a condition that makes practice harder in the moment and learning more durable. Retrieval, spacing and interleaving, described next, are all desirable difficulties. A session in which every answer comes easily usually stores little, whereas a session in which you miss half the questions and check each miss is often the better one, even though it feels worse.

The difficulty must, however, come from the retrieval and not from confusion about the task. If you cannot even start because the instructions are unclear, that is a problem to fix rather than a useful struggle. Reading the goal first ensures you know what you are trying to do.

Watching someone lift weights does not make you stronger. Rereading a lesson is the watching, and answering a question from memory is the lifting, which feels heavier precisely because it is doing the work. The comparison also shows the limit: a weight far too heavy simply fails, so if a question is well beyond you, go back to the section first and then try again.

Spaced review

Spaced review returns to earlier material after a gap, so that the memory is rebuilt each time. Practising a skill today, again in three days and again in a week requires a fresh retrieval at each return, and each of those strengthens the memory more than the same practice crammed into one day. The gap is essential, since the memory has to fade a little before rebuilding it does any good.

The course builds this review in. From lesson 5 on, each lesson ends with a short "Spaced review" quiz drawn from lessons at least two back. You do not need to memorise any schedule, only to answer the quiz and notice what you cannot recall. Reviewing each lesson once a few days later and once more after a week works well, and that is the rhythm the spaced review follows.

  1. You learn something on day 0. Without review it fades fast (the dashed line): by day 3 there is little left to recall.
  2. On day 1 you retrieve it, with effort, and check the answer. The memory is rebuilt, and this time it fades more slowly.
  3. A second review on day 3, after a longer gap, rebuilds it again. Each gap can be longer than the last.
  4. After a review on day 7 the memory lasts for weeks. Three short reviews did more than three rereads on day 0. The curve shapes illustrate the idea; they are not measurements.

Interleaving

Interleaving mixes topics within one session, so that each problem forces you to choose the right method. Drilling a single topic until it feels smooth gives a pleasant sense of progress and a narrow skill. When topics are mixed, the question of which method a problem needs comes up every time, and answering it is a retrieval step in its own right.

Consider two ways to practise the same six questions. In blocked practice you answer two on resistor dividers, then two on diodes, then two on registers, and each block feels smooth because the method is fresh. In mixed practice the same six questions arrive in random order, so you must decide the method each time, which is harder and also far closer to real work.

Metacognition

Metacognition is thinking about your own thinking, which in practice means checking whether you actually know something before assuming you do. It is the skill that tells you which lesson to review, and the Retrieval Practice site lists it alongside retrieval and interleaving as a related strategy.

The simplest way to practise it is to write down three things from memory at the end of each lesson and then check them against the page. If you cannot write three things without looking, you know exactly what to review. The check takes a minute and tells you far more than a feeling of confidence.

Worked example: planning a 40-minute session

The plan below applies all of these habits to a session on lesson 5. The times are a suggestion that you can adjust, but the order of the steps matters, and the third column explains why each step sits where it does.

TimeStepWhy this order
0:00 to 0:05Read the goal, and nothing elseYou know what you are aiming for before you start, so the attempt has a target.
0:05 to 0:20Try the task or the first part of the explainer's exercise, without the hintThe attempt is the retrieval. The struggle is the point, and the hint comes after.
0:20 to 0:25Answer the quiz from memory, then read each reasonYour answers show what you actually stored, not what you can recognise on the page.
0:25 to 0:30Reread only the parts you got wrongTargeted rereading after a failed retrieval is worth more than a full reread.
0:30 to 0:35Write three things from memory, then checkThe check shows what you know and what to review next time.
0:35 to 0:40Answer two or three spaced-review questions from lessons 1 to 3Returning to older material after a gap is the spacing that keeps it.

It is worth noticing what the plan leaves out. There is no step where you read the whole explainer from the top and no step where you watch someone else do the task. Those activities feel like learning, and they are exactly what this method replaces.

Course structure and materials

The course consists of plain HTML pages, a simulator for the hands-on work and a few supporting folders. The table shows where each piece lives and what it is for.

PieceWhat it is for
The index (index.html)Every part and lesson in order. Lessons you have marked complete show a tick, and each part shows how many are done. The progress is stored in this browser.
Lesson pages (lessons/)The explainer, the hands-on task, a quiz, a primary source, and a prompt for your tutor. Each lesson ends with a "Mark lesson complete" bar.
Projects: projects/<id>/starter and solutionThe Wokwi projects for each hands-on lesson. Starter is for you; solution is for comparison.
Reference sheets (reference/index.html)Dense, printable summaries of formulas, registers and APIs, to look things up quickly.
Glossary (reference/glossary.html)The course's terms, each defined once. Lessons use these words exactly.
Learning records (learning-records/)Your progress notes, so the tutor knows what to review and what to skip. The folder is not created by the course; the tutor starts it when you first record progress, and if it is missing you can ask for it.

Every lesson follows the same order: an opening that links back to earlier lessons, the ideas one at a time, a worked example, the code, and the hands-on task with its win. After that come folded sections that you open when you need them. Common mistakes, tagged When stuck, helps when the task does not work, and sections tagged Go deeper, such as industry practice, are meant for a second read. The summary and the questions close the lesson, with the questions shown one at a time as cards and a dot per question that turns green or red when you answer.

Two kinds of box appear inside the text. A box headed Picture it gives an everyday comparison for a hard idea and says where the comparison stops working, and a box headed In a real device shows the idea inside a product.

Each part ends with a checkpoint, placed before the part's lab when it has one. It is a quick multiple-choice test, one question at a time, with an I don't know button. Each question quietly draws on one to three lessons, so the result estimates how well each lesson has stuck and lists the ones to go over again before the next part, and the course index then marks every lesson with its result. Each checkpoint also has a bank of about ten questions per lesson, so you can build your own test on chosen lessons, for example only those still marked shaky.

The questions at the end of each lesson are optional, since the checkpoint is what tests you. They are still worth the few minutes, because recalling an idea right after reading it, and again a few lessons later, makes it last much longer than reading it twice. If a question surprises you, read that section again before moving on.

Your marks are saved in the browser you use for the course, so they stay on that device. Export progress on the index saves them to a file, which is a sensible backup before you change browsers.

Keeping your work on a git branch

Your exercise work lives in the course folder, while the course itself keeps receiving fixes. To keep the two apart, work on a git branch of your own: run git switch -c my-work once, then commit whenever you finish an exercise. Each commit also serves as a note to your future self about what you tried.

To update the course, commit first, then run git switch main, git pull, git switch my-work and git merge main. Afterwards, the VERSION file in the course folder should match the version shown under the title of the index page. If it does not, the update did not happen, and a starter may then look broken when it is not. The README section "Keep your work on your own branch" has the details.

The AI agent as a tutor

The AI agent in this workspace acts as your tutor. It can quiz you, explain a point again or set an extra exercise, and once learning records exist it can read them too. Some prompts to start with:

  • "Quiz me on lessons 5 to 8, mixing the topics."
  • "Explain this register again, using the bus and memory, not a formula."
  • "Give me an extra exercise on the resistor from lesson 2."

Ask the tutor to quiz you rather than to tell you the answer. An answer given straight away removes the retrieval step, which is the step that builds storage, so when you catch yourself asking for one, try the question once more from memory first.

A study routine

  1. Read the goal. Know what you are trying to be able to do before you start.
  2. Try the task first. Make a real attempt before opening a hint or the solution. Set a timer for about 15 minutes and write down where you got stuck.
  3. Answer the quiz from memory. Do it before rereading the explainer, so the answers you give are your own.
  4. Write three things from memory at the end of the lesson, then check them against the page.
  5. Come back in a few days. Do the spaced-review quiz in a later lesson, or ask the tutor to quiz you on this one.
You have a plan for studying this course: try first, answer from memory, and come back to earlier lessons. You will use it in every lesson from here on.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

Reading the solution before trying

The solution looks obvious when you read it, yet you cannot write the first line in a blank file. You recognised the answer instead of producing it. Make a real attempt during the timed block, even a wrong one, and compare only afterwards.

Rereading instead of answering

The quiz feels easy because you have just read the answers, and a week later the topic is gone. The explainer was on screen while you answered, so the quiz only measured fluency. Close the explainer, answer the quiz, and then reread only what you got wrong.

Cramming into one long session

You finish a four-hour session feeling like an expert, and the following week you cannot reproduce the code. All the practice happened in one place, so the memory faded before any review. Spread the same time over several shorter sessions with gaps between them.

Drilling one topic until it feels smooth

You score well on a block of similar questions and then fail a mixed quiz, because you learned the method for that block but not how to choose a method. Mix the topics, and ask the tutor for a mixed quiz.

Moving past a wrong answer

The same mistake returns in a later quiz. You read the reason and moved on without retrieving the right answer yourself. After each wrong answer, write the correct reason in your own words and keep the note in the learning-records folder.

How working engineers keep learning

Working firmware engineers learn in much the same way, although they rarely call it that. They approach a new microcontroller family by trying a small task first and then reading the reference manual for the part where they got stuck. Many keep a notebook of things they had to look up twice, which amounts to the three-things habit. Teams also review each other's work and write short post-mortems after a failure, which is a form of retrieval, since the team has to reconstruct what went wrong from memory before the report is written.

The course rests on the idea that embedded engineering is best learned by building things, watching them fail and fixing them. The simulator makes failure cheap enough to happen often, and the spaced review keeps earlier work alive while you move on.

  • Spaced review returns to earlier material after a gap, so each return is a fresh retrieval.
  • Interleaving mixes topics, so you must choose the method each time.
