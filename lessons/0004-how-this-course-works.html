How this course works

The method behind every lesson: try before you look, answer from memory, come back later, and mix the topics.

By the end of this lesson you can plan a study session that tries a task before reading its solution, retrieves from memory, and returns to earlier lessons.

Where we are and why it matters

Lessons 1 to 3 gave you a toolchain, a first program running on a simulated chip, and a picture of the jobs the course prepares you for. This lesson gives you the method for the rest of the lessons, the labs and the checkpoints. The content will get harder: by lesson 20 you will be working with binary masks, by lesson 57 with interrupts, and by lesson 86 with a real-time operating system. Each of those is easy to read and hard to remember unless you study it the right way.

The usual way people study is rereading. They open the lesson, read it again, nod at the parts that look familiar, and move on. That feels productive, and it is one of the least effective habits in learning. The method in this lesson is slower in the moment and faster over months. You will meet the same ideas several times, with a gap between, and you will be asked to produce the answer rather than recognise it. Each habit below supports that.

Retrieval practice

Retrieval practice means bringing an answer out of your memory, rather than reading it again. Every quiz in this course is a retrieval task. You see a question, you produce an answer, and only then do you read the reason. The research summary on retrievalpractice.org puts the problem plainly: "Easy learning leads to easy forgetting." Rereading feels productive because the material is familiar. Familiar is not the same as remembered.

The reason retrieval works is that producing an answer is effortful, and the effort changes the memory. When you struggle to recall a fact and then check it, the correction is stored more firmly than a fact you simply read. Rereading skips the struggle, so the page does the remembering for you, and your memory is not asked to do anything.

Fluency and storage

Two things can be mistaken for learning, and the difference matters for the rest of this lesson. Fluency is how easily you perform or recall something right now. Storage strength is how well it is learned, and it is what lasts. Practice that is easy builds fluency fast, and fluency can rise without much storage behind it. You can answer a question while the page is open and fail the same question a week later.

Robert and Elizabeth Bjork describe this split in their "new theory of disuse", in which fluency and storage are separate measures. Their 2020 paper, Desirable difficulties in theory and practice, is the primary reference; read it if you want the theory in the authors' words.

The practical rule follows directly. An answer you struggle to reach, and then check, builds more storage than one you read straight off the page. A quiz you can answer with the explainer open is a fluency check, not a storage check. That is why the course asks you to answer the quiz before you reread the explainer.

Desirable difficulty

A desirable difficulty is a condition that makes practice harder in the moment and makes learning last longer. The Bjorks coined the term. Retrieval, spacing and interleaving, which come next, are all desirable difficulties. A session where every answer comes easily is a sign that little is being stored, and a session where you miss half the questions and check each miss is often a better session, even though it feels worse.

Be careful with the word difficult. The difficulty must come from the retrieval, not from confusion about what the task is. If you cannot start the task because the instructions are unclear, that is a problem to fix, not a useful struggle. Read the goal first, so you know what you are trying to do.

Watching someone lift weights does not make you stronger. Re-reading a lesson is watching; answering a question from memory is lifting. The lift feels heavier, and that effort is what builds the muscle. The picture also shows the limit: a weight far too heavy just fails. If a question is far beyond you, go back to the section first, then try again.

Spaced review

Spaced review returns to earlier material after a gap, so the memory is rebuilt each time. If you practise a skill today, then again in three days and again in a week, each return requires a fresh retrieval, and each retrieval strengthens the memory more than the same amount of practice crammed into one day. The gap is the point: the memory has to fade a little before you rebuild it.

The course builds in this review. From lesson 5 on, each lesson has a short "Spaced review" quiz with questions from lessons at least two back. You do not need to memorise the schedule. You need to answer the quiz and to notice what you cannot recall. A plan that works is to review each lesson once a few days later and once more after a week, which is the rhythm the spaced review quiz follows.

  1. You learn something on day 0. Without review it fades fast (the dashed line): by day 3 there is little left to recall.
  2. On day 1 you retrieve it, with effort, and check the answer. The memory is rebuilt, and this time it fades more slowly.
  3. A second review on day 3, after a longer gap, rebuilds it again. Each gap can be longer than the last.
  4. After a review on day 7 the memory lasts for weeks. Three short reviews did more than three rereads on day 0. The curve shapes illustrate the idea; they are not measurements.

Interleaving

Interleaving mixes topics in one session, so that each problem makes you choose the right method. Drilling one topic until it feels smooth gives you a good feeling and a narrow skill. Mixing topics means that the question "which method does this need?" comes up every time, and answering it is a retrieval step in its own right.

Compare two ways to practise the same six questions. In blocked practice you answer two questions on resistor dividers, then two on diodes, then two on registers. Each block feels smooth, because the method is fresh. In mixed practice the same six questions come in a random order, and you must decide the method each time.

Metacognition: knowing what you know

Metacognition is thinking about your own thinking: noticing whether you actually know something before you assume you do. It is the skill that tells you which lesson to review. The Retrieval Practice site lists metacognition alongside retrieval and interleaving as related strategies.

The easiest way to practise metacognition is to write three things from memory at the end of each lesson, then check them against the page. If you cannot write three things without looking, you know exactly what to review. The checks are cheap, and they tell you more than a feeling of confidence does.

A worked example: planning one 40-minute session

Here is a session for lesson 5, planned with all of the habits above. The times are a plan, not a rule, and you can change them, but the order matters.

TimeStepWhy this order
0:00 to 0:05Read the goal, and nothing elseYou know what you are aiming for before you start, so the attempt has a target.
0:05 to 0:20Try the task or the first part of the explainer's exercise, without the hintThe attempt is the retrieval. The struggle is the point, and the hint comes after.
0:20 to 0:25Answer the quiz from memory, then read each reasonYour answers show what you actually stored, not what you can recognise on the page.
0:25 to 0:30Reread only the parts you got wrongTargeted rereading after a failed retrieval is worth more than a full reread.
0:30 to 0:35Write three things from memory, then checkThe check shows what you know and what to review next time.
0:35 to 0:40Answer two or three spaced-review questions from lessons 1 to 3Returning to older material after a gap is the spacing that keeps it.

Notice what is missing. The session has no step where you read the whole explainer from the top, and no step where you watch someone else do the task. Those activities feel like learning, and they are the ones this method replaces.

How the course is organised

The course is a set of plain HTML pages, a simulator for the hands-on work, and a few supporting folders. Here is where each piece lives, and what it is for.

PieceWhat it is for
The index (index.html)Every part and lesson in order. Lessons you have marked complete show a tick, and each part shows how many are done. The progress is stored in this browser.
Lesson pages (lessons/)The explainer, the hands-on task, a quiz, a primary source, and a prompt for your tutor. Each lesson ends with a "Mark lesson complete" bar.
Projects: projects/<id>/starter and solutionThe Wokwi projects for each hands-on lesson. Starter is for you; solution is for comparison.
Reference sheets (reference/index.html)Dense, printable summaries of formulas, registers and APIs, to look things up quickly.
Glossary (reference/glossary.html)The course's terms, each defined once. Lessons use these words exactly.
Learning records (learning-records/)Your progress notes, so the tutor knows what to review and what to skip. The folder is not created by the course; the tutor starts it when you first record progress, and if it is missing you can ask for it.

Every lesson runs in the same order: why it matters, the ideas one at a time, a worked example, the code, and the hands-on task with its win. Then come two folded sections you open when you need them: Common mistakes, tagged When stuck, for when the task does not work, and sections tagged Go deeper, such as industry practice, for a second read. The summary and the questions close the lesson. The questions show one at a time as cards, with a dot per question that turns green or red when you answer. Inside the text, a box headed Picture it gives an everyday comparison for a hard idea and says where the comparison stops working, and a box headed In a real device shows the idea inside a product. Each part ends with a checkpoint: a quick multiple-choice test, one question at a time, with an I don't know button. Each question quietly draws on one to three lessons, so the result estimates how well each lesson has stuck and lists the ones to go over again before the next part. The course index then marks every lesson with its result.

Your marks are saved in the browser you read the course in, so they stay on that device. Export progress on the index saves them to a file, which is a good backup before you change browsers.

Your exercise work lives in the course folder, and the course itself keeps getting fixes. Keep the two apart with a git branch of your own: run git switch -c my-work once, then commit whenever you finish an exercise. A commit is also a note to your future self of what you tried. To update the course, commit first, then run git switch main, git pull, git switch my-work and git merge main. The VERSION file in the course folder should match the version under the title of the index page. If it doesn't, the update did not happen, and a starter may look broken when it isn't. The README's section "Keep your work on your own branch" has the details.

Using the agent as a tutor

The AI agent in this workspace is your tutor. It can quiz you, explain a point again, or set an extra exercise, and it can see your learning records once they exist. Some prompts to start with:

  • "Quiz me on lessons 5 to 8, mixing the topics."
  • "Explain this register again, using the bus and memory, not a formula."
  • "Give me an extra exercise on the resistor from lesson 2."

Ask the tutor to quiz you, not to tell you the answer. A tutor that gives you the answer straight away removes the retrieval step, which is the step that builds storage. If you find yourself asking for the answer, try the question once more from memory first, then ask.

A study routine

  1. Read the goal. Know what you are trying to be able to do before you start.
  2. Try the task first. Make a real attempt before opening a hint or the solution. Set a timer for about 15 minutes, and write down where you got stuck.
  3. Answer the quiz from memory. Do it before you reread the explainer, so the answers you give are yours.
  4. Write three things from memory at the end of the lesson, then check them against the page.
  5. Come back in a few days. Do the spaced-review quiz in a later lesson, or ask the tutor to quiz you on this one.
You have a plan for studying this course: try first, answer from memory, and come back to earlier lessons. You will use it in every lesson from here on.

Common mistakes and how to spot them
  1. Reading the solution before trying. Symptom: the solution looks obvious when you read it, then you cannot write the first line on a blank file. Cause: you recognised the answer rather than producing it. Fix: make a real attempt for the timed block, even if it is wrong, and then compare.
  2. Rereading instead of answering. Symptom: the quiz feels easy because you just read the answers, and a week later the topic is gone. Cause: the explainer was on the screen when you answered. Fix: close the explainer, answer the quiz, and then reread only what you got wrong.
  3. Cramming one long session. Symptom: you finish a four-hour session feeling expert, and the next week you cannot reproduce the code. Cause: the practice was concentrated in one place, so the memory faded before any review. Fix: spread the same time over several shorter sessions with gaps.
  4. Drilling one topic until it feels smooth. Symptom: you score well on a block of similar questions, then fail a mixed quiz. Cause: you learned the method for that block, not how to choose a method. Fix: mix topics, and ask the tutor for a mixed quiz.
  5. Passing a wrong answer over. Symptom: the same mistake appears in a later quiz. Cause: you read the reason and moved on without retrieving the right answer. Fix: after each wrong answer, write the correct reason in your own words, and keep the note in the learning-records folder.

In real projects

Working firmware engineers learn in the same way, although they rarely call it that. They learn a new microcontroller family by trying a small task first, then reading the reference manual for the part they got stuck on. They keep a notebook of things they had to look up twice, which is the same as the three-things habit. Teams review each other's work and write short post-mortems after a failure, which is a form of retrieval: the team must say what went wrong from memory, before the report is written.

The course is built on the idea that the best way to learn embedded engineering is to build things, see them fail, and fix them. The simulator is cheap enough to fail often, and the spaced review keeps the earlier work alive while you move on.

  • Spaced review returns to earlier material after a gap, so each return is a fresh retrieval.
  • Interleaving mixes topics, so you must choose the method each time.
  • Write three things from memory at the end of each lesson, then check them, to find what to review.
