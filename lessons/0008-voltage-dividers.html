Voltage dividers

Two resistors in series give you a smaller voltage at the midpoint. It is the simplest way a microcontroller can read a sensor, and an easy way to be misled about a supply.

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the output of an unloaded and a loaded voltage divider, and explain why a divider cannot replace a power supply.

Where we are and why it matters

Lesson 7 showed that two resistors in series share one current, and that their voltage drops add up to the supply. This lesson uses that fact on purpose. If you tap the wire between the two resistors, the voltage at that tap is a fixed fraction of the supply. A tap like that is called a midpoint, and it lets one resistor pair turn a supply voltage into a smaller one that a pin can read.

The problem is a common one. A microcontroller pin can read a voltage between 0 V and its supply, but a sensor often changes its resistance rather than its voltage. A thermistor, which is a resistor whose value depends on temperature, is the classic case. You need to turn its changing resistance into a changing voltage that the pin can measure. A divider does this with one extra resistor, and it is in almost every temperature sensor on a 3D printer.

The divider formula

A voltage divider is two resistors in series across a supply. The top resistor is R1 and the bottom one is R2. The midpoint, between them, is the output, and its voltage is:

Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)

The formula follows from lesson 7. The same current flows through both resistors, so the voltage across R2 is the current times R2, and the current is Vin divided by the total resistance R1 + R2. Put those together and the bottom resistor's share of the supply is R2 ÷ (R1 + R2). The output is that share of Vin.

The fraction depends only on the ratio of the two resistors, not on their size. Ten kilohms and ten kilohms give exactly half, and so do 100 kΩ and 100 kΩ. Scaling both resistors by the same factor leaves the output unchanged, but it changes how much current the divider wastes, and that is one of the trade-offs you will weigh later in the lesson.

Concept: the load

A load is anything that draws current from a point in a circuit. The formula above assumes that nothing takes current from the midpoint. The only current through R1 and R2 is the divider current, and the output is simply the fraction of the supply. A real connection to the midpoint is a load, and it changes the answer.

The reason is that the load sits in parallel with R2. Current that leaves the midpoint through the load does not flow through R2, so R2 effectively becomes R2 in parallel with Rload. That combined value is lower than R2, and the output, which depends on it, falls. Lesson 7 gave the rule for two resistors in parallel, and it is exactly the rule you need here.

A load does not have to be a resistor. A microcontroller input draws a very small current, a sensor draws some current, and a second circuit draws whatever it needs. The question to ask is always the same: does anything draw current from the midpoint, and how much? If the answer is "a little" with a high-resistance divider, the error may be small. If the answer is "some" with a low-resistance divider, the error can be large.

Worked examples with real numbers

Example A: an unloaded divider. A 3.3 V rail feeds 4.7 kΩ on top and 4.7 kΩ on the bottom, with nothing connected to the midpoint. Work through it step by step.

  1. Write the knowns in base units: Vin = 3.3 V, R1 = 4700 Ω, R2 = 4700 Ω.
  2. Find the total resistance: R1 + R2 = 9400 Ω.
  3. Find the bottom share: R2 ÷ (R1 + R2) = 4700 ÷ 9400 = 0.5.
  4. Multiply by the supply: Vout = 3.3 V × 0.5 = 1.65 V.

Example B: a loaded divider. Now take 10 V across 10 kΩ on top and 10 kΩ on the bottom, with a 10 kΩ load on the midpoint. Without the load, the output is 5 V. With the load, the bottom becomes 10 kΩ in parallel with 10 kΩ, which is 5 kΩ. Then:

  1. Find the loaded bottom: R2 ∥ Rload = (10 000 × 10 000) ÷ (10 000 + 10 000) = 5000 Ω.
  2. Apply the divider formula with 5000 Ω as R2: Vout = 10 V × 5000 ÷ (10 000 + 5000) = 10 V × 0.333.
  3. Result: Vout = 3.33 V.

The load pulled the output down by a third, which is far more than most people expect from a single 10 kΩ. The divider had not changed, and neither had the supply. The only change was one extra current path at the midpoint.

  1. Unloaded: 10 V across 10 kΩ + 10 kΩ drives 0.5 mA through both resistors, and the midpoint sits at 10 × 10k ÷ 20k = 5.00 V.
  2. Now connect a 10 kΩ load from the midpoint to ground. It is in parallel with R2: both ends join the same two nodes.
  3. The bottom of the divider is now 10k ∥ 10k = 5 kΩ, so the whole chain is 15 kΩ and 0.667 mA flows through R1.
  4. The output is 10 × 5k ÷ 15k = 3.33 V. R1's 0.667 mA splits at the midpoint: 0.333 mA through R2 and 0.333 mA through the load.
  5. One 10 kΩ load pulled 5 V down to 3.33 V. A divider's output depends on what you connect to it, which is why it is not a supply.

Why a divider is not a supply

A supply holds its output steady as the load changes. A divider does not. Seen from the midpoint, a divider looks like a voltage source behind a resistance. The source voltage is the unloaded output, Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2), and the source resistance is R1 ∥ R2. The output sags as the load draws more current, because the source resistance drops the voltage across itself.

A divider also wastes power even when nothing is connected to its output. In Example B's network, 10 V across 20 kΩ draws 0.5 mA all the time, and 10 V × 0.5 mA = 5 mW is turned into heat in the two resistors, as lesson 6 would calculate. A regulator holds its output voltage with feedback and does not waste that power in the same way. Lesson 15 covers regulators. The short version: a divider sets a voltage, and a regulator holds one.

A common rule of thumb is to keep the load at least ten times the bottom resistor, so the divider stays close to its design value. Check it with your own numbers, because it is not exact. With R1 = R2 and a load of 10 × R2, the bottom resistance falls by about 9 % (R2 ∥ 10R2 = 10/11 × R2), but the output falls from 0.500 to 0.476 of the supply, which is about 5 % low.

Where you meet dividers on a 3D printer

A thermistor in a printer's hotend or bed is almost always read through a divider. The thermistor sits on the bottom, and a fixed pull-up resistor sits on top, connected to the supply. The microcontroller's analogue input is wired to the midpoint. When the thermistor is hot, its resistance drops, the bottom share shrinks, and the midpoint voltage falls. The firmware turns the voltage into a temperature.

Klipper's documentation uses the same design. The sensor settings in its config reference list the pull-up resistor as 4700 Ω by default, and the sensor_pin is the analogue input that reads the midpoint. The reference shows a divider in real use, and you can check it against the firmware you run. The code: a divider and its load in C

A firmware engineer often needs the divider formula, to convert a measured voltage back into a resistance, or to check that a design is loaded correctly. The program below computes the unloaded and loaded output for the Example B network. 

#include <stdio.h>

/* Unloaded divider: Vout = Vin x R2 / (R1 + R2), in volts. */
static float divider_vout(float vin, float r1, float r2)
{
    return vin * r2 / (r1 + r2);
}

/* Two resistors in parallel, product over sum, in ohms. */
static float parallel_ohms(float a, float b)
{
    return (a * b) / (a + b);
}

int main(void)
{
    float unloaded = divider_vout(10.0f, 10000.0f, 10000.0f);
    float r2_loaded = parallel_ohms(10000.0f, 10000.0f);
    float loaded = divider_vout(10.0f, 10000.0f, r2_loaded);
    printf("unloaded %.2f V, loaded %.2f V\n", (double)unloaded, (double)loaded);
    return 0;
}

divider_vout is the formula in one line. It multiplies the supply by R2 and divides by the total resistance. The multiplication happens first, because C evaluates vin * r2 before the division, and with float arguments both steps are floating-point. Writing it as vin * (r2 / (r1 + r2)) would also work, and some teams prefer it because it reads as "the fraction times the supply".

parallel_ohms is the product-over-sum rule from lesson 7. The load is in parallel with R2, so the code first replaces R2 with that parallel value. This is the step that matters in the loaded case, and it is easy to forget when you are writing a quick calculation.

In main, the unloaded result is 5.00 V, and the loaded result is 3.33 V. The first call uses the original R2. The second call computes the loaded R2 as 5000 Ω, then passes it to the same divider function. The program prints the two values to two decimal places, and the output line is unloaded 5.00 V, loaded 3.33 V.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

  1. Swapping the two resistors. You write Vout = Vin × R1 ÷ (R1 + R2) and get the top share instead of the bottom one. The symptom is a midpoint that reads close to the supply when the sensor is cold, the opposite of what you expected. The fix is to remember that R2 is the bottom resistor, and that the bottom share is the output.
  2. Forgetting the load. You design the divider with the unloaded formula and then connect the sensor, which draws current from the midpoint. The symptom is a reading that is lower than the calculated value, and that changes when the sensor is connected or disconnected. The fix is to include the load in parallel with R2, as in Example B, or to add a buffer so the midpoint draws almost no current.
  3. Using the divider as a supply. You power a sensor that draws 5 mA from a divider, and the sensor's supply sags as it starts working. The symptom is a sensor that works on the bench with a meter but misbehaves when it runs. The fix is a regulator, which holds the voltage, or a buffer for the measurement, which takes almost no current from the divider.
  4. Integer division in C. You store the supply and resistances as int and compute vin * r2 / (r1 + r2). For a 3.3 V supply in millivolts with 4700 Ω and 4700 Ω, the result is correct only if the multiplication happens before the division. If the divide happens first on integers, the answer collapses to 0. The symptom is an output of zero, or a value that is far too small. The fix is to multiply first, use float, or scale by a constant on purpose.
A divider does not check its own load. If you want a reading that does not depend on what is connected, the measurement must draw so little current that the divider barely notices, or the design must add a stage that holds the voltage.

In real projects

Dividers are everywhere because they are cheap, and because the mistakes they cause look like sensor faults. A senior engineer reviewing a sensor circuit will ask four questions: what is the source resistance of the divider, what load does the input draw, how much power does the divider waste, and what happens to the reading at the temperature extremes of the product. The answers are often hidden in a single resistor value, so a review that asks for them catches many errors early.

Designers choose the divider's resistance values with the load in mind. A divider with resistances in the kilohm range draws little current and wastes little power, but its source resistance is high, so a load draws the output down. A divider with lower resistances holds its voltage better under load, but it wastes more power all the time. That trade-off is the design decision, and it is why there is no single correct value.

Summary

  • A voltage divider gives Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2), where R2 is the bottom resistor.
  • The output depends on the ratio of the resistors, not on their size. Bigger resistors waste less current but are more sensitive to loads.
  • A load on the midpoint is in parallel with R2, which lowers the bottom resistance and the output.
  • A divider looks like a source behind R1 ∥ R2, and its output sags under load. It is not a supply.
  • A divider wastes power all the time, so compute Vin ÷ (R1 + R2) for the standing current.
  • Thermistors on 3D printers are read through a divider with a pull-up resistor, and Klipper's default pull-up is 4700 Ω.
