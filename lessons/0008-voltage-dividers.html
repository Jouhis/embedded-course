Voltage dividers

Two series resistors that give a fraction of a supply voltage

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the output of an unloaded and a loaded voltage divider, and explain why a divider cannot replace a power supply.

Reading a resistance as a voltage

Lesson 7 showed that two resistors in series carry the same current and that their voltage drops add up to the supply. This lesson puts that fact to deliberate use. The wire between the two resistors, called the midpoint, sits at a fixed fraction of the supply, so a single resistor pair can turn a supply voltage into a smaller one that a pin can read.

The need arises constantly. A microcontroller pin can measure a voltage between 0 V and its supply, but many sensors change their resistance rather than their voltage. The classic case is a thermistor, a resistor whose value depends on temperature, whose changing resistance must somehow become a changing voltage. A divider does this with one extra resistor, which is why one sits behind almost every temperature sensor on a 3D printer.

The divider formula

A voltage divider is two resistors in series across a supply, with R1 on top and R2 at the bottom. The midpoint between them is the output, and its voltage is

Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)(1)

where Vin is the supply voltage. The formula follows directly from lesson 7. Since the same current flows through both resistors, the voltage across R2 is that current times R2, and the current itself is Vin divided by the total resistance R1 + R2. Combining the two gives the bottom resistor's share of the supply, R2 ÷ (R1 + R2), and the output is that share of Vin.

It follows that the output depends only on the ratio of the two resistors, not on their size. Two 10 kΩ resistors give exactly half the supply, and so do two 100 kΩ resistors. Scaling both by the same factor leaves the output unchanged but alters how much current the divider wastes, which is one of the trade-offs weighed later in this lesson.

Loading the midpoint

A load is anything that draws current from a point in a circuit. Equation 1 assumes that nothing draws current from the midpoint, so the only current through R1 and R2 is the divider's own. Any real connection to the midpoint is a load, however, and it changes the result.

The reason is that the load sits in parallel with R2. Current that leaves the midpoint through the load bypasses R2, so the bottom of the divider effectively becomes

R2 ∥ Rload = (R2 × Rload) ÷ (R2 + Rload)(2)

where Rload is the resistance the load presents. This is the product-over-sum rule from lesson 7, and its result is always lower than R2, so the output falls.

A load does not have to be a resistor. A microcontroller input draws a very small current, a sensor draws somewhat more, and a second circuit draws whatever it needs. The question to ask is therefore always the same: does anything draw current from the midpoint, and how much? A small current from a high-resistance divider may cause only a small error, whereas a larger current from a low-resistance divider can cause a large one.

Worked examples: an unloaded and a loaded divider

The first example is an unloaded divider. A 3.3 V rail feeds 4.7 kΩ on top and 4.7 kΩ at the bottom, with nothing connected to the midpoint. Before working through it, estimate the answer from the ratio alone.

  1. Write the knowns in base units: Vin = 3.3 V, R1 = 4700 Ω, R2 = 4700 Ω.
  2. Find the total resistance: R1 + R2 = 9400 Ω.
  3. Find the bottom share: R2 ÷ (R1 + R2) = 4700 ÷ 9400 = 0.5. Equal resistors always give half.
  4. Multiply by the supply: Vout = 3.3 V × 0.5 = 1.65 V.

The second example adds a load. A 10 V supply feeds 10 kΩ on top and 10 kΩ at the bottom, so the unloaded output is 5 V, and a 10 kΩ load is then connected to the midpoint. Since the load is in parallel with R2, the first step is to replace R2 using Equation 2.

  1. Find the loaded bottom: R2 ∥ Rload = (10 000 × 10 000) ÷ (10 000 + 10 000) = 5000 Ω.
  2. Apply Equation 1 with 5000 Ω as R2: Vout = 10 V × 5000 ÷ (10 000 + 5000) = 10 V × 0.333.
  3. Result: Vout = 3.33 V.

The load pulled the output down by a third, far more than most people expect from a single 10 kΩ resistor. Neither the divider nor the supply changed; the only difference was one extra current path at the midpoint.

  1. Unloaded: 10 V across 10 kΩ + 10 kΩ drives 0.5 mA through both resistors, and the midpoint sits at 10 × 10k ÷ 20k = 5.00 V.
  2. Now connect a 10 kΩ load from the midpoint to ground. It is in parallel with R2: both ends join the same two nodes.
  3. The bottom of the divider is now 10k ∥ 10k = 5 kΩ, so the whole chain is 15 kΩ and 0.667 mA flows through R1.
  4. The output is 10 × 5k ÷ 15k = 3.33 V. R1's 0.667 mA splits at the midpoint: 0.333 mA through R2 and 0.333 mA through the load.
  5. One 10 kΩ load pulled 5 V down to 3.33 V. A divider's output depends on what you connect to it, which is why it is not a supply.

Dividers compared with power supplies

A supply holds its output steady as the load changes, and a divider cannot. Seen from the midpoint, a divider behaves like a voltage source behind a resistance. The source voltage is the unloaded output from Equation 1, and the source resistance is R1 ∥ R2. As the load draws more current, that resistance drops more voltage across itself, so the output sags.

A divider resembles a tap fed through a thin pipe. With the tap closed, the gauge shows the right pressure, but opening a second tap downstream makes the pressure collapse, because all the water must pass through the thin pipe, R1. A supply is more like a wide pipe from a water tower, whose pressure stays put whatever you draw, up to its rated flow. The comparison breaks down for a divider with nothing connected, which still passes a standing current through both resistors, while a closed tap passes nothing.

A divider also wastes power even with nothing connected to its output. In the second example's network, 10 V across 20 kΩ draws 0.5 mA continuously, and 10 V × 0.5 mA = 5 mW turns into heat in the two resistors, as lesson 6 would calculate. A regulator, by contrast, holds its output with feedback and does not waste power in the same way; lesson 15 covers regulators. In short, a divider sets a voltage, whereas a regulator holds one.

A common rule of thumb keeps the load at least ten times the bottom resistor, so the divider stays close to its design value. Check it with your own numbers, since it is not exact. With R1 = R2 and a load of 10 × R2, the bottom resistance falls by about 9 % (R2 ∥ 10R2 = 10/11 × R2), and the output falls from 0.500 to 0.476 of the supply, about 5 % low.

Thermistor dividers on a 3D printer

A thermistor in a printer's hotend or bed is almost always read through a divider. The thermistor forms the bottom resistor, a fixed pull-up resistor connected to the supply forms the top, and the microcontroller's analogue input is wired to the midpoint. As the thermistor heats up, its resistance drops, the bottom share shrinks and the midpoint voltage falls, and the firmware converts that voltage into a temperature.

Klipper's documentation describes the same design. The sensor settings in its configuration reference list a default pull-up of 4700 Ω, and the sensor_pin is the analogue input that reads the midpoint, so you can compare the theory with the firmware you run. Since the thermistor's resistance changes with temperature, the midpoint voltage tells the firmware how hot the sensor is.

Divider calculations in C

Firmware often needs the divider formula, either to convert a measured voltage back into a resistance or to check that a design is correctly loaded. The program below computes the unloaded and loaded outputs of the second worked example. 

#include <stdio.h>

/* Unloaded divider: Vout = Vin x R2 / (R1 + R2), in volts. */
static float divider_vout(float vin, float r1, float r2)
{
    return vin * r2 / (r1 + r2);
}

/* Two resistors in parallel, product over sum, in ohms. */
static float parallel_ohms(float a, float b)
{
    return (a * b) / (a + b);
}

int main(void)
{
    float unloaded = divider_vout(10.0f, 10000.0f, 10000.0f);
    float r2_loaded = parallel_ohms(10000.0f, 10000.0f);
    float loaded = divider_vout(10.0f, 10000.0f, r2_loaded);
    printf("unloaded %.2f V, loaded %.2f V\n", (double)unloaded, (double)loaded);
    return 0;
}

divider_vout is Equation 1 in one line: it multiplies the supply by R2 and divides by the total resistance. C evaluates vin * r2 before the division, and since the arguments are float, both steps are floating-point. Writing it as vin * (r2 / (r1 + r2)) works equally well, and some teams prefer that form because it reads as the fraction times the supply.

parallel_ohms implements Equation 2. Because the load is in parallel with R2, the code first replaces R2 with that parallel value, which is the step that matters in the loaded case and the one most easily forgotten in a quick calculation.

In main, the first call uses the original R2 and returns 5.00 V. The second computes the loaded R2 as 5000 Ω and passes it to the same divider function, which returns 3.33 V. The program prints both values to two decimal places, so the output line reads unloaded 5.00 V, loaded 3.33 V.

Try it in CircuitJS

  1. Open the voltage divider example. The left side is a 10 V source across two 10 kΩ resistors. Find the midpoint, which is wired to an output probe, and measure its voltage. It should read 5 V.
  2. Add a 10 kΩ load resistor from that midpoint to the bottom wire, so that it sits in parallel with the bottom 10 kΩ. In CircuitJS, add a resistor from the menu and set its value to 10 kΩ by double-clicking it. Confirm that it connects to the midpoint and the bottom wire, not to the top.
  3. Predict the new midpoint voltage, then measure it. You should see about 3.33 V. Type the measured value into the question below and check it with the calculator.
Hint

Work out the bottom resistance first. The load and the bottom 10 kΩ are in parallel, so the bottom is 5 kΩ, and the output is 10 V times 5 kΩ divided by the total of 10 kΩ plus 5 kΩ.

You calculated a divider's output with and without a load, and you saw the load pull the midpoint down. You now know where a sensor's voltage comes from, and why the next stage must be a buffer or a regulator rather than another divider.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

The two resistors swapped in the formula

Writing Vout = Vin × R1 ÷ (R1 + R2) gives the top share instead of the bottom one. The symptom is a midpoint that reads close to the supply when the sensor is cold, the opposite of what was expected. Remember that R2 is the bottom resistor and that the output is its share.

A load left out of the design

The divider is designed with the unloaded formula, and then a sensor that draws current from the midpoint is connected. The reading comes out lower than calculated and changes when the sensor is connected or disconnected. Include the load in parallel with R2, as in the second worked example, or add a buffer so that the midpoint supplies almost no current.

A divider used as a supply

A sensor drawing 5 mA is powered from a divider, and its supply sags as soon as it starts working. It behaves on the bench with only a meter attached but misbehaves in operation. Use a regulator, which holds the voltage, or a buffer for the measurement, which draws almost no current from the divider.

Integer division in C

The supply and resistances are stored as int and the code computes vin * r2 / (r1 + r2). With a 3.3 V supply in millivolts and two 4700 Ω resistors, this is correct only if the multiplication happens first; if the division happens first on integers, the result collapses to 0. The symptom is an output of zero or a value far too small. Multiply first, use float, or scale by a constant deliberately.

A divider does not account for its own load. If a reading must not depend on what is connected, the measurement has to draw so little current that the divider barely notices, or the design needs a stage that holds the voltage.

Choosing divider values in real designs

Dividers appear everywhere because they are cheap, and the mistakes they cause tend to look like sensor faults. A senior engineer reviewing a sensor circuit therefore asks four questions: what the divider's source resistance is, what load the input draws, how much power the divider wastes, and what happens to the reading at the product's temperature extremes. The answers are often hidden in a single resistor value, so a review that insists on them catches many errors early.

Designers choose the resistance values with the load in mind. Resistances in the tens of kilohms draw little current and waste little power, but the high source resistance lets a load pull the output down. Lower resistances hold the voltage better under load but waste more power continuously. That trade-off is the actual design decision, which is why no single value is correct.

Summary

  • A voltage divider gives Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2), where R2 is the bottom resistor.
  • The output depends on the ratio of the resistors, not their size. Larger resistors waste less current but are more sensitive to loads.
  • A load on the midpoint is in parallel with R2, which lowers the bottom resistance and therefore the output.
  • A divider behaves like a source behind R1 ∥ R2, and its output sags under load, so it cannot serve as a supply.
  • A divider wastes power continuously; Vin ÷ (R1 + R2) gives its standing current.
  • Thermistors on 3D printers are read through a divider with a pull-up resistor, and Klipper's default pull-up is 4700 Ω.
