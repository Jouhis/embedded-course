Voltage dividers

Two resistors in series give you a smaller voltage at the midpoint. It is the simplest way a microcontroller can read a sensor, and the simplest way to fool yourself about a supply.

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the output of an unloaded and a loaded voltage divider, and explain why a divider cannot replace a power supply.

The idea

A voltage divider is two resistors in series across a supply. The midpoint voltage is a fraction of the supply: Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2), where R1 is the top resistor and R2 the bottom one. The fraction is set by the ratio of the resistors, not by their size, so 10 kΩ and 10 kΩ give exactly half, and 100 kΩ and 100 kΩ also give exactly half. The ratio is the important part. Scaling both resistors by the same factor leaves Vout unchanged, but changes how much current the divider wastes.

The formula assumes nothing else takes current from the midpoint. Connect a load, which is anything that draws current from the midpoint, and the formula changes. The load sits in parallel with R2, so the bottom of the divider becomes R2 ∥ Rload, which is lower, and Vout falls.

Worked examples with real numbers

Unloaded. A 3.3 V rail across 4.7 kΩ and 4.7 kΩ. Vout = 3.3 × 4.7 ÷ 9.4 = 1.65 V.

Loaded. Now the same divider, but 10 V across 10 kΩ on top and 10 kΩ on the bottom, with a 10 kΩ load on the midpoint. Unloaded, Vout = 5 V. With the load, the bottom becomes 10 k ∥ 10 k = 5 kΩ, so Vout = 10 × 5 ÷ (10 + 5) = 3.33 V. The load pulled the output down by a third, which is far more than most people expect from a single 10 kΩ.

Why a divider is not a supply. A supply holds its output steady as the load changes. A divider does not. Its output looks like a voltage source behind a resistance, equal to R1 ∥ R2, and the output sags as the load draws more current. The divider also wastes power even with no load: 10 V across 20 kΩ draws 0.5 mA all the time, and 5 mW is lost in the two resistors. A regulator (lesson 15) holds the voltage instead of dividing it.

A common rule of thumb is to keep the load at least ten times the bottom resistor, so the divider stays close to its design value. Even then, the output is about 9 % low: with RL = 10 × R2, R2 ∥ RL = 10/11 × R2. Check the error with your own numbers rather than trusting the rule.

Where you meet dividers on a 3D printer

Klipper reads a thermistor through an analogue input. The thermistor forms a divider with a pullup resistor to the supply, and the MCU reads the midpoint. Klipper's default pullup is 4700 Ω, and its config reference names the parameter pullup_resistor for exactly this reason. Klipper: Config Reference, the extruder and thermistor settings Lesson 58 uses the same divider to read a thermistor with the Pico's ADC. The thermistor's resistance changes with temperature, so the midpoint voltage tells the firmware how hot the sensor is.

Try it in CircuitJS

  1. Open the voltage divider example. The left side is a 10 V source across two 10 kΩ resistors. Find the midpoint, which is wired to an output probe. Measure its voltage. It should be 5 V.
  2. Add a 10 kΩ load resistor from that midpoint to the bottom wire, so it sits in parallel with the bottom 10 kΩ. Use the element menu in CircuitJS to place it, and double-click it to set its value.
  3. Measure the midpoint again. Type the new voltage into the question below. Then check it with the calculator.
Hint

Work out the bottom resistance first. The load and the bottom 10 kΩ are in parallel, so the bottom is 5 kΩ. Then the output is 10 V times 5 kΩ over the total, 10 kΩ plus 5 kΩ.

You calculated a divider's output with and without a load, and you saw the load pull the midpoint down. You now know where a sensor's voltage comes from, and why the next step must be a buffer or a regulator, not another divider.

"Why is a resistor divider a poor way to power a sensor that draws 5 mA?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

The divider's output is a fraction of the input only while nothing draws current from the midpoint. A 5 mA sensor draws current, so the output sags and the reading depends on the load. The divider also burns power continuously. The fix is a regulator that holds the voltage, or a buffer amplifier if the job is only to measure the midpoint without loading it.
