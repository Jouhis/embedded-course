Mutexes, priority inversion, and deadlock

Locks with owners and their failure modes

By the end of this lesson you can explain priority inversion with three tasks, show it in UART output, fix it by using a mutex with priority inheritance, and state a lock-ordering rule that prevents deadlock.

Shared resources between tasks of different priority

Lesson 90 moved data and signals between tasks and used a mutex to keep log lines from mixing on the UART, saying that a mutex is not a binary semaphore without saying why, and this lesson explains the difference and the failure it prevents. Lab 5 protects the I2C bus with a mutex, because the sensor task and the UI task both use it, so the same rule applies there.

The bug affects real devices, since a shared bus or log touched by two tasks of different priorities can make the most urgent task wait for the least urgent one, and on a device that resets or misses a sample under that condition, the bug can look like a hardware fault while passing every test that runs the tasks one at a time.

Mutexes as locks with an owner

A mutex is a lock held by at most one task at a time, a task trying to take it while it is held waiting, and a critical section is the code that must not run in two places at once, such as an update of a shared structure, which the mutex lets a task protect.

FreeRTOS builds a mutex from the queue machinery, xQueueCreateMutex() at line 647 of queue.c calling xQueueGenericCreate() with a length of 1 and an item size of 0, so the queue holds a token rather than data, taking the mutex removing the token and giving it back returning it. The difference from a binary semaphore is that the kernel also records the owner, the task that took the token, in the queue's xMutexHolder field, only the owner being supposed to give the token back, and that one extra field makes the next idea possible.

Priority inheritance

Priority inheritance is the rule that while a high-priority task waits on a mutex, the owner runs at the waiter's priority, the owner being the only task the kernel can help, because it knows who it is. In FreeRTOS, when a task blocks on a mutex, the queue code calls xTaskPriorityInherit() (called at queue.c line 1791 and defined in tasks.c at line 6639), raising the owner's priority to the waiter's, and when the owner gives the mutex back, xTaskPriorityDisinherit() (tasks.c line 6742) restores its original priority.

As a result no task of middle priority can preempt the owner, which now outranks it, whereas a binary semaphore has no owner, so the kernel has no task to raise and cannot do this, which is the difference between the two objects that the table below sets side by side.

Priority inversion with three tasks

Priority inversion is the bug that inheritance prevents. A high-priority task H waits for a resource held by a low-priority task L while a medium-priority task M, which does not need the resource, is ready, and because M outranks L, L cannot run, so H waits for M, which has nothing to do with the resource, the higher task in effect running at the lowest level. The glossary entry calls it a bounded but unjustified wait, a precise description of a delay that is neither a deadlock nor starvation.

Priority inversion resembles a junior worker holding the only key to the store room while the manager, needing the key, waits, and a mid-level colleague keeps the junior busy with other jobs, so the manager ends up waiting for the colleague, who has nothing to do with the store room, while priority inheritance gives the junior the manager's rank as long as they hold the key, so nobody below the manager can keep them busy. The comparison breaks on visibility, though, because the manager could see who holds the key and complain, whereas the kernel can raise only the owner of a mutex and knows nothing about who holds a binary semaphore.

The project projects/0091-priority-inversion reproduces this, low (priority 1) taking the resource and holding it for 200 ms, medium (priority 2) starting at 20 ms and running for 300 ms, and high (priority 3) starting at 50 ms and needing the resource for 10 ms, while the controller at priority 4 runs the two phases in turn, phase 1 using a binary semaphore as the lock and phase 2 a mutex.

Worked example: the waits in the two phases

The project's timings are easiest to follow by stepping through each phase. The tasks do not sleep while they hold or wait but busy-wait on the tick count, which keeps them Ready, so a higher-priority task can take the core from them, a detail that changes the numbers. Before reading on, predict how long high waits in each phase.

  1. Phase 1, the start. At 0 ms low takes the binary semaphore and starts a 200 ms busy-wait, and at 20 ms medium becomes ready with a higher priority than low, so it takes the core and low is preempted.
  2. Phase 1, high blocks. At 50 ms high wakes and asks for the semaphore, which is held, so high blocks, and since the semaphore has no owner, the kernel does not raise low's priority and medium keeps running.
  3. Phase 1, the release. Medium's 300 ms busy-wait, started at 20 ms, ends at 320 ms, and only then does low get the core back, its 200 ms busy-wait being measured in elapsed ticks since 0 ms, so with 320 ms passed its critical section ends at once and it releases the semaphore at 320 ms, high taking it then after waiting 320 − 50 = 270 ms.
  4. Phase 2, the start. With the mutex the sequence is the same until 50 ms, low taking the mutex at 0 ms and running until 20 ms, when medium preempts it.
  5. Phase 2, inheritance. At 50 ms high asks for the mutex and blocks, and the kernel, seeing the owner, raises low's priority to 3, the same as high, so medium at priority 2 cannot take the core back.
  6. Phase 2, the release. Low runs from 50 ms until its elapsed time reaches 200 ms, releases the mutex and falls back to priority 1, and high takes the mutex at 200 ms after waiting 200 − 50 = 150 ms.

The two results differ by a single line of code in the controller, the difference lying in how long medium holds the core rather than in any change to high, since in phase 1 high waits for medium's whole run and in phase 2 only for low's own hold time. The fix bounds the wait by the resource's holding time, the best a design can do without changing the critical section.

The fix with a mutex

Phase 2 changes the constructor to xSemaphoreCreateMutex() in place of xSemaphoreCreateBinary() and deletes the give that makes the binary semaphore available, because a mutex is available when created, while the take and give calls in the tasks stay the same. The owner is recorded, so when high blocks, the kernel raises low's priority and medium can no longer preempt it, whereas the starter's phase 2 deliberately keeps the binary semaphore, so its output ends with PI NOT FIXED.

Do not use a mutex in an interrupt, and do not use a binary semaphore to protect shared data. A common mistake is "a semaphore is a lock too", which is true in the simplest sense and wrong for priority inversion.

Deadlock and lock ordering

Deadlock is a cycle of waiting, task 1 holding lock A and waiting for B while task 2 holds B and waits for A, so neither can proceed, and no timeout ends it unless one is written. In the classic analysis four conditions must hold together, mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption of a lock and a circular wait, and a design removes deadlock by breaking one of them, most simply the circular wait, with a lock ordering rule in which every task takes locks in the same global order, for example always A before B, so that no cycle can form.

  • Take locks in a fixed order, and document the order next to the locks.
  • Hold each lock for as short a time as possible; never call a blocking function inside a critical section.
  • Use timeouts on lock takes in code that might be stuck, and handle the timeout as an error, not as a retry loop.

A plain FreeRTOS mutex is not recursive, so a task taking a mutex it already holds waits for itself and never returns. If a function needing the lock is called by another that already holds it, the design needs a recursive mutex, which the kernel provides through xSemaphoreTakeRecursive() (documented in semphr.h), although most code is better off with one lock and one path, so that the question never arises.

Deadlock resembles two people who both need the store-room key and the office key, one picking up the store-room key and the other the office key, each then waiting for the other's, so neither ever moves, and the rule preventing it is that everyone picks up the keys in the same order. The comparison breaks because two people would eventually talk it over, whereas two blocked tasks wait forever unless a timeout was written.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

A binary semaphore used as a lock

A high-priority task waits a long time whenever a medium task runs, 270 ms in this project, the phase-1 pattern, because a developer protected the UART with a binary semaphore, which looks the same in the code. Use xSemaphoreCreateMutex() with the same take and give calls.

A mutex taken from an interrupt

An assertion fires or the device hangs, because an ISR takes the UART mutex to print a fault, and the take may block while an interrupt cannot. Set a flag or queue the event in the ISR, and let a task take the mutex and print.

An early return that keeps the lock

The next task to take the mutex waits forever, often at portMAX_DELAY, and the system looks stuck, because a function took the mutex, hit an error and returned without giving it back. Give the function one exit path that always gives the mutex, or put a timeout on each take so that the stuck task reports an error.

Two locks taken in opposite orders

The device stops at a random moment with every task waiting on either lock blocked, because task 1 takes the sensor mutex and then the bus mutex while task 2 takes the bus mutex and then the sensor mutex. Document a global order, such as always the sensor first, and follow it everywhere.

A mutex for data an interrupt also touches

A count is sometimes one short, because a developer protected a counter with a mutex while an ISR increments the same counter, and a mutex cannot be taken in the ISR, so it does not protect the counter. Use a critical section, which masks interrupts for the short update, or a design in which the ISR only sets a flag that the task reads.

A historical case: Mars Pathfinder

In July 1997 the Mars Pathfinder lander landed on Mars and a few days into its mission began to reset repeatedly, each reset losing data, and Mike Jones, writing on 7 December 1997 about a keynote by David Wilner of Wind River Systems, described the cause in the RISKS Digest (volume 19, issue 49). The spacecraft ran VxWorks, a preemptive priority kernel, in which a high-priority bus management task shared a mutex with a low-priority meteorological task, while a medium-priority communications task, which did not use the mutex, could preempt the low task as it held the mutex, so the high task waited for the medium task's run, the inversion this lesson shows.

Jones's account says that the mutex had been created with priority inheritance turned off, whereas with it on the low task would have inherited the high task's priority while holding the mutex and the medium task could not have preempted it, so the fix was to change the value controlling that mutex. Jones reports that the JPL engineers used the VxWorks C interpreter, left enabled on the spacecraft, to upload a short program changing the flag from FALSE to TRUE, after which the resets stopped. He credits the three authors of the priority inheritance paper, L. Sha, R. Rajkumar and J. P. Lehoczky, with first describing the problem and its solution, while a reply in the next issue, by Bob Rahe, dates that paper to 1990 and points out that an earlier mainframe operating system had a similar locking scheme.

The original post gives the details, and it is best read as one account of a talk rather than the team's own record. The post is in the RISKS Digest, volume 19, issue 49. Its author reports a talk and the engineers' analysis as he understood it, so compare it with other accounts before you quote a detail.

Lock inventories and reviews in product teams

Teams keep a list of every mutex in the system with its owner tasks and the order in which locks are taken, and they review any change that adds a lock. A common review question is what a function does while it holds the lock, because a call to a slow bus or a blocking queue inside the lock is a frequent source of long waits, which is exactly why the Lab 5 design holds the I2C mutex only across the bus transfer, never across a delay or a queue send.

Summary

  • A mutex is a lock with an owner, built from a length-1 queue of zero-byte items, and only the owner gives it back.
  • Priority inheritance raises the owner to the priority of the highest waiter while it holds the mutex and restores it on give.
  • Priority inversion is a high-priority task waiting for a low-priority owner while a medium task runs, which a binary semaphore cannot fix, because it has no owner.
  • In the project phase 1 gives high a wait of 270 ms, and phase 2 with a mutex 150 ms.
  • Deadlock needs a circular wait, which a global lock order breaks.
  • A plain mutex is not recursive, and a mutex cannot be taken from an interrupt.
