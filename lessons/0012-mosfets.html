MOSFETs

A voltage-controlled switch

By the end of this lesson you can size an N-channel MOSFET low-side switch for a 3.3 V gate drive, which means checking that the gate is fully on, calculating the power the switch dissipates and placing its flyback diode.

From bipolar transistors to MOSFETs

In lesson 11 we switched a load from a pin with a bipolar transistor, which cost a base resistor, a base current the pin supplies for as long as the load is on, and a gain that varies between parts. This lesson replaces it with a MOSFET, the device most real power switches use, and applies the power formula from lesson 6 to work out what the switch costs in heat.

The situation comes up on most powered boards. A 3D printer's heated bed draws several amps, and so do fans, solenoid valves and heater cartridges, while a bipolar transistor sized for that current needs an awkward amount of base drive. A MOSFET holds its gate on with almost no current, so a 3.3 V pin can switch a 2 A load directly. Two questions then decide the design: is the switch fully on at the pin's voltage, and how hot does it get?

The MOSFET as a voltage-controlled switch

A MOSFET is a switch controlled by a voltage instead of a current. Its gate behaves like one plate of a capacitor, so it draws almost no steady current, and the drain and source are the two ends of the channel being switched. In an N-channel device the channel conducts once the gate is higher than the source by more than the threshold voltage, VGS(th), which for the IRLZ44N lies between 1.0 and 2.0 V (IRLZ44N datasheet, Electrical Characteristics: VGS(th) 1.0 to 2.0 V). The gate is isolated by an oxide layer, so DC gate current is essentially zero. The GPIO pin only has to charge the gate capacitance once per switch-on.

Think of the gate as a small bucket that must be filled before the switch turns over, with the pin as a hose of limited flow. Once full, the bucket stays full with no flow at all, which is why a MOSFET takes current from the pin only while it switches and why a weak pin turns a large MOSFET on slowly. The picture breaks down halfway, because a half-charged gate does not give half a switch: the channel stays closed below the threshold and then opens along a curve.

Structure of the gate and the channel

The name spells out the structure: metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor. A gate of metal or polysilicon sits on a thin insulating layer of oxide, which sits on a silicon body, and the drain and source are two heavily doped regions in that body, kept apart by the body itself. A conductor, an insulator and a conductor stacked like this form a capacitor, a part that stores charge when a voltage is placed across it and that lesson 13 treats in full.

When we apply a voltage between the gate and the source, that capacitor charges. The charge on the gate attracts carriers to the surface of the silicon just under the oxide, and once enough of them gather they form a conducting channel between the drain and the source. The electric field from the gate therefore controls the channel, hence the name field-effect.

Two consequences follow. Because the oxide is an insulator, there is no DC path into the gate, so it draws almost no steady current. Because the gate is a capacitance, changing its voltage moves charge, which the pin delivers at switch-on and removes at switch-off, so a fast switch needs a large pin current for a short time. The datasheet quantifies both effects, and we use its numbers below.

An N-channel device conducts when the gate is positive relative to the source, and a P-channel device is its mirror image, conducting when the gate is negative relative to the source. This lesson uses an N-channel part, the IRLZ44N, the usual choice when a microcontroller switches a load to ground, and the next section shows where it goes.

Low-side and high-side switches

Figure 1 shows the two places a switch can sit. In a low-side switch the MOSFET sits between the load and ground, with the load's other end on the supply rail, so its source is at ground and a 3.3 V pin on the gate turns it on directly. A high-side switch sits between the supply and the load, where an N-channel device would need its gate above the supply rail, so that position calls for a P-channel device or a gate driver. For a microcontroller, the low-side N-channel switch is the simplest circuit that works.

Threshold voltage, overdrive and logic-level parts

The threshold VGS(th) is the gate-source voltage at which the channel just starts to conduct, which is a long way from the voltage at which the switch is on. For a low resistance the gate must sit well above threshold, and the margin it has is called the overdrive:

Vov = VGS − VGS(th)(1)

where VGS is the voltage from gate to source and VGS(th) is the threshold. The channel resistance falls as the overdrive grows, so every on-resistance figure in a datasheet belongs to a stated gate voltage.

A logic-level MOSFET is one whose datasheet specifies the on-resistance at 4 V or 5 V, close to what a 5 V logic output delivers. The IRLZ44N, for example, lists its on-resistance at VGS = 4.0 V and 5.0 V and carries the label "logic-level gate drive" (IRLZ44N datasheet, first page and Electrical Characteristics). The label says nothing about 3.3 V, so a part specified at 5 V may be only partly on with a 3.3 V gate, and the curve or table has to be read at the gate voltage you actually apply. Parts also spread across the threshold range, which makes the highest threshold the safe one to design with. The IRLZ44N datasheet gives no on-resistance at 3.3 V. Lesson 18 shows how to read a datasheet and spot a gap like this.

Gate charge and switching time

Since the gate is a capacitance, the pin must deliver a fixed charge before the switch changes state, and the time that takes is the charge divided by the pin current:

t = QG ÷ I(2)

where QG is the total gate charge and I is the pin's drive current. The IRLZ44N datasheet gives QG as at most 48 nC, measured with 25 A of drain current, 44 V from drain to source and a 5.0 V gate, so it is the charge that took the gate to 5 V in that test. Before reading on, guess whether the time is nanoseconds, microseconds or milliseconds.

  1. Charge needed. The datasheet figure is the only charge we have: QG = 48 nC = 48 × 10−9 C.
  2. Time at the default pin drive. The pad's default drive is 4 mA, so t = Q ÷ I = 48 × 10−9 ÷ 4 × 10−3 = 12 µs.
  3. Time at 8 mA. Doubling the drive halves the time, so with the pad set to 8 mA, t = 48 nC ÷ 8 mA = 6 µs. The pad drive settings are described in the RP2040 datasheet, section 2.19.6.3.
  4. What it means. For a heater that switches a few times a minute, 12 µs is negligible, and even PWM at a few kilohertz has a much longer period. Charge and pin current together set the time, so a design that must switch fast needs the stronger drive or a gate driver.

The datasheet gives no gate charge at 3.3 V, so treat the 5 V figure as a reference and check the device at your own gate voltage. It is the same gap we found in the on-resistance. The animation below walks through the switch-on in order.

  1. With the gate at 0 V there is no channel between drain and source. The heater has no path to ground.
  2. The pin goes to 3.3 V. The gate is a capacitor, so current flows into it until it is charged: 4 mA at the pad's default drive. The gate voltage climbs.
  3. Past the threshold voltage the channel starts to conduct, but only partly. In this region the MOSFET has both voltage across it and current through it, so it heats up: get through it quickly.
  4. The IRLZ44N needs a gate charge of about 48 nC. At 4 mA, t = 48 nC ÷ 4 mA = 12 µs, and then the MOSFET is fully on with a low on-resistance. (48 nC is the datasheet's figure at 5 V; use it as a reference at 3.3 V.)
  5. Once the gate is charged it draws almost no current, and the pin just holds the voltage. With the lesson's example of 0.05 Ω on-resistance and a 2 A heater, the switch dissipates I² × R = 0.2 W.

Datasheet checks for choosing a MOSFET

Choosing a MOSFET means passing a set of checks, and the IRLZ44N datasheet gives the figures that matter for a 12 V heater. The drain-source breakdown voltage, V(BR)DSS, is 55 V, comfortably above a 12 V supply plus the spikes an inductive load can add. The continuous drain current is 47 A at a case temperature of 25 °C with a 10 V gate, so a 2 A heater leaves ample margin.

The on-resistance is at most 0.035 Ω with a 4.0 V gate and 0.025 Ω with a 5.0 V gate, both measured at 21 A to 25 A of drain current, and the gate threshold is 1.0 to 2.0 V. Without a heatsink, the thermal resistance from junction to ambient is 62 °C/W, and from junction to case it is 1.4 °C/W. Together these figures answer five questions: whether the voltage rating is safe, whether the current rating is safe, whether the switch is fully on at your gate voltage, whether the part will overheat, and whether your pin can deliver the gate charge. A part that fails one, such as an on-resistance quoted only at 10 V, is the wrong part however good its current rating.

Conduction loss in the on-resistance

Once the MOSFET is fully on, it behaves like a small resistor, RDS(on), so its power loss follows from the lesson 6 formula:

P = I² × RDS(on)(3)

where I is the load current and RDS(on) is the on-resistance at the applied gate voltage. For a 2 A load and 0.05 Ω, that is 2² × 0.05 = 0.2 W of heat in the MOSFET. Because the current is squared, the heat grows faster than the current. Ohm's law and the power formula are covered in lessons 5 and 6. Here the MOSFET is just a resistor that is on.

The heated bed of a 3D printer can draw around 10 A, and the mainboard switches it with a low-side MOSFET. With RDS(on) = 0.01 Ω the MOSFET dissipates 10² × 0.01 = 1 W, which a modest copper area on the board can spread. With 0.05 Ω it would dissipate 5 W and need a heatsink. The same pin and the same code drive both, so the choice of part alone decides whether the board stays cool.

Junction temperature rise without a heatsink

Take a 2 A heater and the lesson's example on-resistance of 0.05 Ω. Without a heatsink the junction rises above the surrounding air by

ΔT = P × RθJA(4)

where P is the dissipated power and RθJA is the junction-to-ambient thermal resistance in °C/W. You will repeat these steps for every switch.

  1. Power in the switch. Equation (3) gives P = I² × RDS(on) = 2² × 0.05 Ω = 0.2 W.
  2. Temperature rise, no heatsink. With RθJA = 62 °C/W from the IRLZ44N datasheet, equation (4) gives 0.2 W × 62 °C/W = 12.4 °C above ambient. That rough still-air figure shows that 0.2 W in a bare TO-220 package is acceptable.
  3. Compare with a stronger gate drive. The IRLZ44N gives 0.025 Ω with a 5 V gate, so at 2 A the loss is 2² × 0.025 = 0.1 W, half of the example. The on-resistance depends directly on the gate voltage you apply.
  4. Check the margin. The dissipation is tolerable when the junction, after the ambient temperature and any heatsink are taken into account, stays well below the 175 °C maximum operating junction temperature in the datasheet's absolute maximum ratings table.

Because the loss rises with the square of the current, doubling the current from 2 A to 4 A quadruples the loss, from 0.2 W to 0.8 W. A designer therefore also checks the worst-case current, such as a stall or a start-up surge.

Inductive loads and the flyback diode

A heater is resistive and needs nothing extra. A relay coil, a solenoid or a motor is inductive, so when the MOSFET switches off, the coil current keeps flowing into the drain and the drain voltage can spike far above the supply. A flyback diode across the coil, reverse-biased in normal operation, gives that current a path and so limits the spike. Lesson 14 measures the spike and shows how a snubber limits it.

Gate-drive code for the RP2040

The firmware is simpler than the transistor stage in lesson 11, because the pin only has to charge the gate and then hold it. The code below configures the pin for a heater, sets the pad to 8 mA so the gate charges in about 6 µs, and starts with the heater off. 

#include "pico/stdlib.h"
#include "hardware/gpio.h"

#define HEATER_GATE_PIN 15   // GPIO that drives the MOSFET gate, through a series resistor

static void heater_init(void) {
    gpio_init(HEATER_GATE_PIN);                                        // SIO function, starts as an input
    gpio_set_dir(HEATER_GATE_PIN, GPIO_OUT);                           // now it can drive the gate
    gpio_set_drive_strength(HEATER_GATE_PIN, GPIO_DRIVE_STRENGTH_8MA); // 8 mA: charges the gate in about 6 us
    gpio_put(HEATER_GATE_PIN, 0);                                      // start with the heater off
}

static void heater_set(bool on) {
    gpio_put(HEATER_GATE_PIN, on);                                     // high turns the MOSFET on, low turns it off
}

gpio_init and gpio_set_dir make the pin an output, as in lesson 11. gpio_set_drive_strength sets the pad's DRIVE field so the pin can supply the gate charge quickly, and gpio_put(..., 0) runs before anything else can turn the heater on, so the heater starts off. heater_set writes the level to the pin, which switches the MOSFET. Because the gate is a capacitor, the pin pushes current only while the gate charges, which is why the pin's drive sets the switching speed.

A real design also needs a pull-down resistor from the gate to ground, because the pin is an undriven input until heater_init runs, and the code cannot control the gate before then. The mistake about a floating gate, further down, shows what happens without it.

Drain current against gate voltage in CircuitJS

Now we can watch the threshold and the overdrive at work in a simulation. The built-in example has a 5 V drain supply and an adjustable gate supply, and it models a MOSFET with a 1.5 V threshold and a transconductance parameter β of 0.02. The drain voltage exceeds the overdrive, so the device is in saturation, where the drain current is

ID = ½ × β × (VGS − VGS(th))²(5)

where β is in A/V² and the bracket is the overdrive from equation (1). In each step, predict the current with equation (5) before you read it.

  1. Open the example: CircuitJS: nmosfet.txt. Its gate and drain are both supplies with sliders.
  2. Set the Gate Voltage slider to 1.5 V. The drain current should be about zero, because the gate is at threshold and the overdrive is zero.
  3. Set the gate to 2.0 V. Predict first: the overdrive is 0.5 V, so ID = ½ × 0.02 × 0.5² = 2.5 mA. Then select the MOSFET and read ID in its info readout, which should be close to that.
  4. Set the gate to 3.3 V and predict again before you read: the overdrive is 3.3 − 1.5 = 1.8 V, so ID = ½ × 0.02 × 1.8² = 32.4 mA. Read ID and write the value down.
  5. Compare the three readings. The current rises with the square of the overdrive, so a gate only a little above threshold leaves the switch only partly on.
Hint

If the readout does not show the current, click the MOSFET once to select it. The threshold and β are shown in the element's properties dialog, so check them before you trust the prediction.

Your reading should match the prediction within a few per cent. If it does not, check which terminal is the drain and whether the gate slider really reads 3.3 V.

You measured a MOSFET's drain current against its gate voltage, and predicted the value from the threshold and the overdrive. That is how you check that a switch is fully on at the voltage your pin provides.

Common mistakes with MOSFET switches

A standard MOSFET driven from a 3.3 V pin

The heater runs weak, the MOSFET gets warm, and the drain-source voltage stays well above 0 V while the gate is high. The part is only partly on, because its datasheet specifies the on-resistance at 10 V and the pin delivers 3.3 V. Measuring the voltage across the switch with the load on confirms it, since a healthy switch shows a few tenths of a volt or less. The fix is a logic-level part, followed by a check of its on-resistance at your gate voltage, as the datasheet checks describe.

An N-channel MOSFET on the high side

The load runs weakly or not at all, even though the gate pin goes high. With the load between the source and ground, the source follows the gate and settles about one threshold below it, at Vgate − VGS(th). With the gate at 3.3 V and the lesson's 1.5 V threshold the source reaches only about 1.8 V, so VGS is just 1.5 V with almost no overdrive, and a part with a 2.0 V threshold stays off. The fix is to move the switch to the low side, as in the section on low-side and high-side switches, or to use a P-channel device or a gate driver.

A floating gate during reset and boot

The heater switches on for a moment at power-up, or a fan spins whenever the board is reset. A pin is not an output until the firmware configures it, and before that it is an input with no defined level, so a gate with nothing holding it low can drift above its threshold and the MOSFET conducts. The fix is a pull-down resistor from the gate to ground, typically around 10 kΩ, which holds the gate low whenever the pin is not driving it. It is the mirror image of the pull-up resistor in lesson 16.

An inductive load without a flyback diode

A fan or a relay works for days and then fails, because the switch-off spike has gradually broken down the MOSFET. The fix is a flyback diode across the coil, as the section on inductive loads describes, and a check of the MOSFET's voltage rating against the supply plus the spike.

On-resistance taken at the wrong gate voltage

The switch is fully on in the bench test with a 5 V gate, and only partly on when the same board drives it from a 3.3 V pin. The on-resistance was read at a gate voltage the design never applies. The fix is to read the on-resistance at the gate voltage you will actually use, or to design with the worst-case value, and then to measure the drop across the switch on the board.

MOSFET load switches in real products

Low-side MOSFET switches sit behind heater outputs, fan drives, LED strings, solenoid valves and the motor driver stage behind a pin. Professional designs run the same checks and usually add three things this lesson left out: a pull-down on the gate, a series resistor in the gate lead to damp the ringing from the fast switching edge, and a current or temperature sense where the load is critical. Heater firmware, for example, usually reads a temperature sensor and turns the MOSFET off if the reading goes wrong.

On a 3D printer the bed and hotend heaters are switched this way, with the heater pins named in a firmware configuration file. A reviewer of that firmware checks that every heater starts off at reset, that the gate pin is configured before anything else runs, and that a fault in the temperature sensor turns the heater off. These are the mistakes above, checked in the code instead of the circuit.

Summary

  • A MOSFET is switched by its gate voltage. The gate is insulated, so it draws almost no steady current, but it is a capacitance that must be charged.
  • An N-channel low-side switch between the load and ground can be driven directly from a 3.3 V pin, if the part is logic-level.
  • The threshold, VGS(th), is where the channel starts to conduct. Full conduction needs more gate voltage, and the on-resistance is only given at the gate voltages the datasheet states.
  • Conduction loss is P = I² × RDS(on), and the temperature rise is P × RθJA without a heatsink.
  • Gate charge sets the switching time: t = QG ÷ I. The IRLZ44N has 48 nC at 5 V, which takes 12 µs from a 4 mA pin.
  • An inductive load needs a flyback diode across the coil, and a gate pull-down keeps the switch off during reset.
  • Check the voltage rating, the current rating, the on-resistance at your gate voltage, the thermal limit and the gate charge before you choose a part.
