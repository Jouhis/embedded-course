MOSFETs: switching a load with a pin

A GPIO pin gives only a few milliamps. A MOSFET lets that pin switch a 2 A heater, and this lesson shows how to choose one.

By the end of this lesson you can size an N-channel MOSFET low-side switch for a 3.3 V gate drive: check that the gate is fully on, calculate the power it dissipates, and say where its flyback diode goes.

Where we are and why it matters

Lesson 11 showed a transistor switching a load from a pin, and the cost of doing it: a base resistor, a base current that the pin must supply, and a gain that varies from part to part. Lesson 6 gave you the power formula and the heating it causes. This lesson replaces the bipolar transistor with a MOSFET, which is common in real power switches, and it shows what the switch costs in heat.

Many powered boards have this problem. A 3D printer's heated bed draws several amps. A fan, a solenoid valve or a heater cartridge does the same. None of those can come from a pin, and a bipolar transistor for a few amps needs a base drive that is awkward to produce. A MOSFET needs almost no current to hold its gate on, so a 3.3 V pin can switch a 2 A load with a few milliamps of drive. The questions to ask are "is the switch fully on at my pin's voltage?" and "how hot does it get?" The rest of the lesson answers those two questions.

The idea

A MOSFET is a switch controlled by a voltage. Its gate is a capacitor-like terminal, so it draws almost no steady current. The drain and source are the two ends of the switched channel. For an N-channel device the channel conducts when the gate is higher than the source by more than the threshold voltage, VGS(th) (IRLZ44N datasheet, Electrical Characteristics: VGS(th) 1.0 to 2.0 V). The gate is isolated by an oxide layer, so DC gate current is essentially zero. The GPIO pin only has to charge the gate capacitance once per switch-on.

Picture the gate as a small bucket that must be filled before the switch turns over. The pin is a hose with a limited flow, so filling the bucket takes a moment. Once it is full, it stays full with no flow at all. That is why a MOSFET takes current from the pin only while it switches, and why a weak pin turns a big MOSFET on slowly.

What the gate does physically

A MOSFET stands for metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor. Its name describes the structure. A metal or polysilicon gate sits on top of a thin layer of oxide, which is an insulator, and the oxide sits on top of a silicon body. The drain and source are two heavily doped regions in that body, separated by the body itself. The gate, the oxide and the body together form a capacitor, and you met capacitors as storage devices in lesson 13. Put a voltage between the gate and the source and the capacitor charges. Charge on the gate attracts carriers to the surface of the silicon under the oxide, and when enough carriers gather, they form a conducting channel between the drain and the source. That is why the device is called a field-effect transistor: the electric field from the gate controls the channel.

Two consequences follow, and both matter for a designer. First, the oxide is an insulator, so there is no DC path from the gate into the device, and the gate draws almost no steady current. Second, the gate is a capacitance, so changing its voltage takes charge. The pin has to deliver that charge when the device switches on, and remove it when the device switches off. A fast switch needs a larger pin current for a short time, and a slow switch needs less. Both effects are measured in the datasheet, and we will use the numbers below.

An N-channel device conducts when the gate is positive relative to the source. A P-channel device is the mirror image: it conducts when the gate is negative relative to the source, so the channel is made of the opposite carriers. The low-side switch in this lesson uses an N-channel device, which is the common choice for a microcontroller's low-side load, and the IRLZ44N in the datasheets below is one. The gate-source voltage that just starts the channel is the threshold, which the next section explains.

Low side or high side

In a low-side switch the MOSFET sits between the load and ground. The load's other end is on the supply rail, and the gate is referenced to ground, so a 3.3 V pin turns the device on directly. A high-side switch puts the MOSFET between the supply and the load. An N-channel device there needs its gate above the supply rail, so you need a P-channel device, or a gate driver that generates that voltage. A low-side N-channel switch is the simplest choice for a microcontroller.

Fully on is not the same as threshold

The gate-source voltage that just starts the channel conducting is VGS(th). It is not the voltage at which the switch is on. To get a low resistance, the gate must be well above threshold. The extra voltage above threshold is the overdrive, VGS − VGS(th). A logic-level MOSFET is one whose datasheet specifies the on-resistance at 4 V or 5 V, which is close to a 5 V logic output. The IRLZ44N, for example, lists its on-resistance at VGS = 4.0 V and 5.0 V, and it is marked "logic-level gate drive" (IRLZ44N datasheet, first page and Electrical Characteristics). The IRLZ44N datasheet gives no on-resistance at 3.3 V. Lesson 18 shows how to read a datasheet and spot a gap like this.

Do not assume that a 3.3 V gate fully enhances a part that is only specified at 5 V. Check the datasheet curve or table at your gate voltage. Real parts also vary, so a worst-case threshold is the safe number to design with.

Worked example: how fast can a 3.3 V pin turn it on?

The gate's capacitance means the pin must deliver charge before the switch changes state. The IRLZ44N datasheet gives the total gate charge, QG, as 48 nC at a drain current of 25 A, a drain-source voltage of 44 V and a gate-source voltage of 5.0 V. Those are the test conditions, so the 48 nC is the charge needed to take the gate up to 5 V in the test.

  1. Charge needed. QG = 48 nC = 48 × 10−9 C.
  2. Time at the default pin drive. The pad's default drive is 4 mA. t = Q ÷ I = 48 nC ÷ 4 mA = 48 × 10−9 ÷ 4 × 10−3 = 12 µs.
  3. Time at 8 mA. With the pad set to 8 mA, t = 48 nC ÷ 8 mA = 6 µs. The datasheet's pad drive settings are described in the RP2040 datasheet, section 2.19.6.3.
  4. What it means. For a heater that switches a few times a minute, 12 µs is nothing. For PWM at a few kilohertz it is still small against the period. The point is that the time is set by charge and pin current together. A design that switches fast needs the stronger drive, or a gate driver.

The datasheet gives no gate charge at 3.3 V. The 48 nC figure is at 5 V, so use it as the reference and check the device at your own gate voltage, not as a guarantee. This is the same gap that appears in the on-resistance, and it is why the design check in the next section matters.

  1. With the gate at 0 V there is no channel between drain and source. The heater has no path to ground.
  2. The pin goes to 3.3 V. The gate is a capacitor, so current flows into it until it is charged: 4 mA at the pad's default drive. The gate voltage climbs.
  3. Past the threshold voltage the channel starts to conduct, but only partly. In this region the MOSFET has both voltage across it and current through it, so it heats up: get through it quickly.
  4. The IRLZ44N needs a gate charge of about 48 nC. At 4 mA, t = 48 nC ÷ 4 mA = 12 µs, and then the MOSFET is fully on with a low on-resistance. (48 nC is the datasheet's figure at 5 V; use it as a reference at 3.3 V.)
  5. Once the gate is charged it draws almost no current, and the pin just holds the voltage. With the lesson's example of 0.05 Ω on-resistance and a 2 A heater, the switch dissipates I² × R = 0.2 W.

Choosing a part: a checklist from the datasheet

A MOSFET choice is a set of checks, not a single number. From the IRLZ44N datasheet, the numbers that matter for a 12 V heater are these. The drain-source breakdown voltage, V(BR)DSS, is 55 V, which is comfortably above a 12 V supply plus the spikes an inductive load can add. The continuous drain current is 47 A at a case temperature of 25 °C and a gate-source voltage of 10 V, far above a 2 A heater, so the current rating has plenty of margin. The on-resistance is 0.035 Ω at a gate-source voltage of 4.0 V and 0.025 Ω at 5.0 V, both at 21 A to 25 A of drain current. The gate threshold is 1.0 to 2.0 V. The thermal resistance from junction to ambient, without a heatsink, is 62 °C/W, and from junction to case it is 1.4 °C/W.

Each line of that checklist answers a question. Is the voltage rating safe? Is the current rating safe? Is the switch fully on at my gate voltage? Will the part overheat? Is the gate charge within what my pin can deliver? A part that passes all five is a reasonable choice. A part that fails one, such as an on-resistance quoted only at 10 V, is not, even if its current rating is perfect.

Conduction loss

When the MOSFET is on it behaves like a resistor, RDS(on). Its power loss is therefore P = I² × RDS(on). For a 2 A load and a 0.05 Ω on-resistance, that is 2² × 0.05 = 0.2 W of heat in the MOSFET. Heat is the design limit. A larger current costs more than proportionally, because the loss rises with the square of the current. Ohm's law and the power formula are covered in lessons 5 and 6. Here the MOSFET is just a resistor that is on.

A 3D printer's heated bed can draw around 10 A, and the mainboard switches it with a MOSFET on the low side. With RDS(on) = 0.01 Ω the MOSFET dissipates 10² × 0.01 = 1 W, which a small copper area on the board can spread. With 0.05 Ω it would be 5 W, and the part would need a heatsink. The same pin and the same code drive both: the choice of part decides whether the board stays cool.

Worked example: the heat, step by step

Take a 2 A heater with a MOSFET whose on-resistance is 0.05 Ω, the lesson's example value. Work through the numbers in order, because each step is a design check you will repeat for every switch.

  1. Power in the switch. P = I² × RDS(on) = 2² × 0.05 Ω = 0.2 W.
  2. Temperature rise, no heatsink. The junction rises above the ambient air by about P × RθJA. With RθJA = 62 °C/W from the IRLZ44N datasheet, that is 0.2 W × 62 °C/W = 12.4 °C above ambient. That is a rough figure for a part in still air, and it shows that a 0.2 W switch on a bare TO-220 package is acceptable.
  3. Compare with a better part. The IRLZ44N gives 0.025 Ω at a gate voltage of 5 V. At 2 A the loss is 2² × 0.025 = 0.1 W, half of the example. The on-resistance you get depends directly on the gate voltage you apply, which is why the earlier section mattered.
  4. Check the margin. Dissipation is tolerable when the junction stays well below the maximum of 175 °C that the datasheet gives for the operating junction temperature, after the ambient temperature and any heatsink are added. The datasheet's absolute maximum table is where that number is stated.

The loss rises with the square of the current. Doubling the current from 2 A to 4 A quadruples the loss, from 0.2 W to 0.8 W. That is why a designer checks the worst-case current, such as a stall or a start-up surge, and not only the steady value.

Inductive loads and the flyback diode

A heater is resistive, so it needs nothing extra. A relay coil, a solenoid or a motor is inductive. When the MOSFET switches off, the coil pushes its current into the drain, and the drain voltage can spike far above the supply. A flyback diode across the coil, reverse-biased in normal operation, gives that current a path, so the spike is limited. Lesson 14 measures the spike and shows how a snubber limits it.

Try it in CircuitJS

The built-in MOSFET example uses a 5 V drain supply and a gate supply you can change. It is a simulation of a MOSFET with a threshold of 1.5 V and a transconductance parameter β of 0.02. For a MOSFET in saturation, which is the case here because the drain voltage is larger than the overdrive, the current is ID = ½ × β × (VGS − VGS(th))².

  1. Open the example: CircuitJS: nmosfet.txt. Its gate and drain are both supplies with sliders.
  2. Set the Gate Voltage slider to 1.5 V. The drain current should be about zero, because the gate is at threshold and the overdrive is zero.
  3. Set the gate to 2.0 V. Select the MOSFET and read ID in its info readout. Predict first: the overdrive is 0.5 V, so ID = ½ × 0.02 × 0.5² = 2.5 mA. The readout should be close to that.
  4. Set the gate to 3.3 V. Read ID again and write down the value. Predict before you read: the overdrive is 3.3 − 1.5 = 1.8 V, so ID = ½ × 0.02 × 1.8² = 32.4 mA.
  5. Compare the three readings. The current does not rise in a straight line with the gate voltage. It rises with the square of the overdrive, so each extra volt above threshold adds more current than the last. This is the square law the formula describes, and it is why a gate a little above threshold is only partly on.
Hint

If the readout does not show the current, click the MOSFET once to select it. The threshold and β are shown in the element's properties dialog, so check them before you trust the prediction.

Your measured value should match the prediction within a few per cent. If it does not, check which terminal is the drain and which is the source, and whether the gate slider reads 3.3 V.

You measured a MOSFET's drain current against its gate voltage, and predicted the value from the threshold and the overdrive. That is how you check that a switch is fully on at the voltage your pin provides.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

Mistake 1: a standard MOSFET on a 3.3 V pin

The symptom is a heater that runs weak, a MOSFET that gets warm with the load on, and a drain-source voltage that stays well above 0 V while the gate is high. The part is only partly on, because its datasheet specifies the on-resistance at 10 V and not at 3.3 V. Measure the voltage across the switch with the load on. A healthy switch shows a few tenths of a volt or less. The fix is a logic-level part, and then a check of the on-resistance at your gate voltage, as the checklist above describes.

Mistake 2: an N-channel device on the high side

The symptom is a load that runs weakly, or not at all, even though the gate pin goes high. With the load between the source and ground, the source follows the gate and sits about one threshold below it, at Vgate − VGS(th). With the gate at 3.3 V and the lesson's 1.5 V threshold, the source reaches only about 1.8 V, so VGS is just 1.5 V and the overdrive is almost zero. A part with a 2.0 V threshold would be off. The fix is to move the switch to the low side, as in the first section, or to use a P-channel device or a gate driver.

Mistake 3: the gate floats during reset or boot

The symptom is a heater that switches on for a moment at power-up, or a fan that spins when the board is reset. A pin is not an output until the firmware configures it, and before that it is an input with no defined level. A gate with nothing holding it low can drift above its threshold, and the MOSFET then conducts. The fix is a pull-down resistor from the gate to ground, typically around 10 kΩ, so the gate is held low whenever the pin is not driving it. This is the mirror image of the pull-up resistor in lesson 16.

Mistake 4: no flyback diode on an inductive load

The symptom is a fan or a relay that works for days, then fails: the MOSFET has been broken down by the switch-off spike. A resistive heater does not need a diode, but a relay coil, a solenoid or a motor does. Put the flyback diode across the coil, as the next section explains, and check the MOSFET's voltage rating against the supply plus the spike.

Mistake 5: a design that depends on a value at a different gate voltage

The symptom is a switch that is fully on in the bench test with a 5 V gate, and only partly on when the same board is powered from a pin at 3.3 V. The fix is to read the on-resistance at the gate voltage you will actually use, or to design with the worst-case value, and to measure the switch on the board.

Code: drive the gate from a pin

The gate stage in C is simpler than the transistor stage in lesson 11, because the pin only has to charge the gate and then hold it. The code below configures the pin for a heater. The pad is set to 8 mA so the gate charges in about 6 µs, as the worked example showed, and the heater starts off. 

#include "pico/stdlib.h"
#include "hardware/gpio.h"

#define HEATER_GATE_PIN 15   // GPIO that drives the MOSFET gate, through a series resistor

static void heater_init(void) {
    gpio_init(HEATER_GATE_PIN);                                        // SIO function, starts as an input
    gpio_set_dir(HEATER_GATE_PIN, GPIO_OUT);                           // now it can drive the gate
    gpio_set_drive_strength(HEATER_GATE_PIN, GPIO_DRIVE_STRENGTH_8MA); // 8 mA: charges the gate in about 6 us
    gpio_put(HEATER_GATE_PIN, 0);                                      // start with the heater off
}

static void heater_set(bool on) {
    gpio_put(HEATER_GATE_PIN, on);                                     // high turns the MOSFET on, low turns it off
}

Line by line: gpio_init and gpio_set_dir make the pin an output, as in lesson 11. gpio_set_drive_strength sets the pad's DRIVE field, so the pin can supply the charge the gate needs quickly. gpio_put(..., 0) is written before anything else turns the heater on, so the heater starts off. heater_set writes the level to the pin, and the MOSFET follows. The gate is a capacitor, so the level has to be held only long enough to charge it. After that the pin supplies almost no current, which is why the pin, not the transistor, sets the switching speed.

A real design also needs a pull-down resistor from the gate to ground, which the code cannot provide. The next mistake explains why.

In real projects

MOSFET low-side switches are common on real boards: heater outputs, fan drives, LED strings, solenoid valves and the motor driver stage behind a pin. Professional designs follow the same checks as this lesson, and they usually add three things that the lesson did not. They add a pull-down on the gate, a series resistor in the gate lead to damp the ringing from the fast switching edge, and a current or temperature sense where the load is critical. A heater, for example, is often controlled by firmware that reads a temperature sensor and turns the MOSFET off if the reading goes wrong.

On a 3D printer, the heated bed and the hotend heaters are switched this way, and their firmware names the heater pins in a configuration file. A reviewer of that firmware checks that every heater starts off at reset, that the switch is driven from a pin that is configured before anything else, and that a fault in the temperature sensor turns the heater off. Those are the same questions as in the mistakes above, asked in code.

Summary

  • A MOSFET is switched by its gate voltage. The gate is insulated, so it draws almost no steady current, but it is a capacitance that must be charged.
  • An N-channel low-side switch between the load and ground can be driven directly from a 3.3 V pin, if the part is logic-level.
  • The threshold, VGS(th), is where the channel starts to conduct. Full conduction needs more gate voltage, and the on-resistance is only given at the gate voltages the datasheet states.
  • Conduction loss is P = I² × RDS(on), and the temperature rise is P × RθJA without a heatsink.
  • Gate charge sets the switching time: t = QG ÷ I. The IRLZ44N has 48 nC at 5 V, which takes 12 µs from a 4 mA pin.
  • An inductive load needs a flyback diode across the coil, and a gate pull-down keeps the switch off during reset.
  • Check the voltage rating, the current rating, the on-resistance at your gate voltage, the thermal limit and the gate charge before you choose a part.
