BJT transistors as switches

A GPIO pin can switch a few milliamps. A transistor lets a small base current control a much larger collector current, which is how a pin switches a relay, a fan or a heater.

By the end of this lesson you can size a base resistor for an NPN switch on a 3.3 V GPIO, explain saturation and why it wastes less power than the in-between region, and say why a pin cannot drive a relay coil directly.

Where we are and why it matters

Lesson 9 gave you the first real decision on a GPIO pin: how big a series resistor an LED needs, and why the pin's drive limit matters. Lesson 10 showed how to measure the current that decision produces. Both lessons assumed the load was small. An LED takes a few milliamps. The pin can supply that, and a resistor is enough to set the current.

Most real loads are bigger. A relay coil pulls tens of milliamps. A cooling fan pulls hundreds. A heater pulls amps. None of them can be driven straight from a pin, and the reason is the same one lesson 9 gave for the LED: the pin can only supply a small current before its voltage starts to sag and its drive is exceeded. The fix is to let the pin control a switch that is itself powered from a separate supply. In electronics, that switch is usually a transistor. Almost every output stage on a board, from a status LED driver to a buzzer, is a transistor or a MOSFET with a resistor on its control input. Learning the transistor as a switch comes before the other output stages you will design.

The idea: a small current controls a large one

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is a three-terminal semiconductor device. Its three terminals are the base, the collector and the emitter. An NPN transistor is one of the two kinds of BJT, and the one you will use most. The name describes the layers inside it: a thin base region sits between an emitter and a collector, and the layers alternate between negative-doped and positive-doped semiconductor. To use the part, you need its terminals and what they do. The physics of doping is not needed here.

The base-emitter pair behaves like a diode, the one you met in lesson 9. It conducts once the base is about 0.7 V above the emitter, and below that it draws almost no current. When it conducts, a small base current, written IB, flows into the base. That small current allows a much larger collector current, IC, to flow from the collector to the emitter. The ratio of the two currents is the current gain, written β or hFE, so that IC = β × IB. The gain is not a constant. It depends on the current, the temperature and the part, and a datasheet gives a range. Design with the worst case, not the typical number. The All About Circuits textbook covers the transistor's operating regions in detail.

Read the gain as a ratio of currents, not as a voltage gain. A current gain of 100 means that 1 mA into the base permits up to 100 mA through the collector, if the rest of the circuit allows it. The collector current is not set by the base current alone. It is set by the supply and the load. The base current only has to be large enough to let the transistor do what the load asks. A switch therefore needs a base current that is big enough, and no bigger.

Three regions, and the one you want

A transistor can operate in three regions. Used as an amplifier, it sits in the middle one, the active region, where the collector current follows the base current in proportion. Used as a switch, you want the two ends of that range instead. In cut-off, the base current is zero, the collector current is zero, and the transistor is an open switch. The collector sits near the supply voltage, and the load is off.

In saturation, the base current is large enough that the collector current is set by the load, not by the transistor. The collector-emitter voltage falls to a small fraction of a volt, and the transistor behaves as a closed switch with a small drop across it. The load is fully on.

The in-between region is the one to avoid. The transistor is partly on, the collector-emitter voltage is somewhere between the supply and a fraction of a volt, and both the current and the voltage are large. Power is voltage times current, as lesson 6 showed, so the transistor gets hot. The same load that the transistor could switch cleanly in saturation becomes a heater inside the transistor if the base drive is too weak. A switch stuck halfway is a common failure on the bench.

Why the base needs a resistor

The base-emitter junction is a diode, and lesson 9 showed why a diode must not be connected straight to a voltage source. Above its forward voltage, its current rises very steeply, so a small change in voltage gives a large change in current. A pin driving the base directly would hold the base at about 3.3 V, the base-emitter drop would settle near 0.7 V, and the base current would be set by nothing but the pin's own internal resistance. That current could be too large, and it would be damaging to the pin.

The base resistor, Rb in the figure, sets the base current. It takes the voltage that is left over after the base-emitter drop, and it drops that voltage across a resistance, so the current is controlled. This is the same job the resistor did for the LED in lesson 9, with one difference: the diode here is the base-emitter junction, and the current you control is the base current, not the load current.

Worked example with real numbers

Goal: switch a 5 V relay coil of 100 Ω from a Pico GPIO at 3.3 V. Work through the steps in order. Each one depends on the one before it, and each one uses a value you can check against a datasheet.

  1. Collector current. In saturation the collector-emitter voltage is small. The PN2222A datasheet gives a maximum of 0.3 V at a collector current of 150 mA with a base current of 15 mA, which is a forced gain of 10. At 48 mA the drop is lower, so 0.2 V is a reasonable estimate. IC = (5 V − 0.2 V) ÷ 100 Ω = 48 mA. The GPIO cannot supply that much, which is the reason for the transistor.
  2. Base current. Pick the base current so the forced gain IC ÷ IB is about 10 or less. The real β is then higher than the forced gain, so the transistor stays saturated when β varies. The PN2222A datasheet guarantees a DC current gain of at least 75 at 10 mA and at least 100 at 150 mA, both at VCE = 10 V, so a forced gain of about 10 leaves a wide margin. IB = 48 mA ÷ 10 = 4.8 mA is the minimum.
  3. Base resistor. The base-emitter junction takes about 0.7 V, so RB = (3.3 V − 0.7 V) ÷ IB. For 4.8 mA, that is 2.6 V ÷ 0.0048 A = 542 Ω. Choose the E12 value at or below 542 Ω, which is 470 Ω. Rounding down here gives more base current, which is the safe direction for a switch.
  4. Check. With 470 Ω, IB = 2.6 V ÷ 470 Ω = 5.5 mA, and the forced gain is 48 ÷ 5.5 = 8.7, which is below 10. The pin must source 5.5 mA. The RP2040 datasheet gives the default drive as 4 mA, which is too small, so set the pad to 8 mA. RP2040 datasheet, section 2.19.4 for the drive strengths and section 2.19.6.3 for the pad register.

The calculation starts from the load, the coil on the 5 V supply, and works back to the one number the pin controls. The transistor is the amplifier that turns a 5.5 mA control current into a 48 mA load current, and the datasheet values are the checks that the amplifier will work across parts and temperatures.

Why a pin cannot drive the coil directly

The coil needs 48 mA. The RP2040 pad's highest drive setting is 12 mA, and the total across the IOVDD bank is limited to 50 mA for all pins together. The pin could only supply a small part of what the coil needs, and it would hold its voltage poorly while doing it. The transistor is the amplifier: the pin provides the small base current, and the supply provides the coil current.

Look at what happens if you try anyway. The pin drives 48 mA into a 3.3 V output. The output voltage falls, because the pad's internal resistance drops voltage when it carries current, so the pin no longer reads as a clean logic high. The pin is then under stress, and the rest of the chip shares the same supply bank. The circuit may appear to work on one test and fail the next, which is the worst kind of fault on a board. The datasheet is clear that the pad is designed to drive a small load, and a coil is not a small load.

Power in the transistor

The reason to aim for saturation shows up in the power dissipated inside the transistor. Power is the product of voltage and current, from lesson 6, and the transistor's dissipation is the collector-emitter voltage times the collector current. In saturation, with a drop of 0.2 V and 48 mA, that is 0.2 V × 0.048 A = 9.6 mW. The PN2222A datasheet lists a power dissipation of 625 mW, so a 9.6 mW device is far inside its limit.

Now take the same transistor, but with a base resistor so large that it sits in the active region with 2.5 V across it. The power becomes 2.5 V × 0.048 A = 120 mW. That is still under the limit, but it is more than ten times as much heat, and a few seconds of operation in that state can heat the package. A slightly weaker base drive, or a supply that drops a little, can push the transistor into that region without any obvious sign until the part warms up. Always check that the base drive is enough for the load, not just enough to turn the transistor on.

A transistor switching an inductive load (a relay coil or a motor) needs a flyback diode across the load, or the coil's back-EMF will destroy the transistor when it switches off. Lesson 14 covers this.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

The first three mistakes below are the ones most beginners make. Each has a symptom you can see on the bench, and each has a fix you can make in minutes.

Mistake 1: driving the load straight from the pin

The symptom is a load that turns on weakly, flickers, or pulls the pin voltage down so the microcontroller resets when the load switches on. A coil that will not pull in, or a fan that starts and stalls, often means the pin is supplying far less current than the load needs. The fix is the transistor stage in this lesson. Measure the voltage on the pin with a meter while the load is on. If it falls far below 3.3 V, the pin is carrying too much current.

Mistake 2: the base resistor too large

The symptom is a transistor that gets warm, a load that is only partly on, and a collector voltage that stays well above 0.2 V when the load should be fully on. The base current is too small to saturate the transistor, so it sits in the active region. Measure the collector-emitter voltage with the load on. If it is more than about 0.5 V, check the base resistor against the worked example. A smaller resistor gives more base current, but do not go below the value that the pin can supply.

Mistake 3: collector and emitter swapped

The symptom is a transistor that does not switch at all, or one that gets hot with the load off. The collector and emitter are not interchangeable, and a swapped pair often works poorly in one direction. The fix is to read the pin order from the datasheet before you place the part. For the PN2222A in the TO-92 package, pin 1 is the emitter, pin 2 is the base, and pin 3 is the collector, as the datasheet's package drawing shows. Check the orientation of the flat face against that drawing, not against the part's appearance.

Mistake 4: no flyback diode on a coil

The symptom appears after days or weeks: the relay works for a while, then the transistor fails short, or the relay chatters. The coil's switch-off spike has overstressed the transistor. The fix is a flyback diode across the coil, which lesson 14 explains in full.

Mistake 5: a design that works on one board and not another

The symptom is a switch that works on the bench board and fails on a second board built from the same schematic. The gain varies between parts and temperatures, and the design only worked because the first transistor had a high gain. The fix is to size the base for the minimum gain in the datasheet, using a forced gain of about 10, as the worked example did, so that the switch does not depend on luck.

In real projects

In professional hardware, a transistor switch is one of the most common circuits on a board. It drives indicator LEDs, buzzers, relay coils, and the enable inputs of regulators and sensors. The design rules are the ones in this lesson. Size the base resistor for saturation with a forced gain of about ten. Check the pin's drive strength, and the total current on its supply bank. Put a flyback diode across every inductive load. Then measure the collector-emitter voltage on the bench to confirm the switch is saturated.

A senior engineer reviewing such a stage looks for the same points each time. They check that the base current is supplied within the pin's limit, that the load's current is within the transistor's rating, and that the datasheet's minimum gain is used rather than the typical value. They also check the power in the transistor, not only the current, because the package has a limit that a few watts would exceed. A reviewer who asks "what is the forced gain?" is checking that the author has worked it out.

Transistor switch questions are common in embedded interviews because they test whether you understand the difference between voltage and current drive and the cost of the middle region. Expect to be asked how to switch a load bigger than the pin can drive, and what goes wrong when the base resistor is too big. The model answer below covers both.

Summary

  • The base-emitter junction conducts once the base is about 0.7 V above the emitter, and a small base current IB lets a much larger collector current flow, with IC = β × IB.
  • As a switch, a transistor should be in cut-off or in saturation. The in-between active region wastes power, and that is where the transistor gets hot.
  • In saturation the collector-emitter drop is small. At 0.2 V and 48 mA the transistor dissipates 9.6 mW, well inside the 625 mW limit of the PN2222A.
  • A GPIO pin cannot drive a 48 mA relay coil directly. The RP2040 pad's highest drive setting is 12 mA, and the transistor carries the coil current while the pin supplies only the base current.
  • Size the base resistor for a forced gain of about 10. A base current of 4.8 mA gives RB = 541.7 Ω, so the worked example chooses the E12 value 470 Ω, which rounds down for more base current.
  • Use the datasheet's minimum gain, not the typical value. A switch that works on one board only because its transistor had a high gain fails on the next board.
  • Put a flyback diode across every inductive load, such as a relay coil or a motor, or the back-EMF will destroy the transistor when it switches off.

Code: set the pin that drives the base

The transistor stage is only half of the design. The pin still has to be configured as an output, and it has to have enough drive strength. The C code below does that with the Pico SDK. It sets the pin to output, selects the 8 mA pad drive that the worked example needs, and drives the pin high. 

#include "pico/stdlib.h"
#include "hardware/gpio.h"

#define BASE_PIN 16   // the GPIO that drives the base resistor

int main(void) {
    gpio_init(BASE_PIN);                                        // SIO function, input, driving low: a known starting state
    gpio_set_dir(BASE_PIN, GPIO_OUT);                           // make it an output, so it can drive the base
    gpio_set_drive_strength(BASE_PIN, GPIO_DRIVE_STRENGTH_8MA); // 8 mA pad, enough for 5.5 mA of base current
    gpio_put(BASE_PIN, 1);                                      // drive high: base current flows and the load turns on
    while (true) {
        tight_loop_contents();                                  // do nothing; the pin holds its level
    }
}

Line by line: gpio_init sets the pin's function to SIO, which is the software-controlled GPIO, and leaves it as an input driving low. That gives a known starting state before the next calls change it. gpio_set_dir sets the direction to output, which lets the pad drive the base. gpio_set_drive_strength writes the DRIVE field of the pad control register for that pin. The field has four values, 2, 4, 8 and 12 mA, and the 8 mA value is the one the worked example chose, because 5.5 mA of base current fits within it. gpio_put(BASE_PIN, 1) drives the output high. The loop at the end keeps the program running so the pin keeps its level. tight_loop_contents is an empty function from the SDK, and calling it in a busy loop only marks the wait as deliberate.

The Pico SDK has these functions in hardware/gpio.h, and the drive-strength enum has the four values shown. Lesson 51 covers GPIO output from C in more depth, including reading the pin and changing its level in a loop.

Try it in CircuitJS

The built-in example is an NPN transistor with a base voltage source and a collector voltage source, and no resistors. Its transistor is set to a gain of 100. You will add the two resistors from the worked example, set the two sources, and read the collector current. Each step says what to change and what to expect.

  1. Open the NPN transistor example. It has a base voltage source, labelled Base Voltage, and a collector voltage source, labelled Collector Voltage, and no resistors.
  2. Use the sliders or the element properties to set the base source to 3.3 V, and the collector source to 5 V.
  3. Add a 470 Ω resistor between the base source and the base. Add a 100 Ω resistor between the collector source and the collector. Use the element menu to add each one, and double-click it to set its value.
  4. Select the collector wire, read the collector current, and type it into the question below. Expect a value near 48 mA, because the transistor should be saturated. The simulator's transistor model will not match the arithmetic exactly, so a value within a few per cent is a pass.
  5. Change the base resistor to 10 kΩ and read the collector current again. It should fall to about 26 mA. The base current is now 2.6 V ÷ 10 kΩ = 0.26 mA, and with a gain of 100 that allows only about 26 mA. The transistor is no longer saturated, so the load is only partly on and the transistor runs hot. This is the too-large base resistor from Mistake 2, seen directly.
Hint

Check that the collector current is close to (5 V minus the collector-emitter drop) ÷ 100 Ω. If it is near 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 50 mA, the transistor is saturated. If the reading stays at zero with the 470 Ω resistor, check that the base source is connected to the base through the resistor, and that the base voltage is set to 3.3 V.

Model answer

A GPIO cannot supply 200 mA, so switch the fan with a transistor. A BJT works with a base resistor sized for saturation, using a forced gain of about 10, so the base needs about 20 mA, and the pin must supply that base current within its limit. For a fan this size, a MOSFET (lesson 12) is more usual, because it needs almost no gate current. If the load is inductive, add a flyback diode. Check the pin's drive strength and the total current limit of its supply bank, and check the transistor's power at the operating current.

PN2222A datasheet (Micro Commercial Components): read the maximum ratings (VCEO 40 V, IC 600 mA, PD 625 mW), the pin order on the package drawing, and the electrical characteristics: the DC current gain at each test condition, and the saturation voltages VCE(sat) and VBE(sat) at 150 mA with 15 mA of base current. Use the test conditions in the table, because the gain depends on them.
