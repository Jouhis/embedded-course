Voltage, current, resistance, and Ohm's law

Three quantities describe every resistor in your printer and on your Pico. Ohm's law links them, and you will use it in every lesson of this part.

By the end of this lesson you can work out the current through a resistor from its voltage and resistance, and confirm the value by measuring it in CircuitJS.

The idea

Voltage is the difference in electric potential between two points, measured in volts. It is always a difference: one point is the reference, usually ground at 0 V, and the voltage is how far the other point sits above it. Current is the rate at which charge passes a point, measured in amperes. Resistance is how strongly a component opposes that flow, measured in ohms (Ω). A useful picture: voltage is the push, current is the flow it causes, and resistance is the narrowing of the pipe. The picture breaks down at the edges, so use the equation rather than the picture when you calculate. The definitions follow the standard DC treatment in the All About Circuits DC textbook.

Ohm's law ties the three together: V = I × R. Rearranged, I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I. Double the voltage across a fixed resistor and the current doubles. Double the resistance at the same voltage and the current halves.

Worked examples with real numbers

Example A, a Pico GPIO. The RP2040 I/O runs at 3.3 V. Put a 330 Ω resistor from a GPIO pin to ground. Current = 3.3 V ÷ 330 Ω = 0.010 A, which is 10 mA. The RP2040 datasheet sets the limits on how much current a pin may supply, and lesson 9 uses them. RP2040 datasheet, section 2.19.4

Example B, USB. The Pico's micro-USB input (VBUS) is nominally 5 V. Across a 100 Ω resistor, the current is 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 0.050 A, which is 50 mA. The Pico datasheet gives the 5 V VBUS rail. Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, section 2.1 (pin descriptions)

Notice the units. Every term must be in base units (volts, amperes, ohms) before you multiply or divide. Write 330 Ω, not 0.33 kΩ mixed with volts. The prefix m means ×10−3, so 10 mA is 0.010 A.

Ohm's law describes a resistor. A GPIO pin, an LED or a motor is not a plain resistor, so the equation can only give the current through the component you have modelled as a resistor. Lessons 9 and 11 add the missing terms.

Try it in CircuitJS

Falstad CircuitJS is an in-browser circuit simulator with a built-in voltmeter, ammeter and scope. Part 1 uses it instead of Wokwi, because Wokwi cannot simulate currents in analogue circuits (see the Wokwi resistor notes). The CircuitJS site is free and GPL-licensed. Its menus change over time, so use the names you see on screen.

  1. Open the built-in example Ohm's law in CircuitJS. It shows a 5 V source across two resistors in parallel: 100 Ω and 1 kΩ.
  2. Before you measure anything, work out the current through the 100 Ω resistor by hand: 5 V ÷ 100 Ω.
  3. Click the 100 Ω resistor to select it, and read the current that CircuitJS shows. Check it against your hand answer.
  4. Double-click the 1 kΩ resistor, and change its value to 470 Ω. Then select it and read the current through it. Type that value into the question below, in amperes or with a prefix such as 10.6m.
Hint

The two resistors are in parallel, so each one sees the full 5 V. The current through either resistor does not depend on the other one. Divide 5 V by its own resistance.

You calculated a current from Ohm's law, checked it against a simulated measurement, and changed a resistor value to predict the new result. That is the basic loop of circuit design.

"A 5 V rail feeds a resistor. Someone doubles the resistor's value. What happens to the current, and why?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

The current halves. Ohm's law gives I = V ÷ R with V fixed at 5 V, so doubling R halves I. Saying "it doubles" mixes up the effect of changing the voltage with the effect of changing the resistance.
