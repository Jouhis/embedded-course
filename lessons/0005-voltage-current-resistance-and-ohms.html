Voltage, current, resistance, and Ohm's law

The three basic circuit quantities and the law that links them

By the end of this lesson you can work out the current through a resistor from its voltage and resistance, and confirm the value by measuring it in CircuitJS.

The electrical side of a GPIO pin

The first four lessons were about software and about how this course teaches. You built a project, lit an LED from a GPIO pin in lesson 2, and planned your study time in lesson 4. In the blink you wrote one line of C that set a pin high and the LED lit, without asking what the pin was doing to the LED, the wires and the board around it. This lesson starts on that question, and the electrical lessons in the rest of this part build on it.

The practical problem is easy to state. A GPIO pin can supply only a small current, so an LED or a sensor connected to it must not draw more than the pin can give, while a heater, a fan or the stepper driver on a 3D printer draws far more, and the wrong part can burn out a board. Before choosing any part, you need to say how much voltage is across it and how much current flows through it. Voltage, current and resistance give those answers, and one equation connects them.

Voltage between two points

Voltage is the difference in electric potential between two points, measured in volts (V). Potential is the energy per unit of charge at a point, so a voltage is the energy each unit of charge gains or loses as it moves from one point to the other. You can work with circuits without the full definition, provided you keep one habit: a voltage is always measured between two points and never at a single point.

The habit matters because every voltage in a circuit needs a reference. The reference is usually ground (GND), which is defined as 0 V, so when a datasheet says a pin is at 3.3 V it means 3.3 V above ground. A battery works the same way, since a "9 V battery" has 9 V between its two terminals, and whether one of those terminals sits at 0 V depends on how you connect it.

Height is a useful picture. Water runs downhill because one place is higher than another, and voltage plays the role of that height difference, pushing charge along a wire. A larger difference pushes harder, but the height difference alone does not tell you how much water actually flows. For that we need the second quantity, current.

Current and its direction

Current is the rate at which charge passes a point in a wire, measured in amperes (A), usually shortened to amps. Written as a formula, it is

I = Q ÷ t(1)

where Q is the charge in coulombs that passes the point and t is the time in seconds, so one ampere is one coulomb per second. A coulomb is a large number of electrons, about 6.24 × 1018 of them. Since a small board rarely carries a full amp, most currents in this course are given in milliamps (mA), thousandths of an amp.

In metal wires the charge carriers are electrons, and they move in the opposite direction to the conventional current. Engineers have drawn current in the conventional direction, from the positive terminal to the negative one, for more than a century, and every schematic and simulator follows that convention. You can use it without thinking about the electrons, because the sign of a current gives its direction and the number gives its size.

Current is usually the quantity you care about. A pin that supplies too much current can overheat or fail to hold its output level, and a battery drains in proportion to the current it delivers. The rest of this part, from power in lesson 6 to diodes in lesson 9, is largely about current and how to control it.

Resistance of a component

Resistance is a component's opposition to current, measured in ohms (Ω). A high resistance lets only a little current flow for a given voltage, and a low resistance lets a lot flow. The wire between your Pico and its LED has a small resistance, while a resistor placed in the circuit on purpose has a much larger one.

Resistance comes from the material and the shape of the part. As electrons move through a metal they collide with its atoms, and those collisions turn some of their energy into heat. Copper has a low resistance, which is why wires are made of it, whereas carbon and metal-film resistors use materials chosen for a higher resistance, with their shape and length setting the final value. To use a resistor you need only that value, which is printed on the part or given in its specification.

Resistance is a property of the component, independent of the voltage across it or the current through it. That sounds obvious, but it is what makes Ohm's law useful, because you can know a resistor's value before you connect it and then predict the current as soon as you know the voltage.

Picture water in a hose. Voltage is the pressure difference between the two ends, current is the number of litres passing each second, and resistance is how narrow the hose is, so more pressure pushes more water through and a narrower hose lets less through. The picture breaks down at a cut. A cut hose spills water, whereas a cut wire spills nothing, because the current stops everywhere in the loop at once.

Ohm's law and its three forms

Georg Ohm found, for a range of metal conductors, that the current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it. Doubling the voltage doubles the current, provided the resistance and the temperature stay the same, and the constant that links the two is the resistance:

V = I × R(2)

where V is the voltage across the component in volts, I is the current through it in amperes and R is its resistance in ohms. The equation applies to one component at a time, so V must be the voltage across that same component and I the current through it.

Rearranging equation (2) gives the two other forms, one for each question you might ask:

I = V ÷ R(3)

R = V ÷ I(4)

where the symbols mean the same as in equation (2). Equation (3) answers "what current flows?" when you know the voltage and the resistor, and equation (4) answers "what resistor do I need?" when you know the voltage and the current a device should draw. Doubling the voltage across a fixed resistor doubles the current, and doubling the resistance at the same voltage halves it.

A phone charges from a 5 V charger at 2 A through a long, thin cable whose two wires add up to 0.25 Ω. Equation (2) gives the drop in the cable as 2 A × 0.25 Ω = 0.5 V, so the phone sees only about 4.5 V. A phone that reacts to this lower voltage by reducing its charging current will charge more slowly through such a cable than through a short, thick one, even from the same charger.

Figure 1 shows the smallest circuit where Ohm's law applies, a single source and a single resistor in a loop, and the animation after it changes the voltage and the resistance so you can watch the current follow equation (3). The figure also introduces the symbols this part uses for a battery, a resistor and ground, which the caption explains.

  1. A 5 V source drives current around the loop through 100 Ω. Ohm's law gives I = 5 ÷ 100 = 0.05 A, which is 50 mA. Conventional current leaves the + terminal and returns to −.
  2. Double the resistance to 200 Ω with the same 5 V, and the current halves to 25 mA. More opposition, less flow.
  3. Example A from below: a 3.3 V pin and 330 Ω. I = 3.3 ÷ 330 = 0.01 A = 10 mA, a safe current for a pin and an LED.
  4. Measured from ground, the top wire is at 5 V and the bottom wire at 0 V. The whole 5 V appears across the resistor: that difference is what pushes the current through it.

Units and prefixes

Every term in Ohm's law has to be in base units, volts, amperes and ohms, before you multiply or divide. If a prefix stays in one term and not in the others, the answer comes out wrong by a factor of a thousand, which is one of the most common slips in hardware arithmetic. The prefixes you will meet most often are these.

PrefixSymbolMultiply byExample
kilok1031 kΩ = 1000 Ω
millim10−310 mA = 0.010 A
microµ10−6100 µA = 0.0001 A
megaM1061 MΩ = 1 000 000 Ω

The rule is to convert first and calculate second. Write 330 Ω instead of 0.33 kΩ next to a value in volts, and write 10 mA as 0.010 A before you divide. The calculator in this lesson accepts prefixes, but when you work by hand, keep every number in base units.

Worked examples with a GPIO pin and USB power

The first example uses a Pico GPIO pin at 3.3 V with a 330 Ω resistor from the pin to ground. It is a thought experiment, because a real pin is not a perfect voltage source, but it shows how the numbers fit together. Before you start, guess whether the current will be closer to a microamp, a milliamp or an amp.

  1. Write down the knowns. V = 3.3 V and R = 330 Ω, both already in base units, so nothing needs converting.
  2. Choose the form. We know V and R and want I, so equation (3), I = V ÷ R, is the one to use.
  3. Divide. I = 3.3 ÷ 330 = 0.01 A.
  4. Convert to milliamps. Since a milliamp is a thousandth of an amp, 0.01 A × 1000 = 10 mA.
  5. Check the size. A few volts across a few hundred ohms should give a few to a few tens of milliamps. Ten milliamps falls in that range, so the answer is plausible.

The second example uses USB power. The Pico's micro-USB input is called VBUS, and the Pico datasheet describes it as the micro-USB input voltage, nominally 5 V, or 0 V when the USB cable is not connected. With a 100 Ω resistor across VBUS, equation (3) gives 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 0.050 A, which is 50 mA. Lesson 6 uses this same resistor to work out how much heat it produces.

Note what the second example leaves open. The 5 V is a nominal value and a real USB supply varies, so the real current depends on the real voltage. Ohm's law gives a good estimate, and a measurement settles it.

Ohm's law in C

Writing Ohm's law in C is a useful exercise, because the units problem from the previous sections appears in code as well. The function below takes volts and ohms and returns amps. 

#include <stdio.h>

/* Ohm's law: current in amperes from volts and ohms. */
static float current_from_ohms(float volts, float ohms)
{
    return volts / ohms;        /* I = V / R */
}

int main(void)
{
    float i = current_from_ohms(3.3f, 330.0f);
    printf("%.4f A\n", (double)i);   /* prints 0.0100 A */
    return 0;
}

#include <stdio.h> brings in the standard input and output header, which declares printf. static limits the function to this file, which is the course's rule for file-local helpers. The function takes two float arguments, one for volts and one for ohms, and returns the current as a float. The f suffix on the literals 3.3f and 330.0f makes them float, whereas without it the compiler treats them as double, which is correct but slower on a small chip.

The body is the single expression volts / ohms. Because both arguments are float, this is a floating-point division and the result is 0.01. In main the call computes the current for 3.3 V across 330 Ω and stores it in i, and the format %.4f prints four digits after the point. The cast to double is there because printf expects a floating-point argument in that form, and the output line, 0.0100 A, matches the hand calculation.

There is a cost to this on a microcontroller. The RP2040 on the Pico uses an Arm Cortex-M0+, and the Arm Cortex-M processor comparison table lists the M0+ as having no floating-point unit, so a float division on the RP2040 runs in software and takes many more cycles than an integer divide. Real firmware therefore tends to keep values as integers in small units, such as millivolts and microamps, and divides only where it has to. You will meet that style in the ADC lesson, lesson 58.

Measuring current in CircuitJS

Now we check a calculation against a measurement. Falstad CircuitJS is an in-browser circuit simulator with a built-in voltmeter, ammeter and scope, and Part 1 uses it instead of Wokwi, since Wokwi has only a very basic analog simulation and cannot combine resistors with analog components (see the Wokwi resistor notes). The CircuitJS site is free and GPL-licensed. Its menus change over time, so use the names you see on screen.

  1. Open the built-in example Ohm's law in CircuitJS. The circuit has a 5 V source across two resistors in parallel, 100 Ω on the left and 1 kΩ on the right.
  2. Before you measure anything, work out the current through the 100 Ω resistor by hand: 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 0.05 A, which is 50 mA.
  3. Click the 100 Ω resistor to select it. CircuitJS shows its current on the display, and the reading should be about 50 mA. If it is not, check that you selected the 100 Ω resistor and not the 1 kΩ one.
  4. Double-click the 1 kΩ resistor and change its value to 470 Ω. Predict the new current first, 5 V ÷ 470 Ω, which is about 10.6 mA, then select the resistor and read the current through it. Type the reading into the question below, in amperes or with a prefix such as 10.6m.
Hint

The two resistors are in parallel, so each one sees the full 5 V. The current through either resistor does not depend on the other one. Divide 5 V by its own resistance.

You calculated a current from Ohm's law, checked it against a simulated measurement, and changed a resistor value to predict the new result. That is the basic loop of circuit design.

Common mistakes in Ohm's law calculations

These mistakes are common in the first months of hardware work. Each one shows up in a recognisable way, so you can catch it before it turns into a damaged board.

Mixed prefixes in one calculation

You write 3.3 V ÷ 0.33 kΩ, get 10, and then wonder why the simulator shows 10 mA. The answer is off by exactly a factor of a thousand, because the k stayed in one term while the other terms were in base units. The fix is to convert every term first, so 0.33 kΩ becomes 330 Ω before you divide.

Multiplication in place of division

You compute R = V × I for 5 V and 25 mA, get 0.125, and read it as 125 Ω. The resistance comes out far too small or far too large for the part, and the units do not come out in ohms, because volts times amps are watts. The fix is to check the units of each equation before trusting the number.

Integer division in C

You declare int variables for volts and ohms and write volts / ohms. For 3 V and 330 Ω the result is 0 instead of 0.0091, because C truncates the fraction, so the current reads as zero or jumps between zero and a whole number as the input changes. The fix is to make at least one operand a float, or to scale to integers on purpose.

An ammeter connected across a supply

A bench meter blows its fuse or gives a loud warning when you try to measure a supply's current. A current is measured by breaking the wire and placing the meter in series, so the current flows through it, and an ammeter has almost no resistance, so across a supply it lets a huge current flow. The fix is to measure voltage across a part and current through it, which are two different connections.

Ohm's law describes a resistor. A GPIO pin, an LED or a motor is not a plain resistor, so the equation can only give the current through the component you have modelled as a resistor. Lesson 9 covers the diode, which breaks this proportionality on purpose, and lesson 11 covers the transistor.

Current checks in real projects

Engineers do this calculation before they buy a part. A hardware engineer reads the voltage rail from the schematic, the resistance from the bill of materials and the current limit from the component's datasheet, then checks that the numbers agree. Ohm's law is the first check in that process, and a design review typically asks for it directly: what current does this resistor carry, and from which rail? A review that cannot answer that question is not finished.

In firmware the same habit shows up in naming. Good embedded code puts the unit in the variable name, so a reviewer can see a mistake without running anything. A line such as int32_t current_ua = adc_to_microamps(raw); says that the value is in microamps, whereas a line that says only current = ... invites the thousand-fold error described above, because the reader cannot tell milliamps from amps.

Summary

  • Voltage is a difference between two points, in volts, and it is always quoted against a reference such as ground.
  • Current is the flow of charge, in amperes, and it sets how hard a part works and how fast a battery drains.
  • Resistance is a part's opposition to current, in ohms, and it is a property of the part.
  • Ohm's law is V = I × R. Rearranged, I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I.
  • Convert every term to base units before calculating. A missing or extra prefix is a factor of a thousand.
  • Measure voltage across a part and current through it. An ammeter goes in series, and a voltmeter goes across the part.
  • Ohm's law holds for a resistor. Diodes and transistors need the next lessons.
