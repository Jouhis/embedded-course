Voltage, current, resistance, and Ohm's law

Three quantities describe every resistor in a phone charger and on your Pico. Ohm's law links them, and you will use it in every lesson of this part.

By the end of this lesson you can work out the current through a resistor from its voltage and resistance, and confirm the value by measuring it in CircuitJS.

Where we are and why it matters

The first four lessons were about software and the way this course teaches. You have built a project, lit an LED from a GPIO pin in lesson 2, and planned your study time in lesson 4. In the blink, you wrote one line of C that set a pin high, and the LED lit. You did not ask what that pin was doing to the LED, the wires and the board around it. This lesson starts that question, and it is the basis for the electrical lessons that follow in this part.

The problem is simple to state. A GPIO pin can supply only a small current, and an LED or a sensor must not draw more than the pin can give. A heater, a fan or a stepper driver on a 3D printer draws far more, and the wrong part can burn out a board. Before you can choose a part, you must be able to say how much voltage is across it and how much current flows through it. Three quantities, voltage, current and resistance, give you those answers, and one equation connects them.

Voltage is a difference

Voltage is the difference in electric potential between two points, measured in volts (V). The word potential is a name for the energy per unit of charge at a point, so a voltage is the energy each unit of charge gains or loses when it moves between two points. You do not need the full definition to work with voltages. You need one habit: a voltage is always measured between two points, never at one point alone.

That habit matters because every voltage in a circuit needs a reference. The reference is usually ground (GND), which is defined as 0 V. When a data sheet says a pin is at 3.3 V, it means 3.3 V above ground. The same is true of a battery: a "9 V battery" means 9 V between its two terminals, and whether one terminal is at 0 V depends on how you connect it.

A useful picture is height. Water runs downhill because one place is higher than another. Voltage is the height difference that pushes charge along a wire. A bigger height difference pushes harder, but the height difference alone does not say how much water actually flows. For that you need the current.

Current is a flow

Current is the rate at which charge passes a point, measured in amperes (A), or amps. One ampere is one coulomb of charge passing a point every second. A coulomb is a large number of electrons, about 6.24 × 1018 of them. You will rarely handle a full amp on a small board, so most of the numbers in this course are milliamps (mA), which are thousandths of an amp.

Electrons are the charge carriers in metal wires, and they move in the opposite direction to the conventional current. Engineers have used the conventional direction, from the positive terminal to the negative one, for more than a century, and every schematic and simulator follows it. You can follow the convention without worrying about what electrons do. The sign tells you the direction, and the number tells you the size.

Current is the quantity you usually care about in a circuit. A pin that supplies too much current can overheat or lose its output level. A battery drains in proportion to the current it supplies. The rest of this part of the course, from power in lesson 6 to diodes in lesson 9, is about current and how to control it.

Resistance opposes the flow

Resistance is a component's opposition to current, measured in ohms (Ω). A component with a high resistance lets only a little current flow for a given voltage. A component with a low resistance lets a lot flow. The wire that connects your Pico to its LED has a small resistance, and a resistor placed on purpose has a much larger one.

Resistance comes from how the material and shape of a part resist the motion of charge. Electrons collide with the atoms in the metal as they move, and those collisions turn some of the energy into heat. Copper has a low resistance, which is why it is used for wires. Carbon and metal-film resistors are made from materials chosen for a higher resistance, and their shape and length set the final value. To use a resistor, you need only its value, which is printed on the part or in its specification.

Resistance is a property of the component, not of the voltage or the current. This sounds obvious, but it is the reason Ohm's law is useful: you can know a resistor's value before you connect it, and then predict the current once you know the voltage.

Ohm's law ties the three together

Georg Ohm found, for a range of metal conductors, that the current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it. Doubling the voltage doubles the current, as long as the resistance and the temperature stay the same. The proportionality constant is the resistance, and the relationship is written:

V = I × R

Rearranged, the equation gives the current, I = V ÷ R, and the resistance, R = V ÷ I. Each form answers a different question. Use I = V ÷ R when you know the voltage and the resistor and want the current. Use R = V ÷ I when you know the voltage and the current a device needs and want the resistance that gives it. Double the voltage across a fixed resistor and the current doubles. Double the resistance at the same voltage and the current halves.

Units and prefixes

Every term in Ohm's law must be in base units before you multiply or divide: volts, amperes and ohms. A common mistake is to mix a prefix into one term and not the others, and that gives an answer off by a factor of a thousand. The prefixes you will meet most often are these.

PrefixSymbolMultiply byExample
kilok1031 kΩ = 1000 Ω
millim10−310 mA = 0.010 A
microµ10−6100 µA = 0.0001 A
megaM1061 MΩ = 1 000 000 Ω

The rule is to convert first and calculate second. Write 330 Ω, not 0.33 kΩ mixed with volts, and write 10 mA as 0.010 A before you divide. The calculator in this lesson accepts prefixes, but when you work by hand, keep every number in base units.

Worked example, step by step

Example A: a GPIO pin feeding a resistor. Imagine a Pico GPIO pin at 3.3 V, with a 330 Ω resistor from the pin to ground. This is a thought experiment, because a real pin is not a perfect voltage source, but it shows how the numbers fit together. Work through it one step at a time.

  1. Write down the knowns. V = 3.3 V, and R = 330 Ω. Both are in base units already.
  2. Choose the form. You know V and R and want I, so use I = V ÷ R.
  3. Divide. I = 3.3 ÷ 330 = 0.01 A.
  4. Convert to milliamps. 0.01 A × 1000 = 10 mA. A milliamp is a thousandth of an amp, so the current is 10 mA.
  5. Check the size. A resistor of a few hundred ohms on a few volts should pass a few to a few tens of milliamps. Ten milliamps fits that range, so the answer is plausible.

Example B: USB power. The Pico's micro-USB input is called VBUS, and it is nominally 5 V. The Pico datasheet describes VBUS as the micro-USB input voltage, nominally 5 V, and it is 0 V when the USB cable is not connected. Put a 100 Ω resistor across VBUS. The current is 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 0.050 A, which is 50 mA. Lesson 6 uses this same resistor to work out how much heat it makes.

Notice what Example B does not tell you. The 5 V is a nominal value, so the real current depends on the real voltage, and a real USB supply varies. Ohm's law gives a good estimate, and a measurement settles it.

The code: Ohm's law in C

Ohm's law is easy to write in C, and it is a useful exercise because it shows the units problem in code. The function below takes volts and ohms and returns amps. 

#include <stdio.h>

/* Ohm's law: current in amperes from volts and ohms. */
static float current_from_ohms(float volts, float ohms)
{
    return volts / ohms;        /* I = V / R */
}

int main(void)
{
    float i = current_from_ohms(3.3f, 330.0f);
    printf("%.4f A\n", (double)i);   /* prints 0.0100 A */
    return 0;
}

#include <stdio.h> brings in the standard input and output header, which declares printf. static limits the function to this file, which is the course's rule for file-local helpers. The function takes two float arguments, one for volts and one for ohms, and returns the current as a float. The f suffix on the literals 3.3f and 330.0f makes them float. Without the suffix, the compiler treats them as double, which is correct but slower on a small chip.

The body is one line: volts / ohms. Because both arguments are float, the division is a floating-point division, and the result is 0.01. In main, the call computes the current for 3.3 V across 330 Ω and stores it in i. The printf format %.4f prints four digits after the point, and the cast to double is the form printf expects for a floating-point argument. The output line is 0.0100 A, which matches the hand calculation.

A note for firmware. The RP2040 on the Pico uses an Arm Cortex-M0+, and the Arm Cortex-M processor comparison table lists the M0+ as having no floating-point unit. A float division on the RP2040 is done in software, which takes many more cycles than an integer divide. Real firmware usually keeps values in integers, for example millivolts and microamps, and does the division only where it matters. You will meet that style in the ADC lesson, lesson 58.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

These mistakes are common in the first months of hardware work. Each one shows up in a recognisable way, so you can catch it before it becomes a damaged board.

  1. Mixing prefixes. You write 3.3 V ÷ 0.33 kΩ and get an answer of 10, and then wonder why the simulator shows 10 mA. The symptom is an answer off by exactly a factor of a thousand. The fix is to convert every term to base units first, so 0.33 kΩ becomes 330 Ω before you divide.
  2. Multiplying where you should divide. You compute R = V × I for 5 V and 25 mA and get 0.125, which you then read as 125 Ω. The symptom is a resistance that is far too small or far too large for the part, and units that do not come out to ohms. The fix is to check the units of each equation, because volts times amps are watts, not ohms.
  3. Integer division in C. You declare int variables for volts and ohms and write volts / ohms. For 3 V and 330 Ω the result is 0, not 0.0091, because C truncates the fraction. The symptom is a current that reads as zero, or a value that jumps between zero and a whole number as you change the input. The fix is to make at least one operand a float or to scale to integers on purpose.
  4. Putting an ammeter across a supply. A current is measured by breaking the wire and placing the meter in series, so the current flows through the meter. If you place an ammeter across the supply, it has almost no resistance, and the current becomes huge. The symptom on a bench meter is a blown fuse or a loud warning. The fix is to measure voltage across a part and current through it, which are two different connections.
Ohm's law describes a resistor. A GPIO pin, an LED or a motor is not a plain resistor, so the equation can only give the current through the component you have modelled as a resistor. Lesson 9 covers the diode, which breaks this proportionality on purpose, and lesson 11 covers the transistor.

In real projects

Engineers do this calculation before they buy a part. A hardware engineer reads the voltage rail from the schematic, the resistance from the bill of materials and the current limit from the datasheet of the component, then checks that the numbers fit. Ohm's law is the first check in that process, and a senior engineer will ask for it in a design review: "What current does this resistor carry, and what is the rail?" If the answer is not written in the review notes, the review stops.

In firmware, the same habit shows up in naming. Good embedded code puts the unit in the variable name, so a reviewer can see the mistake without running anything. A line such as int32_t current_ua = adc_to_microamps(raw); says that the value is in microamps. A line that says only current = ... invites exactly the thousand-fold error described above, because the reader cannot tell milliamps from amps.

Summary

  • Voltage is a difference between two points, in volts, and it is always quoted against a reference such as ground.
  • Current is the flow of charge, in amperes, and it sets how hard a part works and how fast a battery drains.
  • Resistance is a part's opposition to current, in ohms, and it is a property of the part.
  • Ohm's law is V = I × R. Rearranged, I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I.
  • Convert every term to base units before calculating. A missing or extra prefix is a factor of a thousand.
  • Measure voltage across a part and current through it. An ammeter goes in series; a voltmeter goes across the part.
  • Ohm's law holds for a resistor. Diodes and transistors need the next lessons.

Try it in CircuitJS

Falstad CircuitJS is an in-browser circuit simulator with a built-in voltmeter, ammeter and scope. Part 1 uses it instead of Wokwi, because Wokwi is a digital simulator and cannot model the analogue behaviour of resistors and sources (see the Wokwi resistor notes). The CircuitJS site is free and GPL-licensed. Its menus change over time, so use the names you see on screen.

  1. Open the built-in example Ohm's law in CircuitJS. The circuit has a 5 V source across two resistors in parallel: 100 Ω on the left and 1 kΩ on the right.
  2. Before you measure anything, work out the current through the 100 Ω resistor by hand: 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 0.05 A, which is 50 mA.
  3. Click the 100 Ω resistor to select it. CircuitJS shows its current on the display, and that reading should be about 50 mA. If it is not, check that you selected the 100 Ω resistor and not the 1 kΩ one.
  4. Double-click the 1 kΩ resistor, and change its value to 470 Ω. Then select it and read the current through it. Type that value into the question below, in amperes or with a prefix such as 10.6m. Before you type, check that the reading is about 10.6 mA, which is 5 V ÷ 470 Ω.
Hint

The two resistors are in parallel, so each one sees the full 5 V. The current through either resistor does not depend on the other one. Divide 5 V by its own resistance.

You calculated a current from Ohm's law, checked it against a simulated measurement, and changed a resistor value to predict the new result. That is the basic loop of circuit design.
