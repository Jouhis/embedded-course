Low-power modes

Sleep modes, wake-up sources and battery budgets

By the end of this lesson you can say what WFI, WFE, Sleep, Stop and Standby do on the STM32F1, name the wake-up sources you would use, and calculate a battery's life from its capacity and a duty-cycled average current.

A CPU with nothing to do

Lesson 72 showed how an interrupt reaches the CPU, a peripheral raising a request, the NVIC checking its priority and the core taking the exception, lesson 70 used EXTI lines to turn a pin change into an interrupt, and lesson 73 woke the loop every millisecond with SysTick. All of them assumed a running CPU, and this lesson asks the next question, what the CPU should do when there is nothing to do.

The answer matters most in battery products. A soil-moisture probe, a door sensor or a car key fob measures something every few minutes, sends a short message and sleeps, so a chip that stays awake empties the battery in days, whereas one that sleeps deeply and wakes only for work can run a year from a coin cell, the difference coming from decisions about sleep modes, wake-up sources and the timing of each awake period.

Supply current and why sleep is not free

A chip draws current from its supply, measured in amperes (A), a milliamp (mA) being one thousandth of an ampere and a microamp (µA) one millionth. A running Cortex-M3 may draw a few milliamps, depending on the clock speed and the peripherals switched on, while a chip in its deepest sleep may draw only microamps, and the ratio between the two is the reason low-power design exists. A device asleep 99 per cent of the time is dominated by its sleep current, and one that wakes for long periods by its awake current.

The current does not come only from the core, because every peripheral with its clock enabled uses some current even when idle, a timer with no work still toggling its counter, and a GPIO pin driving a load, or a pull-up pulling against a pin held low, draws current through the pin. A sleeping chip can therefore draw far more than its datasheet sleep figure because the firmware left something on, and the clock gating whose clock-enable pattern lesson 69 showed is the same mechanism low-power design uses to switch parts off (RM0008 Rev 21, section 5.3.2).

The WFI and WFE sleep instructions

A Cortex-M core sleeps when it executes one of two instructions, WFI, wait for interrupt, which stops the core until an interrupt is pending, or WFE, wait for event, which stops it until an event arrives (PM0056 Rev 7, section 2.5.1), an event being a wake-up signal that need not be an interrupt, though every interrupt is also an event. The CMSIS header cmsis_gcc.h defines these as the intrinsics __WFI() and __WFE(), together with __SEV(), which sets an event from software so that a waiting WFE wakes.

While asleep the core fetches no instructions, its clock being gated so that it stops switching while only the wake-up logic stays powered, and when the wake-up condition arrives it resumes at the instruction after WFI, which is why a sleep in the middle of a loop works, the loop pausing at the instruction and continuing from the same place. Waking is not instant. The clocks have to restart, and a regulator may need time to come up. A deeper sleep usually takes longer to wake from, so measure the wake-up time on your board before you set a deadline on it.

Two similar-looking mechanisms are often confused: a pending interrupt wakes WFI only if the interrupt is enabled in the NVIC (lesson 72; RM0008 Rev 21, section 5.3.3), while an event for WFE may be a peripheral event that never enters an interrupt handler. A typical sensor loop uses WFI, because its wake-up is an interrupt that runs a handler.

The SCB system control register

One core register changes what WFI and WFE do, the System Control Block register SCB->SCR, whose three relevant bits are named in core_cm3.h.

  • SLEEPDEEP (SCB_SCR_SLEEPDEEP_Msk) makes the chip enter its deep sleep, Stop or Standby, instead of plain Sleep when the core sleeps (PM0056 Rev 7, section 4.4.6).
  • SLEEPONEXIT (SCB_SCR_SLEEPONEXIT_Msk) sends the core back to sleep as soon as the interrupt handlers return to Thread mode, with no main-loop code in between (PM0056 Rev 7, sections 2.5.1 and 4.4.6), which suits a device that does nothing but react to interrupts.
  • SEVONPEND (SCB_SCR_SEVONPEND_Msk) makes a newly pending interrupt wake WFE even if the interrupt is disabled (PM0056 Rev 7, section 4.4.6), a bit to set only with a clear reason.

SLEEPDEEP does not sleep by itself, only choosing the depth of the sleep that WFI or WFE will start, and the sleep begins when the instruction runs.

Sleep, Stop and Standby on the STM32F1

The STM32F1 has three levels of sleep, described in the Power control chapter of RM0008 (Rev 21, section 5.3 and Table 11), trading wake-up speed for power, so that the deeper the level, the lower the current and the more state is lost.

The three modes resemble three ways of stopping work. Sleep is a nap at the desk, from which you wake at once while the chair and the light stay in use, Stop is going home for the night, the office dark but the papers left on the desk, so returning takes a little longer, and Standby is moving out, which uses almost nothing but clears the desk, so work starts again from the beginning, from reset. The comparison breaks for Standby's backup registers, though, a few words of state that survive like a note left with the landlord.

  • Sleep. The CPU clock stops while the rest of the chip keeps running, so peripherals with their clocks on still work and their interrupts wake the core, which makes Sleep the shallowest and easiest mode, everything keeping its state (RM0008 Rev 21, section 5.3.3).
  • Stop. The main clocks stop while the SRAM and the registers keep their contents, and the core resumes where it stopped (RM0008 Rev 21, section 5.3.4), but only some wake-up sources work, because most peripheral clocks are off, an EXTI line still working because the EXTI logic runs on a clock that stays on.
  • Standby. At this deepest level the SRAM contents are lost, and the chip wakes through reset, so the program starts again at the reset handler with every SRAM variable gone (RM0008 Rev 21, section 5.3.5).

The bits selecting between these modes lie in the PWR (power control) peripheral rather than the core. The CMSIS header defines PWR_CR_PDDS, which selects Standby instead of Stop when the core enters deep sleep, and PWR_CR_LPDS, which puts the voltage regulator into its low-power state during Stop, the header comment marking PWR_CR_LPDS as the low-power regulator bit rather than the Stop-or-Standby selector, so their descriptions in RM0008 Rev 21, section 5.4.1, are worth reading before use. PWR_CSR_WUF reports that a wake-up happened and PWR_CR_CWUF clears it (RM0008 Rev 21, sections 5.4.1 and 5.4.2) RM0008 Rev 21, section 5.3, Power control (PWR), and its low-power mode table (Table 11).

Standby loses the SRAM, so anything you need after the wake-up must be in backup registers or flash. It also restarts the chip from reset, so the wake-up path is the reset path. Design the state you keep before you choose Standby.

Wake-up sources for each mode

Each mode accepts only some wake-up sources, because of clock gating, a wake-up source having to keep working while the mode is active. In Stop most peripheral clocks are off, so a timer counting on a stopped clock cannot raise its interrupt, whereas an EXTI line detects a pin change without a running peripheral clock (lesson 70), so EXTI wakes Stop while an ADC or timer interrupt does not (RM0008 Rev 21, section 5.3.4).

Standby has fewer sources, the usual ones being the WKUP pin, enabled by the PWR bit PWR_CSR_EWUP, and a real-time clock alarm (RM0008 Rev 21, sections 5.3.5 and 5.4.2), the RTC counting on its own low-power clock and so serving as the standard timed wake-up from Standby (RM0008 Rev 21, section 5.3.6). The RTC is not simulated in Wokwi, so its wake-up path is checked on hardware, and the sources for each mode are listed in RM0008 Rev 21, Table 14 (Stop) and Table 15 (Standby).

A quick way to choose is to list the events the device must react to and find the deepest mode that still has a source for each. A button in a sensor that must answer at once needs an EXTI source, so Stop is the deepest option, while a sensor measuring every ten minutes needs the RTC, so Standby is possible if it can afford to lose RAM and restart from reset.

Worked example: the battery budget of a duty-cycled sensor

Suppose a sensor wakes once per second, works for 50 ms and sleeps for the remaining 950 ms, drawing an example current of 10 mA awake and 0.005 mA (5 µA) asleep, practice values rather than device figures, and the question is its battery life from a 2 000 mAh cell. The average current of a duty-cycled device is the time-weighted mean of its states

Iavg = d × Ia + (1 − d) × Is(1)

where d is the duty cycle, the awake time divided by the period, Ia the awake current and Is the sleep current, here d = 50 ms ÷ 1 000 ms = 0.05, and the battery life is

tlife = C ÷ Iavg(2)

where C is the capacity in milliampere-hours. Before reading the calculation, guess which term of equation (1) dominates. 

d        = 50 ms ÷ 1000 ms                 = 0.05
I_avg    = d × I_a + (1 − d) × I_s
         = 0.05 × 10 mA + 0.95 × 0.005 mA
         = 0.500 mA + 0.00475 mA
         = 0.50475 mA
life (h) = 2000 mAh ÷ 0.50475 mA          = 3962.4 h
life (d) = 3962.4 h ÷ 24                  = 165.1 days

The first line is the duty cycle, the fraction of each second the chip is awake, and the second the formula, while the third multiplies each state by its share of time, the awake term coming out about a hundred times larger than the sleep term although the sleep term spans almost the whole second, for a total of 0.50475 mA. The last two lines convert the battery capacity into hours and then days.

The sleep term contributes only 0.00475 mA against the awake term's 0.5 mA, so a designer seeking a longer life should first shorten the 50 ms: cutting the awake time to 10 ms makes d = 0.01 and the average about 0.105 mA, more than quadrupling the battery life, whereas reducing the sleep current from 5 µA to 2 µA would help far less. This is why the course advises measuring the awake time first.

The formula assumes an ideal battery. Real cells lose capacity at high current, self-discharge, and fall below the regulator's dropout (lesson 15). Common mistakes and how to spot them

Low-power firmware fails in predictable ways, and in each case the chip still works, which is why the mistake survives until someone measures the current.

A peripheral left on

The sleep current measured with a meter is tens of microamps or more instead of the few microamps of the datasheet and does not change when the firmware sleeps, because a developer enabled the USART clock for debugging and forgot to disable it. Before each sleep, turn off the clock of every peripheral not needed or power the peripheral down in its own register, then measure again and compare with the expected figure.

Sleeping with an interrupt already pending

The average current stays close to the active current and a logic analyser shows the core waking every few microseconds, because an enabled interrupt already pending when WFI runs wakes the core at once, so the loop spins between sleep and wake without doing its work. Clear the peripheral's flag in the handler so that nothing stays pending, and check the flag before sleeping.

Standby chosen without designing for it

A counter restarts from zero after every wake-up, the timing drifts and the device sends duplicate messages, because the developer chose Standby for its low current but kept the counter in ordinary RAM. The bug appears only after the first real sleep, so bench tests with a debugger attached often miss it, the debugger holding the chip awake. Keep state that must survive a sleep in backup registers or in flash, and treat every wake-up as a reset.

A timer expected to wake Stop

The chip sleeps and never wakes, or wakes only when a button is pressed, because a timer interrupt was set up before entering Stop, where the timer's clock stops. Use a wake-up source that works in that mode, EXTI for Stop, and for timed wake-ups the RTC or another source that keeps its clock running (RM0008 Rev 21, sections 5.3.4 and 5.3.6).

Power budgets and sleep design in products

Battery products start from the power budget: a product manager asks for a battery life of one year, and the firmware engineer turns that into a current budget, then into a duty cycle and a wake-up plan, which is measured on real hardware with a power analyser before every release. The datasheet figure is a typical or maximum value for one chip, while a real board adds regulators, pull-ups and leakage that the datasheet does not count.

A common production pattern is a super-loop built around WFI, the main loop doing its work and then sleeping while each interrupt does a small job, sets a flag and returns, and in more complex devices an RTOS keeps its idle task in a sleep instruction, so that the chip sleeps whenever no task is ready. Reviewers ask of any sleep design which wake-up source serves each mode and what state survives the wake-up, and a design that answers both has been thought through as a whole system.

Summary

  • A Cortex-M core sleeps when it executes WFI (wait for interrupt) or WFE (wait for event) and wakes when the wake-up condition arrives.
  • The SCB->SCR bits SLEEPDEEP and SLEEPONEXIT change how the sleep behaves without sleeping by themselves.
  • Sleep keeps every peripheral running, Stop keeps SRAM and wakes on EXTI, and Standby loses SRAM and restarts from reset.
  • A wake-up source must keep its clock running in the chosen mode, so a timer does not wake Stop while an RTC or an EXTI line does (RM0008 Rev 21, Table 14).
  • The average current is d × Ia + (1 − d) × Is, and the battery life is the capacity divided by that average.
  • Shortening the awake time usually saves more than a small change in the sleep current.
  • The real sleep current must be measured, because a stray peripheral clock can cost more than the whole sleep budget.
