Capacitors: decoupling, time constants and filters

Every chip on your board has a small capacitor next to its power pins. This lesson shows why, and how a resistor and a capacitor set a time.

By the end of this lesson you can calculate an RC time constant, predict how long a capacitor takes to settle, and say why a 100 nF capacitor sits next to every chip's supply pin.

Where we are and why it matters

Lessons 5 to 9 gave you resistors, and lesson 9 used a resistor to set a current. Those lessons assumed steady, unchanging values. Lesson 11 and lesson 12 switched loads on and off, and the switch-off transient did not appear in any of those calculations. A capacitor explains much of what happens during a change. It holds charge, so the voltage across it cannot jump, and that one fact explains why a board needs capacitors next to its chips, why a reset line needs a delay, and why a filter can clean up a noisy signal.

Problems show up as soon as a board runs. A microcontroller resets when a motor starts. A sensor reads noise when the nearby relay clicks. A button bounces, and the firmware sees five presses where you pressed once. Each of those has a capacitor either as the cure or as the cause. In this lesson you will learn what the capacitor stores, how a resistor and a capacitor together set a time, and how that time becomes a filter. Lesson 14 will then treat the inductor, which is the capacitor's counterpart for current.

The idea

A capacitor stores charge. The voltage across it cannot change instantly, because changing it means moving charge through the resistance in the circuit. Its behaviour is set by the capacitance, C, in farads (F). A useful size for microcontroller work is the microfarad (µF) or the nanofarad (nF), where 1 µF = 1000 nF. The farad is a large unit. A typical decoupling capacitor is 100 nF, which is 0.0000001 F.

How a capacitor stores charge

A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator, called the dielectric. When a voltage is applied, charge moves onto one plate and off the other. The plates hold equal and opposite charges, and no current flows through the insulator itself. The charge Q on a capacitor is proportional to the voltage across it, with the constant of proportionality being the capacitance: Q = C × V. A bigger capacitance stores more charge for the same voltage.

Current is the rate at which charge moves. Combine that with the charge formula and you get the rule that matters for circuits: i = C × dV/dt. The current into a capacitor equals its capacitance times how fast its voltage changes. Read it the other way and it explains the core behaviour. To change the voltage across a capacitor quickly, you need a large current, and a current that is limited by a resistor cannot change the voltage instantly. The voltage on a capacitor is therefore continuous: it can rise or fall, but it cannot jump in zero time.

A charged capacitor also stores energy, E = ½ × C × V². The energy is what a decoupling capacitor delivers to a chip during a burst, and what a bulk capacitor delivers during a motor start. The energy in a 100 nF capacitor at 3.3 V is only ½ × 100 × 10−9 × 3.3² = 0.54 µJ, which shows that a single small decoupling capacitor serves the local supply during short bursts. It cannot power a board.

Capacitance is measured in farads, F, and real capacitors are sized in microfarads (µF, 10−6 F), nanofarads (nF, 10−9 F) and picofarads (pF, 10−12 F). A farad is a very large unit, which is why almost every capacitor on a board is a fraction of one.

Decoupling: the capacitor next to each chip

A chip's supply pins draw current in short bursts whenever its logic switches. The supply wire has some inductance and resistance, so the voltage at the chip dips each time. A capacitor placed close to the supply pin supplies that burst locally, so the rail stays steady. The RP2040 datasheet asks for a 100 nF capacitor close to each IOVDD pin (RP2040 datasheet, section 2.9.1). "Close" matters more than the value. A capacitor at the far end of a wire does little, because the wire's inductance is in the way. The same reasoning is why the capacitor's leads should be short.

Picture a tall building fed by one long pipe from the street. If everyone on a floor turns on a tap at the same moment, the pressure on that floor drops, because the long pipe cannot speed up its flow at once. A small tank on each floor fixes it: the tank covers the burst, and the pipe refills it slowly afterwards. The decoupling capacitor is that tank, and it only helps if it sits on the same floor, right next to the chip.

Worked example: why placement matters

Use illustrative numbers, not measured ones, to see the two effects. Suppose a chip draws a 20 mA burst for 5 ns each time its logic switches, and the supply wire between the regulator and the chip has an inductance of 10 nH. Both numbers are chosen to show the arithmetic, and a real board will differ.

  1. The capacitor supplies the burst. If a 100 nF capacitor sits right at the pin, it supplies the charge for the burst. The voltage it loses is ΔV = I × Δt ÷ C = 20 mA × 5 ns ÷ 100 nF = 0.02 A × 5 × 10−9 s ÷ 10−7 F = 1 mV. A millivolt is harmless.
  2. The wire supplies the burst. If the capacitor is far away, the burst comes down the wire instead. The wire's inductance opposes the change in current, and the voltage it drops is V = L × di/dt = 10 nH × 20 mA ÷ 5 ns = 10 × 10−9 H × 0.02 A ÷ 5 × 10−9 s = 40 mV. That is forty times larger, and it is the dip the chip sees.
  3. What to take from it. The capacitor's value helped, but the distance mattered more. A short, direct connection from the capacitor to the supply pin lowers the inductance, and that is why the datasheet asks for the capacitor to be close.

The same logic says that a decoupling capacitor cannot supply a long burst. A 100 nF capacitor can cover a few nanoseconds of switching, but a motor that draws amps for milliseconds needs a much larger bulk capacitor, nearer the motor, and a supply that can refill it. Lesson 14 explains why a motor is the classic source of that kind of burst.

  1. A regulator feeds a chip through a long wire, and a 100 nF capacitor sits right at the chip's supply pin. The numbers here are illustrative, as in the worked example.
  2. The chip's logic switches and draws a burst: 20 mA for 5 ns.
  3. The capacitor at the pin supplies the burst from its own charge. It loses ΔV = 20 mA × 5 ns ÷ 100 nF = 1 mV, which is harmless.
  4. Between bursts, the regulator refills the capacitor through the wire, slowly. The wire never has to change its current quickly.
  5. Remove the capacitor and the burst must come down the wire. Its 10 nH opposes the fast change: 10 nH × 20 mA ÷ 5 ns = 40 mV, forty times the dip. Distance mattered more than value.

The RC time constant

Put a resistor R in series with a capacitor C and apply a step voltage. The capacitor charges exponentially, and the time constant is τ = R × C seconds. After one τ the capacitor has reached 63.2 % of the step. After 5τ it is at 99.3 %, which is close enough to call it settled. These values come from the exponential 1 − e−t/τ (All About Circuits textbook, RC circuits).

Worked numbers: a 100 Ω resistor with a 200 µF capacitor has τ = 100 × 200×10−6 = 0.02 s, or 20 ms. It settles in about 5 × 20 ms = 100 ms. A 10 kΩ resistor with the same capacitor gives 2 s, so the resistor you pick sets the speed directly.

Be careful with units. Use ohms and farads in the formula. A value written as 200 (with no unit) is a guess, and the error is a factor of a million.

Worked example: the charging curve, value by value

Use the same circuit as the figure: a 5 V step, a 100 Ω resistor and a 200 µF capacitor that starts at 0 V. The time constant is τ = 100 Ω × 200 µF = 20 ms. The charging equation is V(t) = 5 V × (1 − e−t/τ). Build it up one time constant at a time, so you can check each value by hand.

  1. At t = 0. The capacitor has no charge, so V = 0 V. The current is the step divided by the resistor, I = 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 50 mA. Current is greatest at the start and falls as the capacitor charges, because the voltage across the resistor shrinks.
  2. At t = 20 ms, one τ. t/τ = 1, so V = 5 × (1 − e−1) = 5 × 0.632 = 3.16 V. The current has fallen to (5 − 3.16) ÷ 100 Ω = 18.4 mA.
  3. At t = 40 ms, two τ. t/τ = 2, so V = 5 × (1 − e−2) = 5 × 0.865 = 4.32 V. Each further τ closes about two thirds of the remaining gap, which is why the curve looks like a slowing approach.
  4. At t = 100 ms, five τ. t/τ = 5, so V = 5 × (1 − e−5) = 5 × 0.9933 = 4.97 V. The capacitor is settled to within about 0.7 % of the step, and the current is down to about 0.3 mA, which is 50 mA × e−5.

The useful habit is the ratio t ÷ τ. Once you know that, every value on the curve follows from a table of exponentials, and a settling time is a number of time constants rather than a new calculation. The next time a board takes a while to come up, count the time constants.

  1. At the step, the empty capacitor has 0 V across it, so the whole 5 V is across the resistor and the current is at its largest, 50 mA.
  2. After one time constant, 20 ms, the capacitor has reached 63 % of the step: 3.16 V. The resistor has only 1.84 V left across it, so the current has fallen to 18.4 mA.
  3. After 2τ it is at 86.5 %, 4.32 V. Each further τ closes about 63 % of the gap that is left.
  4. After 5τ, 100 ms, it is at 4.97 V and the current has almost stopped. Engineers call it settled.

Filters: a resistor and capacitor together

The same pair works as a low-pass filter. Signals with a frequency well below fc = 1 / (2π × R × C) pass to the output almost unchanged, and faster signals are attenuated. At fc the output is 70.7 % of the input. Decoupling is the same idea: the capacitor shunts fast noise on the supply to ground.

Worked example: a filter's cut-off and its attenuation

Take a 187 Ω resistor and a 10 µF capacitor, connected as a low-pass filter with the signal going in across the resistor and the output taken across the capacitor. The cut-off frequency is the point where the output has fallen to 70.7 % of the input, and it is fc = 1 ÷ (2π × R × C).

  1. Cut-off. 2π × 187 Ω × 10 × 10−6 F = 0.01175 s, so fc = 1 ÷ 0.01175 = 85.1 Hz.
  2. Attenuation at the cut-off. The gain of a first-order low-pass filter is |H| = 1 ÷ √(1 + (f ÷ fc)²). At f = fc, that is 1 ÷ √2 = 0.707, or 70.7 %.
  3. Attenuation at ten times the cut-off. At f = 851 Hz, the gain is 1 ÷ √(1 + 100) = 1 ÷ 10.05 = 0.0995, or about 10 %. A signal ten times faster than the cut-off is reduced to about a tenth. That is the practical meaning of a low-pass filter.
  4. Check the units. The answer is in hertz only if R is in ohms and C is in farads. Writing 10 for the capacitance, instead of 10 µF, moves the cut-off by a factor of a million.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

Mistake 1: the capacitor value written without its unit

The symptom is a time constant that is off by a factor of a million. You predict 20 ms and measure 20 seconds, or a filter whose cut-off is far below anything you designed. The cause is writing 200 for 200 µF, and the fix is to write every value with its prefix and convert to base units before you multiply. Use the formula with ohms and farads, and then convert the answer.

Mistake 2: a decoupling capacitor placed far from its pin

The symptom is a board that resets when a motor or a relay switches, even though the board has a 100 nF capacitor on it. The capacitor is there, but the wire between it and the pin is long, so the burst is supplied through the wire's inductance and the voltage dips. You can see the dip on a scope probing the chip's supply pin during the event. The fix is to move the capacitor to the pin, with the shortest possible connection to the supply and the ground plane, which is what the RP2040 hardware guide asks for.

Mistake 3: an electrolytic capacitor the wrong way round

The symptom is a capacitor that gets hot, swells or vents, usually soon after power-up. Electrolytic and tantalum capacitors are polarised: they must have the positive terminal at the higher voltage. Reversed, the oxide layer that forms the dielectric breaks down. The fix is to read the polarity marking on the part and check it against the schematic before you solder, and to use a non-polarised ceramic capacitor where a polarised one is not needed.

Mistake 4: a filter whose output is loaded

The symptom is a filter whose cut-off measures lower than the calculation, because the next stage draws current through the output. The load sits in parallel with the capacitor, as the voltage divider in lesson 8 showed, and it changes the effective resistance and capacitance. The fix is to put a buffer or a much higher-impedance input after the filter, or to measure the cut-off with the real load connected.

In real projects

Every powered chip on a professional board has decoupling capacitors, and the placement is reviewed as carefully as the logic. The standard rule is a 100 nF capacitor for each supply pin, placed close, with a larger bulk capacitor near the regulator or the power-hungry part. The RP2040 hardware design guide describes it in its decoupling section (2.1.2), and it adds that the regulator's own input and output need 1 µF capacitors close to the pins (section 2.1.3). A reviewer checks those placements against the datasheet's rules before the board is sent for manufacture.

Filters are common too: a reset input with a time constant, an analogue sensor with an anti-aliasing filter ahead of an ADC, and a supply ripple filter after a switching regulator. Each one is a resistor and a capacitor whose time constant is chosen on purpose. In a design review, the question is usually "what is the time constant, and what does it do to the signal you care about?" If the answer is a number with units, the reviewer can check it.

Summary

  • A capacitor stores charge, Q = C × V, and its current is i = C × dV/dt, so its voltage cannot change in zero time.
  • The energy stored is ½ × C × V². Decoupling capacitors deliver short bursts of charge; they are not a power source.
  • The RC time constant is τ = R × C. The capacitor reaches about 63 % of a step after one τ and about 99 % after five τ.
  • The charging curve is V(t) = Vstep × (1 − e−t/τ), so every value follows from t ÷ τ.
  • A decoupling capacitor belongs at the supply pin, with a 100 nF value per pin, because the wire's inductance dominates a fast burst.
  • A low-pass filter has fc = 1 ÷ (2π × R × C), and it attenuates signals far above fc.
  • Write every value with its prefix, convert to ohms and farads, and check the units before you trust a number.
