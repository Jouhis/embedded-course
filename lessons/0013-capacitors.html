Capacitors: decoupling, time constants and filters

Every chip on your board has a small capacitor next to its power pins. This lesson shows why, and how a resistor and a capacitor set a time.

By the end of this lesson you can calculate an RC time constant, predict how long a capacitor takes to settle, and say why a 100 nF capacitor sits next to every chip's supply pin.

The idea

A capacitor stores charge. The voltage across it cannot change instantly, because changing it means moving charge through the resistance in the circuit. Its behaviour is set by the capacitance, C, in farads (F). A useful size for microcontroller work is the microfarad (µF) or the nanofarad (nF), where 1 µF = 1000 nF. The farad is a large unit. A typical decoupling capacitor is 100 nF, which is 0.0000001 F.

Decoupling: the capacitor next to each chip

A chip's supply pins draw current in short bursts whenever its logic switches. The supply wire has some inductance and resistance, so the voltage at the chip dips each time. A capacitor placed close to the supply pin supplies that burst locally, so the rail stays steady. The RP2040 datasheet asks for a 100 nF capacitor close to each IOVDD pin (RP2040 datasheet, section 2.9.1). "Close" matters more than the value. A capacitor at the far end of a wire does little, because the wire's inductance is in the way. The same reasoning is why the capacitor's leads should be short.

The RC time constant

Put a resistor R in series with a capacitor C and apply a step voltage. The capacitor charges exponentially, and the time constant is τ = R × C seconds. After one τ the capacitor has reached 63.2 % of the step. After 5τ it is at 99.3 %, which is close enough to call it settled. These values come from the exponential 1 − e−t/τ (All About Circuits textbook, RC circuits).

Worked numbers: a 100 Ω resistor with a 200 µF capacitor has τ = 100 × 200×10−6 = 0.02 s, or 20 ms. It settles in about 5 × 20 ms = 100 ms. A 10 kΩ resistor with the same capacitor gives 2 s, so the resistor you pick sets the speed directly. Be careful with units. Use ohms and farads in the formula. A value written as 200 (with no unit) is a guess, and the error is a factor of a million.

Filters: a resistor and capacitor together

The same pair works as a low-pass filter. Signals with a frequency well below fc = 1 / (2π × R × C) pass to the output almost unchanged, and faster signals are attenuated. At fc the output is 70.7 % of the input. Decoupling is the same idea: the capacitor shunts fast noise on the supply to ground.

Try it in CircuitJS

The built-in example charges a 200 µF capacitor through a 100 Ω resistor from a 5 V supply. You will measure how long the capacitor takes to reach 63 % of 5 V, and compare with τ.

  1. Open the example: CircuitJS: cap.txt.
  2. Check the values before you run it. The resistor is 100 Ω and the capacitor is 200 µF, so predict τ = R × C before you measure anything.
  3. Click the switch to connect the capacitor to the supply, so it starts to charge from 0 V. The scope shows the capacitor voltage against time.
  4. Read the time at which the trace reaches 3.16 V, which is 63 % of 5 V. Use the scope's time axis. Give the answer in milliseconds.
Hint

If the trace does not move, the switch may be in the other position. Click it again. Read the time where the curve crosses the 3.16 V line, not where it starts to rise.

The measured time should be close to τ. If it is about 5 times τ, you read the time to 99 % instead of 63 %.

You measured an RC time constant from a scope trace and matched it to R × C. You can now size a decoupling network or a filter cut-off from a resistor and a capacitor.

"A microcontroller resets when a motor starts. Where do you look first, and what do you add?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

I would check the supply at the chip's pins on a scope while the motor starts, to confirm a dip. Then I would look for decoupling: a 100 nF capacitor should sit right at each supply pin, with a bulk capacitor such as 10 µF nearby to supply the larger burst. If the motor shares a rail, I would separate the motor's supply from the logic supply with a filter or a separate regulator, so its current does not pull the rail down.

RP2040 datasheet, section 2.9.1 "Digital IO Supply (IOVDD)": the 100 nF per-pin decoupling rule, stated by the chip's maker. The same page sets the supply range and explains the pin supply.
