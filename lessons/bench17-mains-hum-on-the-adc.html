Bench lab 17: Mains hum on the ADC

Mains interference on an analog input and a filter tuned to remove it

By the end of this lab you can measure interference picked up by an analog input, and choose a filter that removes a known frequency completely, explaining why a moving average one period long does that.

Where the hum comes from

A 30 cm wire on an ADC pin picks up the 50 Hz field of the wiring in your walls. This lab measures the hum's size and frequency on the Pico 2, then cancels it with a moving average exactly one mains period long and compares that with lesson 107's IIR filter. It is optional, needing a Pico 2, a 30 cm jumper wire and a 1 MΩ resistor, it does not count towards your progress, and it takes about 45 minutes.

The mains wiring in a building carries 230 V at 50 Hz in Europe (120 V at 60 Hz in North America and parts of Asia), and its electric field reaches any nearby conductor through a tiny stray capacitance, a few picofarads. A wire on a high-impedance input, like the ADC pin with only a 1 MΩ resistor to ground, turns that into a small 50 Hz voltage, and a sensor on a long cable picks it up in exactly the same way. Wokwi has no walls, so its ADC input is clean.

Lesson 107 gave you filters against noise, a moving average, an exponential moving average (an IIR filter) and a median. Random noise needs a filter that averages, whereas hum is a known frequency, so a filter can be designed to remove that frequency specifically.

Cancellation over one mains period

Averaging a sine wave over exactly one of its periods gives zero, because the positive half cancels the negative half, whatever phase you start at. The same holds for its harmonics, 100 Hz, 150 Hz and so on, because a whole number of their periods fits in the same window, so a moving average whose window is exactly 20 ms long passes the slow signal you want and nulls 50 Hz and all its harmonics.

The program in projects/bench17-mains-hum samples ADC0 at 20 samples per mains period, 1000 samples a second for 50 Hz, with sleep_until() on a fixed schedule, so that the rate does not drift. Once a second it prints the peak-to-peak size of three signals.

  • raw: the ADC codes as read;
  • iir: lesson 107's exponential moving average with k = 3, a smoothing factor α of 1/8;
  • ma20: the moving average, whose window you choose.

It also counts how often the raw signal crosses its mean going upwards, with a small hysteresis band so that noise does not add false crossings, and over one second that count is the hum's frequency. Streaming the samples to the PC and plotting them would give the same answer, but the on-board numbers need no plotting script, although you can still plot if you like.

To compare the filters, we work out what each does to 50 Hz at 1000 samples a second. An N-sample moving average has the gain

GMA(f) = |sin(Nπf ÷ fs) ÷ (N · sin(πf ÷ fs))|(1)

where f is the frequency and fs the sample rate, a gain that is zero whenever N samples hold a whole number of periods of f. The EMA with smoothing factor α has the gain

GEMA(f) = α ÷ |1 − (1 − α) · e−j2πf/fs|(2)

where j is the imaginary unit, a gain that falls with frequency but never reaches zero (Smith, The Scientist and Engineer's Guide to DSP, chapters 15 and 19).

Lesson 107's moving average had a ring buffer of 16 entries, and this project raises MA_MAX_WINDOW to 32, because one period needs 20.

Stage 1: in Wokwi

  1. Build and run the starter, in which a potentiometer stands in for the wire. Every line shows p-p raw=0 iir=0 ma20=0 and a frequency of 0 Hz, a perfectly clean input.
  2. Turn the knob during a block, and the raw span jumps while the filtered ones follow more slowly, the step response lesson 107 described and the only thing the simulator can show here.

Stage 2: on the board

Wire the 1 MΩ resistor from GP26 to AGND (pin 33), and push a 30 cm jumper into GP26 with its other end free. Lay the wire near a mains cable or an extension lead without ever opening anything, then flash the starter and open the serial port.

Work only with low-voltage wiring on the bench. The wire picks up the mains field through the air; it must never touch mains conductors. Don't open plugs, sockets or extension leads.
  1. Read a few blocks, in which raw should show tens to hundreds of codes peak to peak and the frequency should be close to 50 (or 60). Touching the free end of the wire makes you a bigger antenna, and the hum grows.
  2. Compare iir and ma20 (still a 16-sample window in the starter) with raw and with the table above.
  3. Task: set the moving average's window to one mains period, PER_PERIOD, then rebuild, flash and compare again. ma20 drops to a few codes, because the hum is gone and what is left is ordinary ADC noise.
  4. If you are on 60 Hz mains, set MAINS_HZ to 60, so that the program samples at 1200 Hz and 20 samples are still one period.

A few codes usually remain even with the right window, because the mains frequency is not exactly 50.000 Hz, grid frequency wandering by small fractions of a hertz while the Pico's sample clock has its own tolerance, so the window is not exactly one period and the cancellation is not perfect. Real products that must reject mains well, such as precision meters, use the same idea, integrating over a whole number of mains periods.

You found a 50 Hz signal that nobody put there, measured its frequency on the chip itself and removed it with a filter designed for exactly that frequency.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

A window that is almost a period

Part of the hum remains with a 16- or 18-sample window, because the cancellation is exact only for a whole number of periods. Set the window to exactly one mains period.

A drifting sample rate

The tuned window still leaves hum, because sleep_ms(1) after a read gives slightly more than 1 ms per sample, so the window no longer spans 20 ms. Schedule each sample from a fixed start with delayed_by_us() and sleep_until().

The code blamed for hum the wiring causes

Filtering helps but the hum keeps coming back with each new setup, because its source is the wiring. A shorter wire, a lower source impedance or a shielded cable remove hum at its source, filtering being the second line of defence.

Hum treated as random noise

More averaging still leaves a periodic residue, because an average of the wrong length only attenuates a periodic signal. Use a window tuned to the mains period.

Summary

  • A high-impedance input with a wire on it picks up the mains field, a 50 or 60 Hz signal in addition to random noise.
  • A moving average exactly one mains period long nulls the mains frequency and all its harmonics, at any phase.
  • An EMA or a moving average of another length only attenuates the hum.
  • Sampling on a fixed schedule keeps the window's length in time correct.
  • Wokwi's inputs have no interference, so this is a bench-only effect.
