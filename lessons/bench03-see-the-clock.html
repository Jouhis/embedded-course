Bench lab 3: See the clock

Put the Pico 2's system clock on a pin and measure it with a logic analyzer. Then change the PLL and watch the UART fall apart on the board while the simulator carries on.

This bench lab is optional. It needs a Pico 2 and the 8-channel logic analyzer with PulseView (see bench lab 0). It doesn't count towards your progress. Time: about 50 minutes.
By the end of this lab you can route an internal clock to a GPIO, measure it, and explain why changing the system clock broke the UART and how to fix it.

Which clock feeds the UART

Lesson 40 drew the clock tree: oscillators and PLLs at the root, and named clocks branching off to the parts of the chip. Each peripheral runs from one of those branches, and the UART runs from clk_peri (RP2350 datasheet, section 8.1). Its baud rate is clk_peri divided by a baud divisor, and the SDK computes that divisor once, in uart_init(), from the clk_peri frequency at that moment.

At start-up the SDK runs clk_sys at 150 MHz and clk_peri from clk_sys, also at 150 MHz. When you call set_sys_clock_khz() to change the system clock, the SDK can't leave clk_peri on clk_sys, because the UART would change speed with it. By default it moves clk_peri to the USB PLL at 48 MHz instead (the SDK option PICO_CLOCK_ADJUST_PERI_CLOCK_WITH_SYS_CLOCK is 0). The UART's divisor still assumes 150 MHz. So after the change the UART sends at 115 200 × 48 / 150 ≈ 36 900 baud, about a third of the rate the receiver expects.

Wokwi's serial monitor reads the characters the UART model emits; it doesn't time the bits on the wire. So in the simulator, the text stays readable. On the board, anything listening at 115 200 baud sees garbage.

Putting a clock on a pin

The RP2350's clock block has four general-purpose clock outputs, clk_gpout0 to 3, that can drive an internal clock out on a pin through a divider. GPIO21 is one of the pins that can carry GPOUT0 (RP2350 datasheet, sections 8.1 and 1.2.3). The program does it in one SDK call: 

clock_gpio_init(21, CLOCKS_CLK_GPOUT0_CTRL_AUXSRC_VALUE_CLK_SYS, 150);

150 MHz is far too fast for a 24 MS/s logic analyzer, so the divider brings it down to 1 MHz. A 24 MS/s capture of a 1 MHz square wave gives 24 samples per period: enough to count. After the PLL change, the same divider gives 100 MHz / 150 ≈ 667 kHz, and you'll see it change on the trace.

Stage 1: in Wokwi

  1. Open projects/bench03-see-the-clock/starter, build and run. The diagram has a logic analyzer part on GP0 (UART0 TX) and GP21.
  2. The log shows clk_sys=150000000 clk_peri=150000000, ten test lines, then after the PLL change: clk_sys=100000000 clk_peri=48000000, and ten more test lines, all readable.
  3. The program reports the new clk_peri itself. It knows the clock changed. The UART divisor doesn't.

Stage 2: on the board

Connect the logic analyzer: D0 to GP0, D1 to GP21, GND to a Pico GND pin. Flash the starter and open the USB serial port: the USB log is unaffected, because USB runs on its own clock, clk_usb.

  1. In PulseView, set 24 MHz and a few million samples, and capture across the moment the log prints after the PLL change.
  2. Measure D1: zoom into a stretch before the change and use a cursor pair over ten periods. You should get 10 µs (1 MHz), and after the change 15 µs (≈ 667 kHz).
  3. Add a UART decoder on D0 at 115 200 baud, 8N1. Lines from before the change decode as BENCH3 UART test 0123456789. Lines from after it decode as garbage and framing errors.
  4. Measure one bit time after the change: the narrowest pulse on D0. At 115 200 baud a bit is 8.7 µs; you'll find about 27 µs.
  5. Your fix: after set_sys_clock_khz(), recompute the divisor with uart_set_baudrate(uart0, BAUD), which reads the current clk_peri frequency. Print the baud rate it returns. Rebuild, flash, capture again: both halves decode.

The value uart_set_baudrate() returns is the rate it actually achieved, not the one you asked for: the divisor has a 16-bit integer part and a 6-bit fraction, so the result is close but rarely exact. Logging it is a cheap way to catch a clock that isn't what you think.

You measured a clock that lives inside the chip, and fixed a bug that only exists on real wires. Every "garbage on the serial port" bug you meet from now on starts with the question you just answered: which clock, and did it change?

Common mistakes
  1. Changing a clock without re-initialising what depends on it. UART, SPI and I2C dividers, PWM periods and timer-based delays may all assume the old frequency. After any clock change, list the peripherals on that clock and redo their rate setup.
  2. Sampling the clock output too slowly. A 24 MS/s analyzer can't show 150 MHz; it shows aliased nonsense. Divide the clock down first.
  3. No common ground. Without the analyzer's GND on a Pico GND, the trace is noise. Connect it first.
  4. Trusting the simulator's serial output for timing. Wokwi passes characters, not bit timing; baud errors only show on real wires.

Summary

  • The UART runs from clk_peri, and its divisor is computed once from clk_peri's frequency.
  • set_sys_clock_khz() moves clk_peri to the 48 MHz USB PLL by default, so the old divisor gives the wrong baud rate.
  • Fix: call uart_set_baudrate() after the change; it recomputes from the current clock and returns the achieved rate.
  • clock_gpio_init() puts an internal clock, divided, on a GPOUT pin, where a logic analyzer can measure it.
  • Wokwi doesn't time UART bits, so baud errors are a bench-only bug.
