PCB design in KiCad: from schematic to board

Every circuit in Parts 1 to 8 ends up on a board. This lesson walks one small design from the schematic to the files a factory needs, and shows where the electronics lessons come back as layout rules.

By the end of this lesson you can outline a KiCad workflow for a small two-layer board, explain what ERC and DRC each check, place a decoupling capacitor and route a ground return correctly, and review a schematic for the mistakes a first board usually has.

This lesson is optional. It is useful if you want to design your own breakout boards or to read a hardware team's schematics and layout reviews. KiCad is not installed in this course, and no hardware is needed: the exercises are concept questions.

Where we are, and why a firmware engineer needs this

Every circuit in Parts 1 to 8 ran on a breadboard, a Wokwi diagram or a dev board. A product needs the same circuit on a printed board, and someone has to draw that board. Lesson 17 taught you to read a schematic, and lesson 18 taught you to read the datasheet that gives a part's pins and package. This lesson turns those two skills around: you draw the schematic, pick each part's package from its datasheet, and lay the copper out so the circuit works on a real board.

The job comes up in the capstone. Part 10 builds a filament-dryer controller (lesson 112), and its heater driver, its thermistor input and its Pico socket need a board. A firmware engineer who can read a layout can spot a bad heater return, a noisy ADC ground or a missing decoupling capacitor in a colleague's board, and that review is where many hardware bugs are caught before a board is ordered. The lesson is optional, but the review skill is worth having.

The idea

A printed circuit board is a schematic turned into copper. The schematic says which pins connect (the netlist); the layout says where the parts sit and how the copper runs. KiCad is a free, open-source suite that does both, plus the rule checks and the output files a manufacturer needs. Its schematic editor is Eeschema and its board editor is Pcbnew.

The workflow has six stages, and each one produces something the next stage uses:

The Eeschema manual for KiCad 9 describes the schematic stages: choosing symbols, assigning footprints, the electrical rules check, the netlist export and annotation of reference designators KiCad 9.0 documentation, Eeschema. ERC checks the schematic against electrical rules, not the board. A clean ERC does not mean the layout is right, and a DRC pass does not mean the circuit is right. Run both.

The words the workflow uses

A schematic is the drawing of a circuit as symbols joined by wires, one symbol per part. The circuit's connections are its netlist, the list of which pins are joined. A PCB (printed circuit board) is the physical board, a stack of copper layers bonded to an insulating core, with the parts soldered to it. Each symbol is linked to a footprint, the pattern of copper pads and outline that the part is soldered onto. The footprint comes from the package drawing in the datasheet, so a wrong footprint is a datasheet-reading error.

On the board, a trace is a strip of copper that carries a signal, and a ground pour is a large area of copper filled with ground. A Gerber file describes one layer of the board in a format the factory can read. The rest of the lesson uses these words, and the glossary defines each one.

A concrete design: the capstone breakout

The capstone (Part 10) is a filament-dryer controller. This breakout board is a small piece of it: a Pico socket, a thermistor input, and a heater driver. It is a good first design because each block uses something from Part 1.

  • NTC input divider. A fixed resistor from 3V3 to the ADC pin, and the NTC from the pin to ground. This is the divider of lesson 8 with the thermistor as the lower arm of lesson 58. A pull-up of 4.7 kΩ is what Klipper's own thermistor configuration assumes Klipper Config Reference, so it is a sensible starting value for a printer-style sensor. The ADC pin should carry only the divider, with a 100 nF capacitor across it to filter fast noise (lesson 13).
  • MOSFET heater driver. A logic-level N-channel MOSFET, low-side, switches the heater's return to ground (lesson 12). The gate gets a series resistor against ringing and a pull-down so the heater stays off during reset. Check the MOSFET's RDS(on) at the gate voltage the Pico gives (3.3 V), not at the datasheet's headline number.
  • Flyback diode. A heater is resistive, so it does not need a flyback diode: no energy is stored in a magnetic field that must go somewhere. A fan, a solenoid or a relay on the same board is inductive, and it does need one (lesson 14). Put it across the load, cathode to the positive supply, as close to the load as the layout allows. Adding the diode to a resistive heater is harmless, but it is not the reason the board works.
  • Pico header. Two rows of pin sockets at the Pico's 2.54 mm pitch, so the board takes the Pico as a plug-in module. Check the Pico datasheet's pin names, not the silkscreen of a clone.

Worked example: the NTC input, with numbers

Take the thermistor input from the breakout list: a 4.7 kΩ fixed resistor from 3V3 to the ADC pin, and a 100 kΩ NTC from the pin to ground. Klipper's sensor list includes 100 kΩ NTC thermistors, so the numbers are realistic (Klipper Config Reference). The NTC is the lower arm of the divider from lesson 8, so the pin voltage is 3.3 V × RNTC ÷ (Rfixed + RNTC).

At 25 °C the NTC is 100 kΩ, so the pin voltage is 3.3 × 100 ÷ 104.7 = 3.15 V. The ADC converts that to a code of 3.15 ÷ 3.3 × 4095 = 3911, using the 3.3 V reference and the 4095 top code from lesson 58. As the NTC warms its resistance falls, the pin voltage falls, and the code falls with it.

The two extremes are a good bench test before any firmware runs. With the NTC unplugged, the pin is pulled to 3.3 V and the code is 4095. With the NTC shorted, the pin is at ground and the code is 0. A board that reads anything else at those two extremes has a fault in the divider or in the connections, and the fault is findable with a multimeter (lesson 10) before the board ever reaches the firmware.

Decoupling capacitors and the power pins

A decoupling capacitor is a local reservoir of charge: it supplies the short current bursts a chip takes when its transistors switch, so the supply does not sag. It must sit close to the pin it serves, because the trace between them adds inductance and the burst arrives late. The RP2040 hardware design guide asks for 100 nF per power pin placed close to the pins, plus 1 µF on each side of its internal regulator, and it stresses that placement is what matters, so the capacitor must sit close to its pin Hardware design with RP2040, section 2.1.2 and 2.1.3.

A decoupling capacitor placed 10 mm from its pin does little against fast edges. Put it on the same side of the board as the pin, with the shortest trace to the power and ground pads.

Two-layer layout rules

A two-layer board has a top and a bottom copper layer and no inner planes. The RP2040 guide's own minimal design is a two-layer board, and its section on making a PCB notes that the design rules are relaxed to keep fabrication cheap Hardware design with RP2040, section 2.7. The rules that matter most on a two-layer board are:

  1. Pour ground on the bottom layer. A solid ground pour gives every signal a low-impedance return path directly beneath it. Route signals on the top layer, and keep the bottom layer clear of signal tracks where you can. The RP2040 guide's minimal design does this: it states that almost the entire bottom copper layer is ground (Hardware design with RP2040, section 2.4.1).
  2. Keep the heater current loop separate. The heater's high current flows from the supply, through the heater, through the MOSFET and back to ground. Make that loop short and wide, and keep the ADC divider's ground connection away from it, so the heater's current does not add a voltage error to the thermistor reading.
  3. Route high-speed or noisy lines over solid copper. A trace that crosses a gap in the ground pour has a long return path and radiates. Move the trace or fill the gap.
  4. Keep the crystal and its load capacitors close to the chip. The guide's section 2.3 shows the crystal with two load capacitors, C2 and C3, each from one crystal terminal to ground, and it asks for them to be placed with the crystal.

Design rules set the minimum track width and clearance for the fabricator. Those numbers come from your manufacturer's capability sheet, not from KiCad's defaults. DRC checks the layout against the rules you set, and it also checks that every pad is connected as the netlist says, so a missing connection is a DRC error as well as a clearance problem (KiCad 9.0 documentation, Pcbnew, design rules checking). A rule that is wrong for your factory passes a board that the factory rejects. Read the capability sheet before you tune DRC.

Gerbers and ordering

The factory does not take the KiCad project. It takes Gerber files, one per copper layer, mask and silkscreen, plus a drill file and a bill of materials and a pick-and-place file if you want assembly. KiCad exports them from the board editor's plot dialog. Check the files in a Gerber viewer before you order: a missing layer or a shifted drill file is the most common first-board mistake. Panelisation, copper weight and the board thickness are chosen at ordering time.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

A first board fails in a few predictable ways. Each mistake below has a symptom in one of the checks or in the bench test, and the fix is a short step in the workflow.

Mistake 1: a power net with no power flag

The Eeschema manual notes that ERC often reports "Input Power pin not driven by any Output Power pins" on power pins that look connected, because the power arrives through a connector or a part that is not marked as a power output. The symptom is that ERC flags the 3V3 pin of the Pico socket even though the wire is there. The fix is a power flag on the rail, which tells ERC the net has a source. The flag does not create any voltage, so the regulator still has to be wired correctly.

Mistake 2: a wire that crosses another wire

The Eeschema manual says that only wire ends create connections, and that a wire crossing the middle of another wire makes no connection. The symptom is a net that is named on one sheet but has a gap, or an unconnected pin that the drawing seems to join. The fix is to end the wires on the pins or on each other, and to place a junction where a joint is meant to be. Look for the small dot as well as the lines.

Mistake 3: a footprint that does not match the datasheet

ERC checks the schematic's electrical rules, not whether the footprint's pads match the part. A footprint with a mirrored or renumbered pad passes ERC. The symptom appears in DRC, where a pad that is not on the netlist shows as unconnected, or at the bench, where a pin that is wired to the heater does not reach the pad. The fix is to compare the footprint's pad numbers with the package drawing in the datasheet, which is the reading skill from lesson 18.

Mistake 4: default design rules instead of the factory's

A board that passes DRC with KiCad's default track width and clearance is not proven for your factory. The symptom comes from the factory, not from KiCad: a board that passed DRC is rejected for a track or drill below the factory's minimum, or the fabricator asks for a change before it starts. The fix is to copy the minimum track width, clearance and drill sizes from the capability sheet into the board setup before the first DRC run.

Mistake 5: a decoupling capacitor far from its pin

The RP2040 guide asks for 100 nF close to each power pin, and lesson 13 explained why. A capacitor on a long trace delivers its charge late, so the supply still dips when a burst of switching arrives. The symptom on the bench is intermittent: the board runs, then resets when a heater or a motor switches on. The fix is to place the capacitor on the same side of the board as the pin, with the shortest trace to the power and ground pads.

In real projects

A hardware team reviews a board in a fixed order before it is ordered. The schematic is reviewed first, with ERC clean and every power net, every connector and every heater path checked by hand. Then the layout is reviewed against the same schematic, with DRC clean against the factory's rules and the decoupling, the heater return and the ADC ground checked by eye. Only then are the Gerbers exported and checked in a viewer. Each step catches a different class of error, and no step replaces the others.

Firmware engineers take part in the review because the board is where firmware assumptions are made. The pin that the firmware uses as an ADC input must not share a trace with a heater current, and the pin that drives a MOSFET must match the logic-level part the schematic shows (lesson 12). A reviewer who reads the netlist against the firmware's pin map catches a whole class of bugs that no simulator sees.

Interviewers often ask about the difference between ERC and DRC, because the answer shows whether you know what each check reads. Practise the model answer below.

Summary

  • A schematic is the circuit as symbols and wires, and its netlist is the list of connected pins that the board must match.
  • ERC checks the schematic's electrical rules: unconnected pins, conflicting outputs and missing power flags. DRC checks the layout against the board's rules and the netlist.
  • A two-layer board routes signals on the top and pours ground on the bottom; keep the heater return away from the ADC ground.
  • Place each 100 nF decoupling capacitor on the same side of the board as its pin, with the shortest trace to power and ground.
  • Set the design rules from the factory's capability sheet before you trust DRC.
  • Check the Gerber files in a viewer before you order, because a missing layer is the most common first-board mistake.

Model answer

ERC checks the schematic's electrical rules: unconnected pins, outputs wired together, power pins without a power source. DRC checks the board's physical rules: copper clearances, minimum track width, drill sizes, and pad-to-track spacing, against the fabricator's limits. A schematic can pass ERC and still have a wrong part value, and a layout can pass DRC and still route the heater current through the ADC ground. Run both, then review the board by hand.
