Bench lab 5: The noisy ADC

ADC noise measured and reduced

By the end of this lab you can measure an ADC's noise as a standard deviation in codes, and reduce it with supply-mode control, oversampling and offset correction, recording what each step gained.

Where the noise comes from on a Pico 2

In Wokwi a potentiometer reads the same code every time, whereas on a Pico 2 the readings jitter by several codes, and this lab measures the spread and then shrinks it with the three remedies the board's datasheet gives. It is optional, needing a Pico 2, a 10 kΩ potentiometer, jumper wires and a multimeter, it does not count towards your progress, and it takes about 45 minutes.

Lesson 58 converted ADC codes to volts with a 3.3 V reference, whereas the RP2350's ADC has no reference of its own but uses its supply, ADC_AVDD, which on the Pico 2 is the board's 3.3 V switch-mode regulator output through a 200 Ω and 2.2 µF R-C filter. The datasheet lists what that costs, the 3.3 V rail's accuracy not being great, ADC_AVDD being "somewhat noisy" and the ADC's own current through the 200 Ω resistor giving an offset of about 30 mV (Pico 2 datasheet, section 5.3).

The same section gives three remedies, each of which this lab tries.

  1. Force the regulator into PWM mode by driving GPIO23 high. At light load the regulator otherwise runs in a power-saving mode with more ripple, and that ripple reaches the ADC supply. PWM mode costs some efficiency at light load.
  2. Average several conversions (oversampling). For random noise, averaging N readings shrinks the spread by about √N.
  3. Measure a grounded channel and use its reading as an estimate of the offset.

The datasheet also offers the stronger fix, an external 3.0 V shunt reference on ADC_VREF, which limits the input range to 0 to 3.0 V, a hardware change beyond this lab.

The program

projects/bench05-noisy-adc reads the potentiometer's wiper on GP27 (ADC1) in blocks of 256 values every 2 s and prints the mean, minimum, maximum and standard deviation of each block in ADC codes, also reading GP28 (ADC2), which you wire to AGND, the zero channel, while the starter has OVERSAMPLE 1 and leaves GPIO23 alone.

Stage 1: in Wokwi

  1. Build and run the starter, whose log shows sd=0.00 with min equal to max, because the simulated ADC returns a perfect, noiseless code.
  2. Turn the knob in the diagram, the mean following it, still without any spread, so every averaging decision you would make from this simulation would be that none is needed.

Stage 2: on the board

Wire the potentiometer's ends to 3V3 (pin 36) and AGND (pin 33) and its wiper to GP27, and wire GP28 to AGND too, using AGND rather than any GND pin, because it is the ground return the ADC reference is measured against, then flash the starter, open the serial port and set the knob somewhere near the middle, leaving it untouched during a run.

  1. Baseline. Record sd, min and max for three blocks, then measure the wiper voltage with the multimeter and compute the code you would expect, V × 4095 ÷ 3.3.
  2. Remedy 1. Where the starter marks remedy 1, add code that sets GPIO23 as an output and drives it high, then rebuild, flash and record three blocks.
  3. Remedy 2. Set OVERSAMPLE to 4 and then 16, recording three blocks each, and compare the sd with √4 = 2 and √16 = 4 times smaller.
  4. Remedy 3. Look at the zero channel figure and the corrected value, checking whether corrected is closer to the multimeter's code than mean is.

Comparing the rows shows two things, the first being that the gain from averaging is usually close to √N but not exact, because noise that is slow compared with the 16 conversions (about 32 µs at the ADC's 500 kS/s) is not random within one average, so averaging cannot remove it. The second is that averaging improves repeatability, the spread, but not accuracy, the gap to the meter, because the offset and the 3.3 V rail's error stay in every average, the zero channel tackling the offset while only a better reference fixes the gain.

You turned jittering readings into a table of numbers with a measured effect next to every change you made, which is the difference between tuning and guessing.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

The knob or the wires touched during a block

One block shows a much larger spread than the others, because your hand moved the wiper and coupled noise in. Set the knob, let go and then read.

The ADC channel number used as the GPIO number

The program reads the wrong pin, because adc_select_input(1) selects GP27 while adc_gpio_init() takes the GPIO, 27, and the two numbers were mixed. Use the channel number for the input selection and the GPIO number for the pin setup.

Averaging expected to fix accuracy

The averaged mean is steady but still differs from the meter, because averaging reduces only random spread, whereas a constant offset survives any amount of averaging. Correct the offset with the zero channel and the gain with a better reference.

5 V fed into an ADC pin

The pin or the chip is damaged, because GPIO26 to 29 have a diode to IOVDD and are not 5 V tolerant (Pico 2 datasheet, section 5.2). Keep the potentiometer on 3V3.

Summary

  • The Pico 2's ADC uses its filtered 3.3 V supply as the reference, so supply noise and offset show in every reading.
  • Wokwi's ADC is noiseless, so noise is a bench-only effect.
  • Driving GPIO23 high puts the regulator in PWM mode, lowering ripple at some efficiency cost.
  • Averaging N conversions reduces random noise by about √N without reducing offset or gain error.
  • A grounded channel estimates the offset, and a precise external reference fixes the gain.
