Lab 1: Design and simulate an LED and MOSFET load driver

A 3.3 V pin switches a 12 V, 24 W heater. You choose every component, justify each value from a datasheet, and check the result in CircuitJS.

By the end of this lab you can design a logic-level low-side MOSFET driver for a 12 V load, justify the gate resistor, pull-down and MOSFET parameters from datasheet numbers, and verify the load current and MOSFET dissipation in a simulator.

Where we are, and why this lab exists

This is the first lab, and it joins the course's electronics lessons into one design. Lesson 12 showed a MOSFET used as a switch, with its gate threshold and its on-resistance. Lesson 14 showed why a heater, a motor or a relay coil needs a flyback diode when it is switched off. Lesson 15 showed how a rail carries power and how that power becomes heat. Lesson 16 showed the logic levels of a 3.3 V pin and why a pull-down holds a line low. Lesson 17 showed how to trace the drawing, and lesson 18 showed how to read the numbers that make a part's behaviour guaranteed. The lab asks you to use all of them on one circuit, and to write down the reason for every value.

The reason to do this is that a driver is the most common circuit a beginner builds and the most common one to build wrong. A pin on a microcontroller is too weak to drive a heater, so a transistor sits between them. The design question is not "does it turn on" but "does it turn on fully, with the right current, without overheating the switch, and without damaging the pin". Those are the questions a senior engineer asks in a design review, and the lab makes you answer each one with a number from a datasheet.

The brief

A microcontroller pin on 3.3 V must switch a 12 V heater that draws about 2 A, which is the kind of load a 3D printer's heated bed or a fan driver switches. The MOSFET sits on the low side, between the heater and ground, and the heater's other end is on the 12 V rail. The pin drives the gate, through a gate resistor, and a pull-down holds the gate low when the pin is not driving it. The heater is modelled as a 6 Ω resistor. That is a steady-state model: a real heater's resistance changes with its temperature, so the 6 Ω value gives the operating point you will check. Use the low side (lesson 12): a 3.3 V pin cannot turn on a high-side N-channel device, because its gate would need to be above 12 V.

You will need three numbers from datasheets. The RP2040 datasheet gives the GPIO's default drive current of 4 mA (Table 614). The IRLZ44N datasheet gives the gate threshold range of 1.0 to 2.0 V, the on-resistance at 4 V and 5 V, and the thermal resistance of 1.4 °C/W from junction to case (Electrical Characteristics and Thermal Resistance). The IRLZ44N is a worked example here. Any logic-level MOSFET is acceptable if you justify it with its own datasheet.

The design choices, one at a time

Each part in the design answers one question. Before you place any part, write the question and the answer in one sentence. The sentences are what the acceptance criteria check.

Why a low-side switch

A low-side switch sits between the load and ground, so the load stays on the 12 V rail. The MOSFET's source is at ground, so its gate voltage is measured from ground, and the 3.3 V pin is a good match for it. A high-side switch would sit between the 12 V rail and the load. Its gate would have to be about 12 V above ground to turn on, and a 3.3 V pin cannot provide that. The low-side position has a cost: the load's terminal sits at the full 12 V rail even when the switch is off, so the load is always live. For a heater that is acceptable, and the source sits at ground, which keeps the gate drive simple.

Why a logic-level MOSFET

A MOSFET conducts when its gate is above its source by more than its threshold voltage, written VGS(th). A standard MOSFET may need 4 V or more on the gate before it conducts well, and a 3.3 V pin would leave it partly on. A logic-level part is designed to switch fully on at 4 V or 5 V, and the IRLZ44N's datasheet gives its on-resistance at 4 V and 5 V (IRLZ44N datasheet, Electrical Characteristics). Its threshold is between 1.0 V and 2.0 V, so at 3.3 V the device has a useful overdrive of at least 1.3 V. The lab sets the threshold to the worst-case maximum, 2.0 V, so the design works for every part that meets the datasheet.

The gate resistor and the pin's drive

The gate of a MOSFET is insulated, so once it is charged it draws no DC current. But charging it is a current pulse. The gate looks like a small capacitor, and a step of 3.3 V on it draws a surge through the pin. The peak current is set by the gate resistor: I = 3.3 V ÷ Rgate. The RP2040's default drive is 4 mA, so the resistor must limit the peak to at most 4 mA, which means Rgate of at least 3.3 V ÷ 4 mA = 825 Ω. A 1 kΩ resistor gives a peak of 3.3 mA, which is inside the drive. The resistor also slows the edge, which is acceptable here because a heater switches slowly.

The pull-down

A pull-down resistor holds the gate low when the pin is high impedance. That happens during reset, which you read about in lesson 16, and when the driver is disconnected. Without it, the gate would float, and a floating gate can turn the heater on by accident. The lab uses 100 kΩ. When the pin is high, the pull-down draws 3.3 V ÷ 100 kΩ = 33 µA, which is small against the 4 mA drive.

The pull-down and the gate resistor make a divider with the pin, and that matters for the gate voltage. The pin supplies the 33 µA that the pull-down draws, and that current flows through the 1 kΩ gate resistor. It drops 33 µA × 1 kΩ = 33 mV. So the gate sits at about 3.3 V × 100 kΩ ÷ (100 kΩ + 1 kΩ) = 3.27 V, not at exactly 3.3 V. The MOSFET itself draws no current, but the pin does supply this small current. That 33 mV is the effect you will see in the solution's values, and it is why the reference values below differ from a calculation that ignores the pull-down.

Beta, the on-resistance and the overdrive

The lab's requirement 3 asks for an on-resistance at 3.3 V of at most 0.1 Ω. The CircuitJS MOSFET has a parameter β, and the simulator's source defines the relation between β and the on-resistance: in the linear region, RDS(on) is 1 ÷ (β × (VGS − VGS(th))) (CircuitJS, MosfetElm.java, beta comment). The overdrive Vov is the gate voltage above the threshold. With the gate at about 3.3 V and a threshold of 2.0 V, Vov is about 1.27 V. For an on-resistance of 0.1 Ω, β must be at least 1 ÷ (0.1 × 1.27) = 7.9. The lab's solution uses β = 10, which gives margin.

The overdrive is the number that matters, not the gate voltage. A common slip is to put the gate voltage 3.3 V into the formula, which gives β = 1 ÷ (0.1 × 3.3) = 3.0, too small to meet the 0.1 Ω limit. The threshold is subtracted for a reason: below it the channel is off, and only the part of the gate voltage above it turns the channel on.

The load and the dissipation

The MOSFET is in series with the heater, so the same current flows through both. With the switch fully on, most of the 12 V drops across the heater, and only a little across the MOSFET. The current is set by the total resistance, 6 Ω plus the MOSFET's on-resistance, so I ≈ 12 V ÷ 6.08 Ω ≈ 1.97 A. The heater's power is 2 A × 12 V ≈ 24 W, which matches the subtitle. The MOSFET's power is its drain-source voltage times its current, VDS × ID. Its drain-source voltage is only about 0.17 V, so its dissipation is about 0.33 W. That is small. Using the junction-to-ambient figure from lesson 18, 0.33 W × 62 °C/W ≈ 20 °C of rise above the air on a bare board, which is a margin you can check on the bench rather than a heatsink you must add.

Requirements

  1. The gate is driven from a 3.3 V GPIO through a gate resistor of your choice, and a gate pull-down resistor is connected from the gate to ground.
  2. The load is a 6 Ω resistor in series with the MOSFET drain, supplied from a 12 V rail.
  3. The MOSFET is a logic-level N-channel device. You set its threshold voltage to the worst-case datasheet maximum, and choose its β so that its on-resistance at 3.3 V is at most 0.1 Ω.
  4. You measure the load current, the MOSFET drain-source voltage and the MOSFET's dissipation, and they meet the acceptance criteria below.
  5. You write one sentence for each component value, saying which datasheet number or calculation justifies it.

Reference values. The pin is at 3.3 V. The pull-down draws 33 µA through the 1 kΩ gate resistor, so the gate sits at 3.27 V, and Vov = 3.27 − 2.0 = 1.27 V. The MOSFET is in the triode region, so ID = β × (Vov × VDS − VDS² / 2) and 12 V = 6 Ω × ID + VDS. Solving those two equations with β = 10 gives VDS ≈ 0.17 V and ID ≈ 1.97 A. The dissipation is about 0.33 W, and the on-resistance at this bias is about 0.085 Ω, which meets requirement 3. If you ignore the pull-down's 33 mV, you get VDS ≈ 0.16 V and P ≈ 0.32 W, which is also inside the checks. Your own answer should state which of the two you used.

Hint: choosing β

The CircuitJS MOSFET model defines β = 1 ÷ (RDS(on) × Vov). Set RDS(on) to 0.1 Ω and Vov to the worst case you can reach. With the gate at about 3.27 V and the threshold at its maximum of 2.0 V, Vov is about 1.27 V, so β = 1 ÷ (0.1 × 1.27) = 7.9. Any β at or above 7.9 meets requirement 3, and β = 10 gives margin.

Hint: the gate resistor

At a 3.3 V step, the peak current into the gate is 3.3 V ÷ Rgate. For 4 mA, Rgate must be at least 825 Ω, so 1 kΩ gives 3.3 mA. In steady state the MOSFET draws no gate current, but the pull-down does, and its 33 µA flows through the gate resistor. That makes a 33 mV drop, which is small against the 3.3 V and must be included in the reference values.

Hint: the pull-down

A 100 kΩ pull-down draws 3.3 V ÷ 100 kΩ = 33 µA from a pin that is high, which is small against the 4 mA drive. It holds the gate low when the pin is high impedance, which is the case during reset or when a driver is disconnected.

Hint: why the gate needs no current

The gate is insulated. Once it is charged, it draws no DC current, so the gate voltage is the pin voltage less the pull-down's drop. The IRLZ44N datasheet gives Qg = 48 nC as a maximum at 5 V gate drive (Electrical Characteristics, Qg), so a 4 mA pin needs 48 nC ÷ 4 mA = 12 µs to switch the gate. That is fine for a heater, but too slow for fast PWM.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

The first mistake is to pick the gate resistor from the on-resistance, or to pick the pull-down so small that it loads the pin. A 100 Ω pull-down draws 33 mA from a 3.3 V pin, which is eight times the pin's default drive. The symptom is a pin that will not hold its level, and a gate that sits at a voltage the solution does not predict. The fix is the one in the lab: a 100 kΩ pull-down, which draws 33 µA.

The second mistake is to use the gate voltage instead of the overdrive in the β formula. The result is β = 3.0. With the true overdrive of 1.27 V, the on-resistance is 1 ÷ (3.0 × 1.27) ≈ 0.26 Ω, more than twice the 0.1 Ω limit. The symptom is a drain voltage near 0.68 V, not 0.17 V, and a MOSFET dissipation near 1.3 W, not 0.33 W. The load current drops to about 1.9 A, and the MOSFET runs hot. The fix is to subtract the threshold first.

The third mistake is to check only one end of the threshold range. The lab sets the threshold to its maximum, 2.0 V, because that is the worst case for the overdrive. A designer who uses the typical value, or the minimum of 1.0 V, gets a larger overdrive and a lower on-resistance, and the design looks comfortable. A part that sits at the maximum may then be only marginally on. The symptom is a switch that behaves on one sample and not another. The fix is to calculate at both ends of the threshold range, and to size the design for the worse end.

The fourth mistake is to leave out the flyback diode in the stretch version. A 6 Ω heater is resistive, so it does not spike. A coil is inductive, and switching it off forces a voltage spike that can destroy the MOSFET, as lesson 14 showed. The symptom is a switch that fails on the first turn-off, not during the steady-state test. The fix is the diode across the load, with its cathode on the supply side.

In real projects

Every driver in a product is a small table like the one in this lab: the source voltage, the gate resistor and why, the pull-down and why, the MOSFET's threshold and on-resistance at the gate voltage in use, the load current, the dissipation, and the margin of each against the datasheet. A design review asks for that table before it asks for a schematic. It also asks what happens at the other end of each range, such as the coldest threshold, the hottest case and the lowest supply.

Interviewers ask this kind of question directly, and a good answer walks through the same chain: why low side, why logic level, why this gate resistor, why this pull-down, how the threshold and the on-resistance set the dissipation, and how each number was confirmed. The lab asks you to write those sentences, because the sentences are what a reviewer reads first.

Summary

  • A low-side N-channel MOSFET can be driven straight from a 3.3 V pin if it is a logic-level part with an on-resistance specified at that gate voltage.
  • The gate resistor limits the peak pin current to at most 4 mA, and at 3.3 V that needs at least 825 Ω.
  • The pull-down holds the gate low, and its current flows through the gate resistor, so the gate sits a few tens of millivolts below the pin.
  • On-resistance is 1 ÷ (β × Vov) in the simulator's linear region, and the overdrive is the gate voltage minus the threshold.
  • With the switch on, the load carries about 2 A, and the MOSFET drops about 0.17 V and dissipates about 0.33 W.
