Power supplies: linear regulators, buck converters, and batteries

Your board needs a steady 3.3 V from a 5 V USB port or from a battery that slowly runs down. The regulator you choose decides how much of the energy you pay for turns into heat.

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the heat in a linear regulator from (Vin − Vout) × I, compare its efficiency with a buck converter, and check that a battery stays above the regulator's dropout.

Where we are, and why power matters

Up to now the course has treated voltage as something you set and current as something you measure. Lesson 6 showed that a resistor turns electrical power into heat, and lesson 8 showed how a voltage divider sets a voltage with two resistors. Both of those ideas return here, because every board needs a power supply, and the supply is itself a circuit that wastes energy. Lesson 14 introduced the inductor, which stores energy in a magnetic field, and that is the part that lets a switching supply avoid most of the waste.

The problem this lesson addresses is practical. A Raspberry Pi Pico has a USB connector that gives about 5 V, but its RP2040 chip needs 3.3 V on its input and output pins. A product that runs from a single lithium cell has a voltage that falls as the cell empties. A motor controller or heater board often takes 12 V or 24 V from its supply and must make a 5 V or 3.3 V rail from that. In each case a regulator sits between the source and the chip, and choosing the wrong type either wastes most of the input power or lets the chip reset when the battery sags.

The vocabulary of power

A rail is a supply net that feeds several parts, such as the 3.3 V rail on a board. The regulator is the part that holds a rail at its target voltage while the input and the load change. The input voltage (Vin) is what the regulator receives, and the output voltage (Vout) is what it delivers to the rail. The load current (Iout) is the current the rail's parts draw. Every regulator has to take its input current (Iin) from the source, and every watt of that input power either reaches the load or is lost inside the regulator.

Power is the rate of energy use, measured in watts (W), and for a voltage across a path with a current through it, P = V × I. The power you lose is the difference between the power going in and the power coming out, and it leaves the part as heat. That is the link to lesson 6: a regulator is a heater that also happens to hold a voltage steady.

The linear regulator: the heat is the difference

A linear regulator holds its output by dropping the extra voltage across a pass element, a transistor in series with the load. A control loop watches the output and changes the pass element's resistance to keep the output at its target. The control loop is the reason the output stays steady, and the pass element is the reason the regulator gets hot. The current through the pass element is the load current, so the regulator carries the full load current through a voltage drop of Vin − Vout.

That gives the power the regulator loses: Ploss = (Vin − Vout) × Iout. The input current is almost the same as the output current, because the control circuit takes only a tiny extra current of its own. So the input power is about Vin × Iout, and the output power is Vout × Iout. The ratio of output power to input power is the efficiency, and it comes out to about Vout ÷ Vin. The efficiency depends only on the voltages, not on the load.

Dropout: how close the input may come to the output

The pass element is a transistor, and a transistor needs some voltage across it to conduct the current the load requires. The dropout voltage is the smallest difference between input and output for which the regulator still regulates. Below it, the pass element runs out of headroom, and the output simply follows the input down. A regulator with a dropout of 0.3 V (an illustrative value, not from a particular part) needs at least 3.6 V in to hold 3.3 V out. The datasheet of the actual part gives its own dropout, and that number is the one to use.

The buck converter: switch, store, deliver

A buck converter does not drop the extra voltage. It switches the input on and off very fast, and stores the energy in an inductor and a capacitor, so the output receives energy in short packets instead of a steady flow through a resistive drop. A MOSFET (lesson 12) is the switch. When the switch is on, the input charges the inductor, and the inductor current rises. When the switch is off, the inductor keeps the current going into the output, and the current falls. The capacitor smooths the result into a steady voltage.

The inductor is what makes this work. Lesson 14 showed that an inductor resists a change in its current, and that the energy it stores is ½ × L × I². Because the buck converter moves energy in packets and the inductor carries the current between packets, the switch is either fully on or fully off most of the time. A fully on MOSFET has a tiny voltage across it, and a fully off MOSFET has almost no current through it, so neither state wastes much power.

The duty cycle D is the fraction of each switching period that the switch is on. In an ideal buck converter with no losses, the inductor's average voltage over a full period must be zero, or its current would climb forever. While the switch is on, the inductor sees Vin − Vout. While it is off, the inductor sees −Vout. Setting D × (Vin − Vout) equal to (1 − D) × Vout and solving gives D = Vout ÷ Vin. This is an ideal result. A real converter needs a slightly larger duty cycle to make up for its losses.

A real buck converter wastes some energy too. The TI application report on DC/DC converter losses names three main causes: inductor conduction losses, MOSFET conduction losses and MOSFET switching losses (TI SLVA390, introduction, "The three main causes of power dissipation in a DC/DC converter"). Each is much smaller than the (Vin − Vout) × I term of a linear regulator at a large voltage difference. The price is noise, more parts, and more careful board layout. A switching regulator on a board can radiate noise into a sensitive analogue input, which is why its layout matters as much as its choice.

Buck-boost: the converter that also steps up

A buck-boost converter can produce an output that is lower or higher than its input. It works in buck mode when the input is high and in boost mode when the input is low, and it moves smoothly between the two. This is the regulator on the Pico. The Pico datasheet says that its on-board switching regulator generates 3.3 V from an input range of about 1.8 V to 5.5 V, and that it can switch seamlessly between buck and boost (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, power chain section). That is why a Pico runs from one lithium cell as the cell falls from 4.2 V towards 3 V, and from 5 V USB, without any change to the wiring.

Batteries and the voltage that falls

A single lithium-ion cell has a nominal voltage of 3.6 V. The Panasonic CGR18650CH datasheet gives a charge limit of 4.2 V at constant voltage, and a discharge end point of 3.0 V at a discharge current of 430 mA at 25 °C (Panasonic CGR18650CH specification, specifications table; this copy is hosted by a third party, so check the manufacturer's own copy). The cell's voltage is therefore not fixed. It starts near 4.2 V, sits near 3.6 V for most of its life, and falls quickly at the end.

The Pico datasheet gives the input range of its buck-boost regulator, VSYS, as 1.8 V to 5.5 V, and it names a single lithium-ion cell as a valid source (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, VSYS description). A linear regulator from the same cell would lose its output once the cell fell to the output voltage plus the dropout. A buck-boost regulator keeps the output steady across the whole range, so the board runs until the cell is nearly flat.

What the Pico asks of its supply

The Pico datasheet gives three numbers that every power design on a Pico has to respect. VBUS, the USB input, is nominally 5 V. VSYS, the main system input, accepts 1.8 V to 5.5 V. The 3V3 output, which the Pico uses for its own chip and can also power external parts, should carry no more than about 300 mA, and the datasheet says that the actual limit depends on the RP2040 load and on VSYS (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, power pin descriptions). The RP2040 itself does not run from 3.3 V directly. Its digital core runs at 1.1 V, from an on-chip linear regulator that is fed from the 3.3 V supply (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, powerchain section).

The chip's own limit matters too. The RP2040 datasheet gives an I/O supply (IOVDD) absolute maximum of 3.63 V (RP2040 datasheet, Table 622, absolute maximum ratings for digital IO). A supply that spikes above that, from a battery charger or a bad connection, can damage the chip even if its average is correct.

A worked example: 12 V to 3.3 V at 0.2 A

Take a 12 V supply feeding a 3.3 V rail that powers a sensor and a microcontroller drawing 0.2 A. Start with the output power: Pout = 3.3 V × 0.2 A = 0.66 W. That is the power that does useful work.

Now the linear regulator. Its input current equals its output current, 0.2 A, so the input power is Pin = 12 V × 0.2 A = 2.4 W. The regulator must lose the difference: 2.4 W − 0.66 W = 1.74 W. The same answer comes from the loss formula, (12 − 3.3) V × 0.2 A = 8.7 V × 0.2 A = 1.74 W. The efficiency is 0.66 ÷ 2.4 = 27.5 %, which matches the rule of thumb Vout ÷ Vin = 3.3 ÷ 12 = 27.5 %.

Double the load to 0.5 A and each term scales. Pout = 1.65 W, Pin = 6 W, and the loss is 4.35 W. The efficiency has not changed, because it depends only on the voltages. A 4.35 W loss in a small regulator is a serious heat source, which is why a linear regulator is a poor choice at this voltage difference.

For comparison, suppose a buck converter at this load runs at 85 % efficiency. This is an assumed figure for illustration, not a datasheet value, so check the real part's efficiency curve. At 0.2 A the input power is 0.66 W ÷ 0.85 = 0.78 W, and the loss is about 0.12 W. The buck converter wastes about one fifteenth of what the linear regulator wastes, at the same output. That is the main reason battery products use switching regulators.

Finally, the battery case. Suppose the 3.3 V linear regulator has the 0.3 V dropout from above, and it runs from one cell. The regulator needs 3.6 V in to hold 3.3 V out. A nominal cell at 3.6 V has no margin at all, and the cell is below 3.6 V for most of its discharge. The regulator would drop out long before the cell is flat, and the board would reset with the battery still holding charge. A buck-boost regulator that accepts the cell's whole range avoids that.

The divider gives Vout = Vin × Rbottom ÷ (Rtop + Rbottom). Check that your output reads 5 V before you measure the current. If the output is wrong, one resistor probably still has its old value.

You measured the voltage and current in a series drop, and worked out the heat that a linear regulator wastes at a given load. That is the calculation you need to choose between a linear regulator and a buck converter.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

The first mistake is to size a linear regulator by its output power alone. A designer looks at 0.2 A at 3.3 V and sees a small load, then forgets that the input power is three and a half times larger at 12 V in. The symptom is a regulator that gets hot to the touch on a bench, or a battery that is flat far sooner than the energy budget predicts. The fix is to compute Pin = Vin × I and then Ploss = Pin − Pout before choosing the part. Check the power dissipation rating on the regulator's datasheet, and compare it with the loss at your worst-case input voltage.

The second mistake is to ignore dropout on a battery. The board works on the bench from a bench supply at 5 V, then resets in the field when the cell sags. The symptom is a reset that happens at a specific battery level, not at random, and the 3.3 V rail reads low on a meter as the battery falls. The fix is to write down the regulator's minimum input (its output plus dropout) and the cell's end voltage, and to check that the cell never falls below the minimum. If it does, use a buck-boost regulator, or a linear regulator with a lower dropout and a higher-voltage pack.

The third mistake is to load the Pico's 3V3 pin without a budget. Someone wires a sensor, a display backlight and a relay to 3V3 and sees the board reset when the relay clicks. The symptom is a brownout that appears only with the external load, and the RP2040 may reset because its supply sags. The fix is to add up the external load and compare it with the datasheet's recommendation of about 300 mA, then power the heavy parts from a separate regulator.

The fourth mistake is to treat the buck converter as free of noise. Its output is steady on average, but the switching frequency is a ripple on top of it. The symptom is an ADC reading that wanders in step with the converter, or a radio that drops packets whenever the converter switches. The fix is to add output filtering, keep the switch loop small on the board, and check the ripple with an oscilloscope, which is the instrument from lesson 10.

In real projects

A professional power budget is kept as a table. Each rail lists its source, its nominal voltage, its minimum and maximum, the load of each part on the rail and the total, and the regulator that makes it with its efficiency at that load. A reviewer checks three things: the worst-case input voltage against the regulator's minimum, the total load against the regulator's current rating, and the power loss against the heat that the board can shed. A rail without a row in that table has not been checked.

On the Pico itself, the choice is already made for you, and the datasheet tells you what to respect: VSYS between 1.8 V and 5.5 V, VBUS nominally 5 V, and a 3V3 external load of about 300 mA. If you build a Pico into a product, the power table is the first thing you write, before the schematic.

Summary

  • A linear regulator's loss is (Vin − Vout) × Iout, and its efficiency is about Vout ÷ Vin, whatever the load.
  • A buck converter switches a MOSFET into an inductor and capacitor, and its ideal duty cycle is D = Vout ÷ Vin.
  • The main losses in a buck converter are inductor conduction, MOSFET conduction and MOSFET switching.
  • A buck-boost converter steps the voltage up or down, which is why the Pico accepts 1.8 V to 5.5 V on VSYS.
  • A single lithium cell is 3.6 V nominal, 4.2 V at full charge, and about 3.0 V at the end of its discharge at 430 mA.
  • Dropout is the minimum headroom a linear regulator needs, and a battery must stay above output plus dropout.
  • On the Pico, respect the datasheet limits: VSYS 1.8 V to 5.5 V, 3V3 external load below about 300 mA, and IOVDD no higher than 3.63 V.
You can now size a rail on paper: input power, output power, loss, efficiency, and the battery's margin over dropout.
