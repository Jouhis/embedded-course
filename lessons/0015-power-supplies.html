Power supplies

Linear regulators, buck converters and batteries

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the heat in a linear regulator from (Vin − Vout) × I, compare its efficiency with a buck converter, and check that a battery stays above the regulator's dropout.

Supply rails for a microcontroller board

So far the course has treated voltage as something you set and current as something you measure. Lesson 6 showed that a resistor turns electrical power into heat, and lesson 8 that a divider sets a voltage with two resistors. Both ideas return here, because every board needs a power supply, and the supply is itself a circuit that wastes energy. Lesson 14 introduced the inductor, which stores energy in a magnetic field, and that is the part that lets a switching supply avoid most of the waste.

The problem is a practical one. A Raspberry Pi Pico's USB connector supplies about 5 V, while its RP2040 chip needs 3.3 V on its input and output pins. A product running from a single lithium cell sees its voltage fall as the cell empties, and a motor controller or heater board often takes 12 V or 24 V and must make a 5 V or 3.3 V rail from it. In each case a regulator sits between the source and the chip, and choosing the wrong type either wastes most of the input power or lets the chip reset when the battery sags.

Rails, regulators and power loss

A rail is a supply net that feeds several parts, such as the 3.3 V rail on a board, and a regulator holds a rail at its target voltage while the input and the load change. The regulator receives an input voltage Vin and delivers an output voltage Vout, while the parts on the rail draw a load current Iout. The regulator in turn draws an input current Iin from the source, and every watt of that input power either reaches the load or is lost inside the regulator.

Power is the rate of energy use, measured in watts (W), and for a voltage across a path carrying a current it is P = V × I. The power lost is the difference between the power going in and the power coming out, and it leaves the part as heat. This links back to lesson 6, since every regulator is partly a heater as well as a voltage source.

Linear regulators

A linear regulator holds its output by dropping the excess voltage across a pass element, a transistor in series with the load. A control loop watches the output and adjusts the pass element's resistance to keep the output at its target. The loop is what keeps the output steady, and the pass element is what makes the regulator hot, because it carries the full load current through a voltage drop of Vin − Vout.

The power the regulator loses is therefore

Ploss = (Vin − Vout) × Iout(1)

where Iout is the load current. The input current is almost equal to the output current, since the control circuit takes only a tiny current of its own, so the input power is about Vin × Iout and the output power Vout × Iout. Their ratio is the efficiency,

η ≈ Vout ÷ Vin(2)

where η is the fraction of input power that reaches the load. A linear regulator's efficiency thus depends only on the voltages and not on the load.

Dropout voltage

Because the pass element is a transistor, it needs some voltage across it to conduct the current the load requires. The dropout voltage is the smallest input-to-output difference at which the regulator still regulates; below it, the pass element runs out of headroom and the output simply follows the input down. A regulator with a dropout of 0.3 V, an illustrative value rather than one from a particular part, needs at least 3.6 V in to hold 3.3 V out. The datasheet of the actual part gives its own dropout, and that is the number to use.

Buck converters

A buck converter avoids dropping the excess voltage. It switches the input on and off very quickly and stores the energy in an inductor and a capacitor, so the output receives energy in short packets rather than as a steady flow through a resistive drop. The switch is a MOSFET from lesson 12. While it is on, the input drives current into the inductor and that current rises; while it is off, the inductor keeps the current flowing into the output and it falls. The capacitor smooths the result into a steady voltage.

The inductor is what makes this work. Lesson 14 showed that an inductor resists a change in its current and stores energy as ½ × L × I², so it can carry the current between packets while the switch spends nearly all its time fully on or fully off. A fully on MOSFET has a tiny voltage across it and a fully off one passes almost no current, so neither state wastes much power.

The duty cycle D is the fraction of each switching period during which the switch is on. In an ideal, lossless buck converter the inductor's average voltage over a period must be zero, or its current would climb without limit. While the switch is on the inductor sees Vin − Vout, and while it is off it sees −Vout, so setting D × (Vin − Vout) equal to (1 − D) × Vout gives

D = Vout ÷ Vin(3)

where D lies between 0 and 1. This is an ideal result, and a real converter needs a slightly larger duty cycle to make up for its losses.

Those losses exist, although they are small. The TI application report on DC/DC converter losses names three main causes, namely inductor conduction losses, MOSFET conduction losses and MOSFET switching losses (TI SLVA390, introduction, "The three main causes of power dissipation in a DC/DC converter"). Each is much smaller than the term in Equation 1 when the voltage difference is large. The price is noise, more parts and more careful board layout, since a switching regulator can radiate noise into a sensitive analogue input, which makes its layout as important as its selection.

A linear regulator resembles holding a car at a steady speed downhill with the brakes: it works, and the unwanted energy turns into heat in the brakes. A buck converter resembles changing gear, trading voltage for current so that most of the energy reaches the load. The gearbox brings more parts and more noise, which is why boards still use a quiet linear regulator where the drop and the current are small. The comparison breaks down on timing, since a gearbox changes ratio occasionally while a buck converter switches hundreds of thousands of times a second.

Buck-boost converters

A buck-boost converter can produce an output either lower or higher than its input, working in buck mode when the input is high and in boost mode when it is low, and moving smoothly between the two. The Pico uses one. Its datasheet states that the on-board switching regulator generates 3.3 V from an input range of about 1.8 V to 5.5 V and switches seamlessly between buck and boost (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, power chain section). That is why a Pico runs both from 5 V USB and from a single lithium cell falling from 4.2 V towards 3 V, without any change to the wiring.

Lithium cells and falling battery voltage

A single lithium-ion cell has a nominal voltage of 3.6 V. The Panasonic CGR18650CH datasheet gives a charge limit of 4.2 V at constant voltage and a discharge end point of 3.0 V at a discharge current of 430 mA at 25 °C (Panasonic CGR18650CH specification, specifications table; this copy is hosted by a third party, so check the manufacturer's own copy). The cell's voltage is therefore far from fixed: it starts near 4.2 V, sits near 3.6 V for most of the discharge and falls quickly at the end.

The Pico datasheet gives the input range of its buck-boost regulator, VSYS, as 1.8 V to 5.5 V, and names a single lithium-ion cell as a valid source (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, VSYS description). A linear regulator on the same cell would lose its output once the cell fell to the output voltage plus the dropout, whereas the buck-boost regulator holds the output across the whole range, so the board keeps running until the cell is nearly flat.

Pico supply pins and limits

The Pico datasheet gives three numbers that every Pico power design must respect. VBUS, the USB input, is nominally 5 V, and VSYS, the main system input, accepts 1.8 V to 5.5 V. The 3V3 output, which powers the Pico's own chip and can also feed external parts, should carry no more than about 300 mA, and the datasheet notes that the actual limit depends on the RP2040 load and on VSYS (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, power pin descriptions). The RP2040 itself does not run its core from 3.3 V; its digital core runs at 1.1 V from an on-chip linear regulator fed by the 3.3 V supply (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, powerchain section).

The chip's own limit matters as well. The RP2040 datasheet gives an absolute maximum of 3.63 V for the I/O supply, IOVDD (RP2040 datasheet, Table 622, absolute maximum ratings for digital IO). A supply that spikes above it, from a battery charger or a poor connection, can damage the chip even when its average voltage is correct.

Worked example: 12 V to 3.3 V at 0.2 A

A 12 V supply feeds a 3.3 V rail powering a sensor and a microcontroller that together draw 0.2 A. We start with the useful output power, Pout = 3.3 V × 0.2 A = 0.66 W, and then compare what each regulator type needs at its input to deliver it.

For the linear regulator, the input current equals the output current, so the input power is Pin = 12 V × 0.2 A = 2.4 W and the regulator loses 2.4 W − 0.66 W = 1.74 W. Equation 1 gives the same answer, (12 − 3.3) V × 0.2 A = 8.7 V × 0.2 A = 1.74 W, and the efficiency is 0.66 ÷ 2.4 = 27.5 %, matching Equation 2's 3.3 ÷ 12.

Doubling the load to 0.5 A scales every term: Pout = 1.65 W, Pin = 6 W and the loss is 4.35 W. The efficiency is unchanged because it depends only on the voltages, but 4.35 W in a small regulator is a serious heat source, which makes a linear regulator a poor choice at this voltage difference.

For comparison, suppose a buck converter runs at 85 % efficiency at this load. That figure is assumed for illustration rather than taken from a datasheet, so check the real part's efficiency curve. At 0.2 A the input power is 0.66 W ÷ 0.85 = 0.78 W and the loss about 0.12 W, roughly one fifteenth of the linear regulator's loss at the same output, which is the main reason battery products use switching regulators.

Finally, consider the battery. Suppose the 3.3 V linear regulator has the 0.3 V dropout from earlier and runs from one cell, so it needs 3.6 V in to hold its output. A nominal cell at 3.6 V leaves no margin, and the cell is below 3.6 V for most of its discharge, so the regulator would drop out long before the cell was flat and the board would reset with charge still left. A buck-boost regulator that accepts the cell's whole range avoids this.

  1. The load needs 3.3 V × 0.2 A = 0.66 W. That is the useful power, the same for either regulator.
  2. A linear regulator's input current equals its output current, so it takes 12 V × 0.2 A = 2.4 W from the supply.
  3. It burns the difference as heat: (12 − 3.3) V × 0.2 A = 1.74 W. Its efficiency is 0.66 ÷ 2.4 = 27.5 %, which is just Vout ÷ Vin.
  4. A buck converter, at an assumed 85 %, takes 0.66 ÷ 0.85 = 0.78 W and loses about 0.12 W.
  5. Same output, one fifteenth of the heat. That is why battery products and big voltage drops use switching regulators.

  2. Read the output voltage at the probe between the resistors. Expect 5.0 V, to one decimal place.
  3. Predict the current, then read it through the bottom resistor. Expect 1.0 mA, or 12 V ÷ (7 kΩ + 5 kΩ), the same everywhere in the circuit because there is only one path.

Hint

The divider gives Vout = Vin × Rbottom ÷ (Rtop + Rbottom). Check that the output reads 5 V before measuring the current, and if it is wrong, check that no resistor value was changed by accident.

You should measure about 5 V at the probe and about 1 mA through the circuit, which puts 7 V × 1 mA = 7 mW in the top resistor. The bottom resistor, standing in for the load, receives 5 mW and the supply delivers 12 mW, so the difference is the heat a linear regulator would have to dissipate. The ratio is what matters: 7 of every 12 milliwatts become heat, and at a 100 mA load the same ratio means 0.7 W. If you see 12 mW from the supply and 5 mW in the load, your numbers agree with Equation 1.

You measured the voltage and current in a series drop and worked out the heat a linear regulator wastes at a given load, which is the calculation behind choosing between a linear regulator and a buck converter. You can now size a rail on paper: input power, output power, loss, efficiency and the battery's margin over dropout.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

A linear regulator sized by its output power alone

A load of 0.2 A at 3.3 V looks small, but with 12 V in, the input power is three and a half times larger. The regulator gets hot to the touch on the bench, or a battery goes flat far sooner than the energy budget predicted. Compute Pin = Vin × I and then Ploss = Pin − Pout before choosing the part, and compare the loss at the worst-case input voltage with the power dissipation rating in the regulator's datasheet.

Dropout ignored on a battery

The board works on the bench from a 5 V supply and then resets in the field as the cell sags. The reset happens at a specific battery level rather than at random, and a meter shows the 3.3 V rail reading low as the battery falls. Write down the regulator's minimum input, its output plus dropout, alongside the cell's end voltage, and check that the cell never falls below that minimum; if it does, use a buck-boost regulator or a lower-dropout regulator with a higher-voltage pack.

The Pico's 3V3 pin loaded without a budget

A sensor, a display backlight and a relay are all wired to 3V3, and the board resets when the relay clicks. The brownout appears only with the external load connected, because the RP2040's supply sags. Add up the external load, compare it with the datasheet's recommendation of about 300 mA, and power the heavy parts from a separate regulator.

A buck converter treated as noise-free

The output is steady on average, but the switching frequency rides on top of it as ripple. An ADC reading wanders in step with the converter, or a radio drops packets whenever it switches. Add output filtering, keep the switching loop small on the board, and check the ripple with an oscilloscope, the instrument from lesson 10.

Power budgets in real projects

A professional power budget is kept as a table. Each rail lists its source, its nominal, minimum and maximum voltage, the load of each part on the rail and the total, and the regulator that produces it with its efficiency at that load. A reviewer checks the worst-case input voltage against the regulator's minimum, the total load against the regulator's current rating, and the power loss against the heat the board can shed. A rail without a row in that table has not been checked.

On the Pico itself the choice has already been made, and the datasheet states what to respect: VSYS between 1.8 V and 5.5 V, VBUS nominally 5 V and a 3V3 external load of about 300 mA. When a Pico goes into a product, the power table is the first thing to write, before the schematic.

Summary

  • A linear regulator's loss is (Vin − Vout) × Iout, and its efficiency is about Vout ÷ Vin whatever the load.
  • A buck converter switches a MOSFET into an inductor and a capacitor, and its ideal duty cycle is D = Vout ÷ Vin.
  • The main losses in a buck converter are inductor conduction, MOSFET conduction and MOSFET switching.
  • A buck-boost converter steps the voltage up or down, which is why the Pico accepts 1.8 V to 5.5 V on VSYS.
  • A single lithium cell is 3.6 V nominal, 4.2 V at full charge and about 3.0 V at the end of its discharge at 430 mA.
  • Dropout is the minimum headroom a linear regulator needs, so a battery must stay above the output plus the dropout.
  • On the Pico, respect the datasheet limits: VSYS 1.8 V to 5.5 V, an external 3V3 load below about 300 mA, and IOVDD no higher than 3.63 V.
