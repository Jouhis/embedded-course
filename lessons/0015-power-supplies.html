Power supplies: linear regulators, buck converters, and batteries

Your board needs a steady 3.3 V from a 5 V USB port or a battery that is slowly running down. The regulator you choose decides how much of the rest turns into heat.

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the heat in a linear regulator from (Vin − Vout) × I, compare its efficiency with a buck converter, and check that a battery stays above the regulator's dropout.

The idea

A regulator turns a changing input into a fixed output. A microcontroller such as the RP2040 needs its digital IO supply in the range 1.8 V to 3.3 V, and the Pico board runs its core from a 3.3 V rail (Pico datasheet, section on power: the input is nominally 5 V on VBUS and 1.8 to 5.5 V on VSYS). The power supply is part of the same design problem as the MOSFET and the load. Current flows from the source through the regulator to the load, and every watt that is not delivered becomes heat.

Linear regulators: the heat is the difference

A linear regulator drops the extra voltage across a pass element, which carries the full load current. Its power loss is therefore Ploss = (Vin − Vout) × Iout. Its efficiency is about Vout ÷ Vin. With 12 V in and 3.3 V out, the efficiency is 3.3 ÷ 12 = 27.5 %, and the rest is heat. The Analog Devices application note AN-140 gives both formulas and this same 27.5 % example (AN-140, Basic Concepts of Linear Regulator and Switching Mode Power Supplies, section "One Drawback"). Heat sets the limit. Check the regulator's maximum power dissipation on its datasheet, and check the heat your load actually creates.

Worked numbers: a 12 V to 3.3 V linear regulator feeding a 0.2 A load wastes (12 − 3.3) × 0.2 = 1.74 W. A 0.5 A load wastes 4.35 W. A linear regulator's dropout voltage is the smallest difference between input and output for which it still regulates. Below that, the output follows the input down.

Buck converters: switch, store, deliver

A buck converter switches a MOSFET on and off at a high frequency and stores the energy in an inductor and capacitor. Its losses are mostly the switching and conduction losses in the MOSFET and the losses in the inductor, not the voltage difference times the current, so it wastes far less heat (TI application report SLVA390 lists the main buck loss sources). The trade-off is noise, a larger part count and more layout care. The Pico board uses a buck-boost switching supply to make its 3.3 V from a wide input range (Pico datasheet, power section).

Batteries and dropout

A single lithium-ion cell is nominally 3.6 V, it charges to about 4.2 V, and it is usually considered empty somewhere between 2.5 V and 3.0 V, depending on the cell (Panasonic CGR18650CH 18650 datasheet, as mirrored here: check the manufacturer's own copy). A linear regulator from a single cell therefore loses regulation when the battery falls to its dropout voltage above the output. A buck or boost converter keeps the output steady across that range, so it lasts longer on the same cell.

Hint

The divider gives Vout = Vin × Rbottom ÷ (Rtop + Rbottom). Check that your output reads 5 V before you measure the current. If the output is wrong, one resistor probably still has its old value.

You should measure about 5 V at the probe and about 0.1 A through the circuit, which puts 0.7 W in the top resistor. The load gets 0.5 W and the input is 1.2 W, so the difference is the heat that a linear regulator would have to dissipate.

You measured the voltage and current in a series drop, and worked out the heat that a linear regulator wastes at a given load. That is the calculation you need to choose between a linear regulator and a buck converter.

"Your board runs from USB but resets when it runs on a single lithium cell. What do you check?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

I would measure the regulator's input and output under load on a scope, as the battery falls. A linear regulator stops regulating when the input drops to its output plus dropout, and the 3.3 V rail then sags and the chip resets. I would compare the cell's voltage range with the regulator's minimum input, and if the cell can drop below it, I would switch to a buck-boost converter that holds the output across that range. I would also check that the cell can supply the peak current without its voltage collapsing.
