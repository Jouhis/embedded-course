Functional safety

Integrity levels, safety mechanisms and evidence

By the end of this lesson you can explain what SIL and ASIL measure, sketch the V-model, name three firmware safety mechanisms and what each detects, and run a March C- RAM test that finds a stuck-at bit.

Reliability tools and the question of how much

The earlier lessons gave you the tools a safety-critical product needs, interrupts and timers from lesson 57, the watchdog and HardFault analysis from lesson 84, the static analysis and MISRA C rules from lesson 83 and the unit tests with fakes from lessons 80 and 81, each making the firmware more reliable, although none of them on its own says how reliable the product must be or how anyone would prove it.

That question comes up on a real product, because a brake controller, a steering motor, an airbag squib driver, a medical pump or the thermal cut-off on a 3D printer's heater can each cause harm when it fails, so a company selling such a product must show a regulator or a customer that the chance of that harm is acceptably small and how it got there. Functional safety is the discipline of deciding how much that matters and then building and proving firmware that is safe enough, and this lesson introduces its vocabulary one idea at a time before building one of its firmware mechanisms.

Failures and hazards

Reliability asks how often a system fails, whereas functional safety asks a narrower question, whether a failure can hurt someone and how often that harmful failure would happen, so a thermostat that stops working is annoying, while a heater that keeps heating when it should stop is a hazard, because the room can catch fire. Both are failures, but only the second needs the safety machinery.

A dead brake light is a failure that becomes a hazard only when someone is behind you, at night, as you brake, so safety work starts from the hazard, the situation that can hurt someone, and asks which failures can lead to it, which is why the same fault can be harmless in one product and serious in another. The comparison breaks for detection, though, because a driver can notice a dead brake light on a walk round the car, whereas a failed bit inside a controller gives no visible sign, so the firmware has to detect it itself.

The formal term for the harm is a functional safety concern, and the standard covering electrical, electronic and programmable electronic systems in general is IEC 61508, the automotive standard built on it being ISO 26262, which adapts the same ideas to road vehicles. Both are paid documents, but a free overview from Freescale, now part of NXP, summarises them in a white paper titled Addressing the Challenges of Functional Safety in the Automotive and Industrial Markets (document FCTNLSFTYWP, revision 1, 2011 and 2013), so read the overview for the vocabulary and then the standards themselves. The white paper is a vendor document from 2011 and 2013, so use its numbers as an overview and check the current standards before you rely on a level.

Integrity levels, SIL and ASIL

Once a harmful failure is identified, the standards assign it an integrity level, a target for how rarely that failure may happen, rising with the severity of the harm, IEC 61508 defining four Safety Integrity Levels (SIL) from SIL 1 to SIL 4, with SIL 4 the most stringent, and ISO 26262 four Automotive Safety Integrity Levels (ASIL) from ASIL A to ASIL D, with ASIL D the most stringent.

The two scales differ, the white paper stating that there is no direct correlation between SIL and ASIL levels, so an ASIL D function is not automatically a SIL 4 function, and you should keep the scales apart in your notes. The white paper's table gives the random hardware failure targets, for SIL 4 a rate from 10−9 up to but not including 10−8 per hour and for ASIL D below 10−8 per hour Freescale (NXP) white paper FCTNLSFTYWP, failure measures table.

A level is a target rather than a property of the chip, a higher level meaning more evidence, more reviews, more independent checks, more traceability and more testing of the safety mechanisms, so buying a chip with a safety label does not by itself make the product meet a level.

The V-model

Safety standards follow a lifecycle shaped like a V, the left side going down from the requirements to the system architecture, the software design and the code, and the right side going back up, testing each level against the matching requirement, unit tests against the design, integration tests against the architecture and system tests against the requirements, each arm having its own evidence, which the safety case collects.

The V is useful because it makes the tests a planned output of the design, so a requirement without a test on the right side is a gap a reviewer will find. The model is general practice, the standards spelling out the steps in detail, and the details differ between IEC 61508 and ISO 26262.

Hazard analysis and risk assessment

The hazard analysis and risk assessment (HARA) starts before any design, listing for each function the ways it can fail harmfully and then rating three things for each hazard, the severity of the harm, how often the situation occurs (exposure) and how controllable it is by the driver or operator, ratings that give the ASIL, or in IEC 61508 the SIL, while the glossary entry for HARA gives the short form.

The heater makes a concrete case, one hazard being that it keeps heating when the controller asks it to stop, whose severity is high, because a heater can start a fire, whose exposure depends on how often the printer runs unattended and whose controllability is low if the user cannot see the heater working. Those three ratings point to a high integrity level and so to more than one independent protection, the rating setting how much evidence the design must carry.

Faults, safe states and safety mechanisms

Three terms come up in every safety discussion, a stuck-at fault being a bit or line that always reads 0 or always reads 1 whatever was written, a safe state the state the system moves to when a fault is detected, chosen so that no harm results, such as the heater off or the brake applied, and a safety mechanism the piece of hardware or software that detects the fault and moves the system to the safe state.

The safe state depends on the product. For a train it is standing still, so a fault applies the brakes. For an aircraft in flight, "everything off" is the most dangerous choice, so its systems must keep working when faulty. For the dryer in the capstone (lesson 113), the safe state is the heater off with the fan running so the part cools, which is why a fault turns off only the heater.

Three mechanisms are common on a small MCU, each detecting a different class of fault, so a product usually uses more than one.

  • Watchdog. A timer that resets the chip unless the code kicks it on time. It detects a program that has stopped making progress. Lesson 84 covers the independent and window watchdogs, which are different on the STM32.
  • RAM and flash self-tests. A March test writes and reads patterns over every byte of RAM, and detects stuck-at bits, which is the case you build below. A CRC over the flash image, computed at startup and compared with a stored value, detects a corrupted program. The CRC is the same idea as the checksums in lesson 103.
  • Plausibility checks. A plausibility check tests whether a value is physically possible, given its last value and the physics of the process. A heater's temperature cannot rise 40 °C in one second; a thermistor reading of 0 counts is a broken wire. The check runs on the sensor data, not on the hardware.

Redundancy, two channels that must agree, and a safe state complete the picture, coding rules such as MISRA C (lesson 83) reducing the faults you put in while the mechanisms above catch the faults that remain. Hardware and software both have to be covered, and a mechanism that only watches another mechanism is not enough on its own.

The safety case

A safety case is the argument, with evidence, that the system is acceptably safe, naming the hazards, the safety goals, the mechanisms meeting each goal and the tests showing that the mechanisms work, the evidence being the test results, the reviews and the traceability from requirement to test, so a reviewer who cannot follow that chain will reject the case however good the code is.

Worked example: a March test that finds a stuck bit

The RAM self-test is the mechanism you can build without hardware, a March test walking the memory in both directions and writing and reading 0 and 1 patterns, so that every bit is seen in both states. The version in this project is March C-, which the Microchip SRAM diagnostic documentation describes as detecting stuck-at faults, addressing faults, transition faults and coupling faults Microchip SRAM diagnostic, DIAG_SRAM_MarchCMinus.

The March C- sequence has six passes, the first writing 0 to every address, the second walking up, reading 0 and writing 1, the third walking up, reading 1 and writing 0, the fourth and fifth repeating that pattern walking down and the sixth walking up once more, reading 0. Pass 1 has one write per byte, passes 2 to 5 one read and one write each and pass 6 one read, which makes 10 operations per byte, as the quiz below checks. Before following the trace, predict in which pass a bit stuck at 0 is first caught, and in which a bit stuck at 1.

  1. The planted fault. The project's simulated RAM has 64 bytes, and the first test sticks bit 3 of address 17 at 0, so that bit reads 0 whatever was written.
  2. Passes 1 and 2. Pass 1 writes 0x00 to address 17, and pass 2 reads it back as 0x00, because the stuck bit is already 0, matching the expected 0x00, after which pass 2 writes 0xFF, stored as 0xFF.
  3. Pass 3. The test reads address 17 expecting 0xFF, but the stuck bit forces bit 3 to 0, so the read returns 0xF7, which is not 0xFF, and the function returns 17 at once.
  4. A bit stuck at 1. The second test sticks bit 0 of address 40 at 1, so although pass 1 writes 0x00, the read in pass 2 returns 0x01, because the stuck bit forces bit 0 high, and since the expected value is 0x00, the function returns 40 in pass 2.

A stuck-at-1 bit is therefore caught earlier than a stuck-at-0 bit in this sequence, because pass 2 is the first to read a stored 0, whereas pass 3 is the first to read a stored 1.

  1. The project's simulated RAM has a planted fault: bit 3 of address 17 is stuck at 0. March C− walks every address six times, so that every bit is seen holding 0 and holding 1.
  2. Pass 1 writes 0x00 to every address. Address 17 stores 0x00; the stuck bit is already 0, so nothing looks wrong.
  3. Pass 2 walks up, reading 0 and then writing 1. Address 17 reads 0x00, as expected. The write of 0xFF is stored as 0xF7, because bit 3 cannot become 1.
  4. Pass 3 walks up, reading 1. Address 17 should hold 0xFF but reads 0xF7: a mismatch.
  5. The test stops at once and returns 17, the failing address. Passes 4 to 6 never run. A bit stuck at 1 would have been caught one pass earlier, by the first read that expects 0.

The March C- code

The solution's march_c_minus() is short, its helper and its first two passes looking like this. 

static int check(const mem_iface_t *m, int addr, uint8_t expected) {
    return m->read(m->ctx, (uint8_t)addr) == expected;
}

int march_c_minus(const mem_iface_t *mem) {
    int n = mem->size;

    for (int a = 0; a < n; a++) {                 /* pass 1 */
        mem->write(mem->ctx, (uint8_t)a, 0x00u);
    }
    for (int a = 0; a < n; a++) {                 /* pass 2 */
        if (!check(mem, a, 0x00u)) return a;
        mem->write(mem->ctx, (uint8_t)a, 0xFFu);
    }

The mem_iface_t argument is a struct with a context pointer, a read function and a write function, through whose pointers the March code calls read and write without ever touching the RAM directly, which is why the same function can test the real RAM on the chip or the simulated RAM in the host test, the callback pattern from lesson 49.

The check() helper reads one address and compares the result with the expected pattern, returning 1 when they match, and it compares against the expected pattern rather than the value written earlier, the choice that makes stuck bits visible, while the cast to uint8_t keeps only the low 8 bits of the address, so it wraps at 256, well beyond the 64-byte RAM.

The first loop is pass 1, writing 0x00 to every address, and the second is pass 2, first checking that each address reads 0x00, returning the address on failure, and then writing 0xFF, the check coming before the write, so that the function always reads what the previous pass left behind. The remaining passes follow the same pattern, with the expected value and the direction changed as the sequence requires.

Try it: a March test that finds stuck bits

  1. Open projects/0112-march-host/starter in VS Code, or run python -I tools/build.py projects/0112-march-host/starter --test from the workspace root, the starter compiling but its tests failing, because the function reports every RAM as healthy.
  2. Write the six passes of march_c_minus() in src/march.c, following the comment at the top of the file, returning the address of the first wrong read, or -1 if every pass succeeds.
  3. The function works when the test summary reports 0 failures, four tests planting a stuck bit and checking that the function returns that address, one checking that healthy RAM passes and one checking that the simulator itself shows the stuck bit on a plain read.

The reference solution, in projects/0112-march-host/solution, passes all 6 tests, and the four fault tests are the ones that matter, because a self-test deserves trust only after it has found faults you planted on purpose.

Hint for the passes

Each pass reads what the previous pass wrote and then writes the next value. Passes 2 and 3 walk up, passes 4 and 5 walk down, and pass 6 is a final read that walks up. Remember that the test writes to every address, so it destroys the RAM contents: that is why a real device runs it at startup.

Hint for the stuck-bit tests

The RAM simulator applies a stuck bit on every read, so the fault is visible only through a read. A pass that writes a cell but never reads it cannot see the fault at all. Each pass must read the cell and compare the result with the value its sequence expects.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

A pass checked against the wrong expected value

A healthy RAM fails at address 0, the healthy-RAM test reporting 0 where it expects -1, because a developer copied the pass 3 check into pass 2, which then expects 0xFF although pass 1 wrote 0x00. Read the sequence from the comment at the top of the file and check each pass against the value the previous pass left behind.

The wrong return value

A fault test reports the right failure but with the wrong number, often one more than the address, because the function returns the value read, or the loop counter after the loop has finished, instead of the address that failed. Return a at the moment of the failed check, and nothing else.

The test run while the RAM holds live data

The program crashes or behaves strangely at random, the fault moving when the code changes, because a developer added the test to main after the stack and the globals were in use, so the March test, which writes every byte, overwrites the stack and the variables. Follow the comment in the solution's header, running the test at startup before the RAM holds anything you need, or testing a region that is not in use.

A passing self-test taken as proof of safety

The product runs for weeks with a failing cell, because the team saw the boot-time March test pass on every unit and wrote the safety case as finished, although a stuck bit can appear later in the product's life and the test runs only at boot. State the test's coverage and interval in the safety case, adding a runtime check or a plausibility check where the standard requires one, since the test is one mechanism rather than the whole case.

Vendor safety libraries and safety reviews

Microcontroller vendors selling into automotive and industrial markets publish safety manuals and self-test libraries for their parts, which a team usually adopts, runs at startup and whose evidence it reuses where it applies, adding its own requirements and tests, because the vendor cannot know the product's hazards, while reviewers ask for the traceability from each hazard to a mechanism and from each mechanism to a test.

A code review of safety firmware is stricter than one of ordinary firmware, reviewers looking for a coding standard, a documented set of deviations from it, static analysis with a clean report and tests that fail on the injected fault before the fix, as well as for what the code does when a mechanism fails, since the safe state must be entered on purpose rather than by accident.

Summary

  • Functional safety asks how often a harmful failure may happen, not how often the system fails.
  • IEC 61508 uses SIL 1 to 4 and ISO 26262 ASIL A to D, and the scales do not correspond directly.
  • The HARA rates severity, exposure and controllability for each hazard, and that rating sets the level.
  • The V-model pairs each level of design with a test on the right side, and the safety case collects the evidence.
  • A safety mechanism, such as a watchdog, a self-test or a plausibility check, detects a fault and moves the system to a safe state.
  • A March C- test uses six passes and finds a stuck-at-0 bit in pass 3 and a stuck-at-1 bit in pass 2.
  • The read is compared with the expected pattern, the test runs before the RAM is in use, and a passing test never counts as the whole case.
