Series and parallel circuits; Kirchhoff's laws

Real boards are networks of resistors, not single parts. Two rules, Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws, let you check any network, including a measured one.

By the end of this lesson you can reduce a series or parallel pair of resistors to one equivalent value, and check a circuit against Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) and voltage law (KVL) with a measurement.

Where we are and why it matters

Lessons 5 and 6 treated one resistor at a time. You found its current with Ohm's law and its power with P = V × I. A real board never looks like that. The Pico has pull-up resistors, LEDs with series resistors, a feedback network on its regulator, and sensors that share supply rails. Each of those is a small network of resistors, and you cannot read a network's current from one resistor's value.

The problem this lesson solves is a practical one. You have a schematic with several resistors, or a breadboard with several parts, and you must answer two questions. What is the total resistance the supply sees? And are the currents and voltages in the circuit what the design says they should be? The first question is answered by the series and parallel rules. The second is answered by two laws that Gustav Kirchhoff stated in the 1840s, and they are the checks you use on the bench and in a design review.

Series: one path, one current

Two components are in series when they form a single path, so the same current must pass through both. There is nowhere else for it to go. A string of holiday lights is a series circuit, and that is why one dead bulb can darken the whole string when the path breaks.

For resistors in series, the resistances simply add. The total is Rtotal = R1 + R2, and the series pair behaves like one resistor of that value. Two 10 kΩ resistors in series act like one 20 kΩ resistor, which is the simplest case. The rule works for any number of resistors in a row.

The series rule also explains how voltage splits. Because the current is the same through both resistors, each one drops a voltage proportional to its resistance. The bigger resistor takes the bigger share of the supply. Lesson 8 uses exactly this fact to build the voltage divider.

Parallel: one voltage, split currents

Components are in parallel when both of their ends join the same two points, called nodes. Every component in a parallel group sees the same voltage, because the voltage is the difference between the two nodes. The current, however, has more than one route, and it splits between them.

For resistors in parallel, the conductances add, not the resistances. Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance, measured in siemens, and it measures how easily current flows. So 1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2. For two resistors, the algebra simplifies to the product over the sum:

Rtotal = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2)

The result is always smaller than the smaller of the two resistors. Adding a second path can only let more current flow, so the total resistance falls. This is the most useful sanity check in the lesson: if your parallel answer is bigger than one of the resistors, you have made an error.

Kirchhoff's current law: charge does not pile up

A node is a point where three or more wires meet. Kirchhoff's current law, or KCL, says that the currents entering a node add up to the currents leaving it. The reason is conservation of charge: a node cannot hold charge, so charge that arrives must leave. Nothing in an ordinary circuit stores charge at a wire junction.

To use KCL, write the currents that enter a node on one side of an equation and the currents that leave it on the other, then check that the two sides are equal. The bench check is the same: put an ammeter in each branch, and the branch currents must add up to the current in the supply wire.

Kirchhoff's voltage law: a loop returns to where it started

A loop is any closed path through a circuit that starts and ends at the same point. Kirchhoff's voltage law, or KVL, says that the voltage rises and drops around any loop add up to zero. If you walk around a loop and return to where you began, you have gained as much voltage as you lost. A battery is a rise, and each resistor is a drop.

In a simple loop with one source and some resistors, the drops across the resistors add up to the source voltage. The same reasoning is why a voltage divider's two parts share the supply: the drops across them must total the supply, and each drop follows the resistance.

Worked examples with real numbers

Example 1: a parallel pair. A 100 Ω and a 1 kΩ resistor sit side by side across a 5 V source. Work through the steps in order.

  1. Write the knowns in base units: R1 = 100 Ω, R2 = 1000 Ω.
  2. Apply the product over sum: Rtotal = (100 × 1000) ÷ (100 + 1000) = 100 000 ÷ 1100 = 90.9 Ω.
  3. Find the total current: Itotal = 5 V ÷ 90.9 Ω = 0.055 A, which is 55 mA.
  4. Find each branch with Ohm's law: the voltage across each branch is 5 V, so I100 = 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 50 mA, and I1k = 5 V ÷ 1000 Ω = 5 mA.
  5. Check KCL at the node: 50 mA + 5 mA = 55 mA, which matches the total. The balance holds.

The step that people skip is step 4. Computing each branch with Ohm's law, and then adding the branch currents, gives an independent check on the equivalent resistance. If the branch currents do not add to the total, one of the numbers is wrong.

Example 2: a series pair. Two 10 kΩ resistors are in series across 10 V. The total resistance is 10 kΩ + 10 kΩ = 20 kΩ. The current is I = 10 V ÷ 20 kΩ = 0.5 mA, and the same 0.5 mA flows through both resistors, which is the series rule at work. Each resistor drops 0.5 mA × 10 kΩ = 5 V. The two drops add to 10 V, which is KVL around the loop.

Notice that the two examples check each other. In Example 1, the equivalent resistance predicts the total current, and the branch currents must add up to that same total. In Example 2, the series rule predicts the current, and the two voltage drops must add up to the supply. Each law is a check on the other method, so when a measured network disagrees with your calculation, the disagreement points to a specific mistake: a wrong resistance, a missing branch, or a meter in the wrong place.

When a series and parallel network mixes, reduce it one piece at a time. Replace each simple group by its equivalent, redraw, and repeat. Check at the end that the currents balance (KCL) and the drops add up (KVL).

The code: series and parallel in C

Firmware rarely needs network reduction, but a test or a calculator in a tool often does. The program below implements both rules as small functions and prints the results for the two examples above. 

#include <stdio.h>

/* Two resistors in parallel: product over sum, in ohms. */
static float parallel_ohms(float r1, float r2)
{
    return (r1 * r2) / (r1 + r2);
}

/* Two resistors in series: the resistances add, in ohms. */
static float series_ohms(float r1, float r2)
{
    return r1 + r2;
}

int main(void)
{
    printf("parallel 100 and 1000: %.1f ohm\n", (double)parallel_ohms(100.0f, 1000.0f));
    printf("series 10k and 10k: %.0f ohm\n", (double)series_ohms(10000.0f, 10000.0f));
    return 0;
}

parallel_ohms writes the product-over-sum form directly. The multiplication and the division are both done in float, because the literals are float and the result has to be fractional. The parentheses are optional: without them, the expression reads as r1 * r2 / (r1 + r2), which C evaluates left to right and gives the same result, but the explicit form shows the intent to the next reader.

series_ohms is the plainer rule, a simple addition. Its one-line body is the entire physics of a series path. The two helpers are static, so they are private to this file.

In main, the first printf prints 90.9 ohms, the parallel result from Example 1, with one decimal place. The second prints 20000 ohms, the series result from Example 2, with no decimal places, because %.0f rounds to an integer. The casts to double match what printf expects for a floating-point argument.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

  1. Adding parallel resistors as if they were in series. You write 100 Ω + 1 kΩ = 1.1 kΩ for a parallel pair. The symptom is a total resistance bigger than the smaller resistor, which cannot happen in parallel, and a current on the bench that is far below the value you calculated. The fix is the sanity check: a parallel total must be smaller than the smallest branch.
  2. Forgetting the reciprocal. You compute 1/Rtotal = 1/100 + 1/1000 and then stop, giving 0.011 as the answer in ohms. The symptom is a number that looks like a resistance but is a conductance. The fix is to invert the sum at the end, or use the product-over-sum form, which does the inversion for you.
  3. Measuring the wrong branch. You place the ammeter in the supply wire and read the total current, then assume it is the current through one resistor. The symptom is a branch reading that matches the total, which is impossible if there is a second branch. The fix is to put the ammeter in series with the branch you want, one at a time.
  4. Breaking KVL with a wrong sign. You walk around a loop and add a drop as a rise, so the sum does not come to zero. The symptom is a loop that does not balance when you check it on paper or in the simulator. The fix is to pick one direction around the loop, and mark every drop and rise against that direction.

In real projects

Series and parallel reduction is the first thing a hardware engineer does with a schematic. Pull-up resistors on a shared line, such as an I2C bus with more than one device fitted with pull-ups, are in parallel with each other, so their combined resistance is lower than any one of them. The designer checks that combined value, because it sets how fast the line can rise. The series resistor in front of an LED, which lesson 9 covers, is a series element whose current is set by the supply and the LED's forward voltage. Reading a schematic means seeing these groups at a glance.

Kirchhoff's laws are the checks that catch real faults. When a board draws more current than expected, the first measurement is the supply current. The second is the current in each branch, and KCL says they must add up. When a rail reads low, the third is the voltage across each series element, and KVL says the drops must total the supply. Engineers who can run these checks in their head find faults faster than engineers who only trust the schematic.

Many teams also keep a short calculation in the design notes for each network that matters, with the equivalent resistance, the expected branch currents and the measured values side by side. When a board is built and tested, the notes show at once which number disagrees. That habit turns Kirchhoff's laws from a textbook exercise into a record that a second engineer can check.

Summary

  • Series components share one current, and their resistances add: Rtotal = R1 + R2.
  • Parallel components share one voltage, and their conductances add: 1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2.
  • For two parallel resistors, Rtotal = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2), which is always smaller than the smaller one.
  • KCL: the currents entering a node equal the currents leaving it.
  • KVL: the voltage rises and drops around any closed loop add to zero.
  • Reduce a mixed network one group at a time, then check the currents at each node and the drops around each loop.

Try it in CircuitJS

You will check KCL with the same parallel pair you calculated above.

  1. Open Ohm's law in CircuitJS. The 100 Ω and 1 kΩ resistors are in parallel across 5 V. Calculate the total current first, using Rtotal = 90.9 Ω, which gives 55 mA.
  2. Select each resistor and read its current. You should see about 50 mA and 5 mA. Add the two currents. Do they equal the total you calculated?
  3. Double-click the 1 kΩ resistor, change it to 470 Ω, and read the total current drawn from the 5 V source. Type that total in amperes or with a prefix into the question below.
Hint

Work out 1/Rtotal = 1/100 + 1/470 first. Then invert it, and divide 5 V by the result. Alternatively, add the two branch currents: 5 V ÷ 100 Ω plus 5 V ÷ 470 Ω. You should get the same total, about 60.6 mA.

Check with the series divider

The voltage divider example has two 10 kΩ resistors in series on the left, across 10 V. Measure the voltage across the top 10 kΩ with the voltmeter. The loop KVL says the top and bottom drops add to 10 V, so each should read 5 V. Lesson 8 uses the same network to read a sensor.
