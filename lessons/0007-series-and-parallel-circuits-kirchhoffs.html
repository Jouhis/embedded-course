Series and parallel circuits; Kirchhoff's laws

Real boards are networks of resistors, not single parts. Two rules, Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws, let you check any network, including a measured one.

By the end of this lesson you can reduce a series or parallel pair of resistors to one equivalent value, and check a circuit against Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) and voltage law (KVL) with a measurement.

The idea

In a series connection, the same current flows through every component, because there is only one path. The resistances add: Rtotal = R1 + R2. In a parallel connection, every component has the same voltage across it, because both ends join the same two nodes. The conductances add, so 1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2. For two resistors this simplifies to R1 × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2), the "product over sum" form. Two resistors in parallel always give a value smaller than either one. Adding a path can only let more current flow, so the total resistance falls.

Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) says that the currents entering a node add up to the currents leaving it. A node is a point where three or more wires meet. Charge does not pile up at a node, so the sums balance. Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) says that around any closed loop the voltage rises and drops sum to zero. In a single loop, the drops across the resistors add up to the source voltage. Both laws are standard in the All About Circuits DC textbook.

Worked examples with real numbers

Parallel. A 100 Ω and a 1 kΩ resistor side by side: R = (100 × 1000) ÷ (100 + 1000) = 90.9 Ω. Across 5 V, the total current is 5 ÷ 90.9 = 55 mA, which splits into 50 mA through the 100 Ω and 5 mA through the 1 kΩ. Those two branch currents add to the total, which is KCL at the node.

Series. Two 10 kΩ resistors in series across 10 V. Rtotal = 20 kΩ, so the current is 10 V ÷ 20 kΩ = 0.5 mA. Each resistor drops 0.5 mA × 10 kΩ = 5 V. The two drops add to 10 V, which is KVL around the loop.

When a series and parallel network mixes, reduce it one piece at a time. Replace each simple group by its equivalent, redraw, and repeat. Check at the end that the currents balance (KCL) and the drops add up (KVL).

Try it in CircuitJS

You will check KCL with the same parallel pair you calculated above.

  1. Open Ohm's law in CircuitJS. The 100 Ω and 1 kΩ resistors are in parallel across 5 V. Calculate the total current first, using Rtotal = 90.9 Ω.
  2. Select each resistor and read its current. Add the two currents. Do they equal the total you calculated?
  3. Double-click the 1 kΩ resistor, change it to 470 Ω, and read the total current drawn from the 5 V source. Type that total in amperes or with a prefix into the question below.
Hint

Work out 1/Rtotal = 1/100 + 1/470 first. Then invert it, and divide 5 V by the result. Alternatively, add the two branch currents: 5 V ÷ 100 Ω plus 5 V ÷ 470 Ω. You should get the same total.

Check with the series divider

The voltage divider example has two 10 kΩ resistors in series on the left, across 10 V. Measure the voltage across the top 10 kΩ with the voltmeter. The loop KVL says the top and bottom drops add to 10 V, so each should read 5 V.

You reduced a parallel pair to one equivalent resistance, then checked both branch currents and the total with KCL. You can now check any simple network you measure.

"Two resistors, one in series and one in parallel, sit across a supply. How do the current and the voltage split, and how would you verify your answer on the bench?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

In series, the current is the same in both and the voltages split in proportion to the resistances. In parallel, the voltage is the same across both and the currents split in inverse proportion to the resistances. On the bench, measure the voltage across each part with a voltmeter in parallel, and measure the current with an ammeter in series. Check KVL around the loops and KCL at each node.
