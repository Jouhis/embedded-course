Series and parallel circuits; Kirchhoff's laws

Combining resistors, and the two laws that check any circuit

By the end of this lesson you can reduce a series or parallel pair of resistors to one equivalent value, and check a circuit against Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) and voltage law (KVL) with a measurement.

Networks of resistors on a real board

Lessons 5 and 6 treated one resistor at a time, finding its current with Ohm's law and its power with P = V × I. A real board rarely looks like that. A board such as the Pico carries LEDs with series resistors, pull-up and pull-down resistors, a regulator circuit and sensors that share supply rails, and each of those is a small network, so the current through any one part depends on the parts around it.

In practice you face a schematic with several resistors, or a breadboard with several parts, and need to answer two questions: what total resistance does the supply see, and are the currents and voltages what the design says they should be? The series and parallel rules answer the first question. Two laws that Gustav Kirchhoff stated in the 1840s answer the second, and they are the checks engineers use on the bench and in design reviews.

Resistors in series

Two components are in series when they form a single path, so the same current must pass through both, having nowhere else to go. A string of old-style holiday lights is a series circuit, which is why one dead bulb can darken the whole string once its filament breaks the path. For resistors in series, the resistances simply add:

Rtotal = R1 + R2(1)

where R1 and R2 are the two resistances and Rtotal is the single resistance that behaves the same way, often called the equivalent resistance. Two 10 kΩ resistors in series therefore act like one 20 kΩ resistor, and the rule extends to any number of resistors in a row by adding one more term for each.

The series rule also explains how the supply voltage divides. Because the current is the same through both resistors, Ohm's law makes each drop proportional to its resistance, so the larger resistor takes the larger share of the supply. Lesson 8 builds the voltage divider on exactly this fact.

A phone's battery gauge measures current with one tiny resistor, a few milliohms, in series with the battery. Since a series path carries one current, that resistor carries every milliamp the phone uses, whichever part draws it. The gauge chip reads the small voltage across it, converts it to a current with Ohm's law, and adds it up over time to estimate how much charge is left.

Resistors in parallel

Components are in parallel when both of their ends join the same two points, called nodes. Every component in a parallel group sees the same voltage, because a voltage is the difference between two points and the group shares both points. The current, however, now has more than one route, so it splits between the branches.

For parallel resistors it is the conductances that add. Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance, measured in siemens, and it describes how easily current flows, which gives

1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2(2)

where each term 1/R is a conductance. For exactly two resistors the algebra simplifies to the product over the sum:

Rtotal = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2)(3)

where the symbols mean the same as in equation (1). The result is always smaller than the smaller of the two resistors, because a second path can only let more current flow, so the total resistance has to fall. That gives the most useful sanity check in the lesson: if a parallel answer is larger than one of the resistors, there is an error somewhere. Figure 1 shows the two arrangements side by side.

Kirchhoff's current law at a node

A node is a point where three or more wires meet. Kirchhoff's current law, or KCL, states that the currents entering a node add up to the currents leaving it:

ΣIin = ΣIout(4)

where Σ means "the sum of" and the two sides list every current flowing into and out of the node. The reason is conservation of charge, since a wire junction cannot store charge, so any charge that arrives must leave. On paper you write the entering currents on one side and the leaving currents on the other and check that the sides are equal. On the bench the check is the same, with an ammeter in each branch whose readings must add up to the current in the supply wire.

  1. A 100 Ω and a 1 kΩ resistor are in parallel: both ends join the same two nodes, so each one has the full 5 V across it.
  2. Ohm's law for the first branch: 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 50 mA.
  3. And for the second: 5 V ÷ 1000 Ω = 5 mA. The smaller resistor takes the larger current.
  4. At the top node, charge does not pile up: the 55 mA arriving from the source equals the 50 mA + 5 mA leaving. That matches 5 V ÷ 90.9 Ω, the parallel pair's equivalent resistance.

Kirchhoff's voltage law around a loop

A loop is any closed path through a circuit that starts and ends at the same point. Kirchhoff's voltage law, or KVL, states that the voltage rises and drops around any loop add up to zero:

ΣV = 0(5)

where each rise counts as positive and each drop as negative, taken in one direction around the loop. If you walk around a loop and return to where you began, you have gained exactly as much voltage as you lost, with a battery as a rise and each resistor, walked in the direction of the current, as a drop.

Picture a stadium exit. At a junction of corridors everyone who walks in walks out again, because nobody stays at the junction, and that is KCL as a headcount for charge. Now picture a round hiking trail, where however the path climbs and drops you end at the height you started from, so the climbs and descents add up to zero, which is KVL with voltage as the height. The picture breaks down for crowds, which can bunch up in a corridor for a while, whereas charge in an ordinary wire cannot.

In a simple loop with one source and a few resistors, KVL means that the drops across the resistors add up to the source voltage. That is the same reasoning that makes the two parts of a voltage divider share the supply, since their drops must total the supply and each drop follows its resistance. The animation below walks once around such a loop and adds up the voltages.

  1. Start at point A, at the bottom of the source, and call it 0 V. The loop carries 10 V ÷ 20 kΩ = 0.5 mA.
  2. Walk up through the source, from − to +: the voltage rises by 10 V.
  3. Through the first resistor, in the direction of the current, it falls by 0.5 mA × 10 kΩ = 5 V.
  4. Through the second resistor it falls by another 5 V.
  5. Along the plain wire back down, nothing changes: an ideal wire has no voltage across it.
  6. Back at A the total is +10 − 5 − 5 = 0. Every closed loop sums to zero, which is Kirchhoff's voltage law.

Worked examples with a parallel pair and a series pair

The first example is a parallel pair: a 100 Ω and a 1 kΩ resistor side by side across a 5 V source. Before working it through, predict whether the total resistance will be above or below 100 Ω, and roughly by how much.

  1. Write the knowns in base units. R1 = 100 Ω and R2 = 1000 Ω, so no prefixes remain.
  2. Apply the product over the sum. Equation (3) gives Rtotal = (100 × 1000) ÷ (100 + 1000) = 100 000 ÷ 1100 = 90.9 Ω, just below the smaller resistor, as the sanity check requires.
  3. Find the total current. Itotal = 5 V ÷ 90.9 Ω = 0.055 A, which is 55 mA.
  4. Find each branch with Ohm's law. Each branch has the full 5 V across it, so I100 = 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 50 mA and I1k = 5 V ÷ 1000 Ω = 5 mA.
  5. Check KCL at the node. 50 mA + 5 mA = 55 mA, which matches the total, so the balance holds.

Step 4 is the one people tend to skip, yet it is what makes the result trustworthy. Computing each branch separately and adding the currents gives an independent check on the equivalent resistance, so if the branch currents do not add up to the total, one of the numbers is wrong.

The second example is a series pair, two 10 kΩ resistors across 10 V. Equation (1) gives a total of 10 kΩ + 10 kΩ = 20 kΩ, so the current is I = 10 V ÷ 20 kΩ = 0.5 mA, and by the series rule the same 0.5 mA flows through both resistors. Each resistor then drops 0.5 mA × 10 kΩ = 5 V, and the two drops add to the 10 V supply, which is KVL around the loop.

The two examples check each other. In the parallel case the equivalent resistance predicts the total current, and the branch currents must add up to that same total, while in the series case the series rule predicts the current and the two drops must add up to the supply. When a measured network disagrees with your calculation, the disagreement therefore points to a specific mistake, such as a wrong resistance, a missing branch or a meter in the wrong place.

When a series and parallel network mixes, reduce it one piece at a time. Replace each simple group by its equivalent, redraw, and repeat. Check at the end that the currents balance (KCL) and the drops add up (KVL).

Series and parallel resistance in C

Firmware rarely needs network reduction, but a test or a calculation tool often does. The program below implements both rules as small functions and prints the results for the two worked examples. 

#include <stdio.h>

/* Two resistors in parallel: product over sum, in ohms. */
static float parallel_ohms(float r1, float r2)
{
    return (r1 * r2) / (r1 + r2);
}

/* Two resistors in series: the resistances add, in ohms. */
static float series_ohms(float r1, float r2)
{
    return r1 + r2;
}

int main(void)
{
    printf("parallel 100 and 1000: %.1f ohm\n", (double)parallel_ohms(100.0f, 1000.0f));
    printf("series 10k and 10k: %.0f ohm\n", (double)series_ohms(10000.0f, 10000.0f));
    return 0;
}

parallel_ohms writes equation (3) directly. The multiplication and the division both run in float, because the literals are float and the result has to be fractional. The parentheses around the product are optional, since C evaluates r1 * r2 / (r1 + r2) from left to right and gets the same result, but the explicit form shows the intent to the next reader.

series_ohms is equation (1), a single addition, and its one-line body is the whole physics of a series path. Both helpers are static, which keeps them private to this file.

In main, the first printf prints 90.9 ohms, the parallel result from the first example, with one decimal place. The second prints 20000 ohms, the series result from the second example, with no decimals because %.0f rounds to an integer. The casts to double match what printf expects for a floating-point argument.

Common mistakes in network calculations

Parallel resistors added as if in series

You write 100 Ω + 1 kΩ = 1.1 kΩ for a parallel pair, and the bench current comes out far above the value you calculated. A total larger than the smaller resistor cannot happen in parallel, because each extra branch adds a path. The fix is the sanity check: a parallel total must be smaller than the smallest branch.

A missing reciprocal

You compute 1/Rtotal = 1/100 + 1/1000 and stop there, giving 0.011 as the answer in ohms. The number looks like a resistance, but it is a conductance in siemens, because equation (2) gives the reciprocal of the total. The fix is to invert the sum at the end, or to use the product over the sum, which does the inversion for you.

A measurement in the wrong branch

You place the ammeter in the supply wire, read the total current, and take it as the current through one resistor. A branch reading that matches the total is impossible when a second branch exists, so the meter is in the wrong place. The fix is to put the ammeter in series with the branch you want, one branch at a time.

A wrong sign in a KVL sum

You walk around a loop and add a drop as a rise, so the sum does not come to zero on paper or against the simulator. The cause is a direction that changed partway round the loop. The fix is to pick one direction around the loop and mark every drop and rise against that direction.

Network checks in real projects

Series and parallel reduction is the first thing a hardware engineer does with a schematic. Pull-up resistors on a shared line, such as an I2C bus with more than one device fitted with pull-ups, are in parallel with each other, so their combined resistance is lower than any one of them, and the designer checks that value because it sets how fast the line can rise. The series resistor in front of an LED, covered in lesson 9, is a series element whose current depends on the supply and the LED's forward voltage. Reading a schematic means seeing these groups at a glance.

Kirchhoff's laws are the checks that catch real faults. When a board draws more current than expected, the first measurement is the supply current and the second is the current in each branch, which KCL says must add up. When a rail reads low, the next step is the voltage across each series element, and KVL says the drops must total the supply. Engineers who run these checks routinely find faults faster than those who trust the schematic alone.

Many teams also keep a short calculation in the design notes for each network that matters, with the equivalent resistance, the expected branch currents and the measured values side by side. When a board is built and tested, the notes show at once which number disagrees, so Kirchhoff's laws become a written record that a second engineer can check.

Summary

  • Series components share one current, and their resistances add: Rtotal = R1 + R2.
  • Parallel components share one voltage, and their conductances add: 1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2.
  • For two parallel resistors, Rtotal = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2), which is always smaller than the smaller one.
  • KCL: the currents entering a node equal the currents leaving it.
  • KVL: the voltage rises and drops around any closed loop add to zero.
  • Reduce a mixed network one group at a time, then check the currents at each node and the drops around each loop.
