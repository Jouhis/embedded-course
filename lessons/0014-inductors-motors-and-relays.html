Inductors, motors, and relays: inductive kickback

A relay or motor coil stores energy in a magnetic field. When you switch it off, that energy has to go somewhere, and it can destroy the switch if you have not planned for it.

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the voltage spike across a switched-off coil, measure the steady coil current in a simulator, and choose between a flyback diode and an RC snubber.

The idea

An inductor opposes a change in current. The voltage across it is V = L × di/dt, where L is the inductance in henries (H) and di/dt is how fast the current changes. A coil with a steady current stores energy as ½ × L × I². Since the voltage depends on the rate of change, a fast switch-off can produce a very large voltage. Kickback is a problem for coils with a large inductance and a fast switch-off. A short PCB trace has too little inductance to matter here.

Kickback worked through

Take a 0.1 H relay coil carrying 50 mA. If the MOSFET switches off the current in 10 µs, the coil voltage is V = 0.1 × 0.05 ÷ 10×10−6 = 500 V. The 12 V supply has no say in that number. The coil forces the drain of the MOSFET to rise until something conducts, or the MOSFET breaks down. That is the kickback. It is a standard inductive-load problem, covered in the All About Circuits textbook, and it is the reason a flyback diode belongs across every coil.

Two protections

A flyback diode across the coil is the cheapest protection, and it works for DC coils only. It makes the coil current decay slowly, so the release time gets longer. An RC snubber, a resistor and capacitor in series across the coil, absorbs the energy instead of recirculating it. It is the usual choice when a faster release matters, and it also suits AC coils, where a diode would conduct on every half-cycle. A diode across the MOSFET does not protect the coil. It protects the MOSFET from the spike, so it has to be placed across the coil, not across the switch.

Motors and relays

A DC motor is a coil with a back-EMF. When it spins, it generates a voltage that opposes the supply, so the current at full speed is lower than the stall current. A motor driven from a MOSFET needs the same flyback diode as a relay, because its winding is inductive. A relay is the same idea with a mechanical contact that switches a second circuit.

Try it in CircuitJS

The built-in example is a 5 V supply, a 1 H inductor and a 100 Ω resistor, with a switch that opens the circuit. A small capacitor across the switch is where the spike appears.

  1. Open the example: CircuitJS: inductkick.txt.
  2. Make sure the switch is closed, by clicking it if needed. Let the simulation run for about 50 ms of simulated time. The coil's time constant is L ÷ R = 1 H ÷ 100 Ω = 10 ms, so five time constants are enough to settle.
  3. Select the inductor and read its current. In steady state a coil with no winding resistance has the current set by the supply and the resistor alone: I = V ÷ R.
  4. Predict the steady current first, then read it off the element. Then open the switch and watch the scope. Compare the spike with the supply voltage. The spike is far larger, because the coil cannot change its current instantly.
Hint

The steady current is the supply divided by the resistor, which is 5 V ÷ 100 Ω. If the reading stays at zero, the switch is still open. Click the switch to close it, then wait for the trace to settle.

The second file, inductkick-snub.txt, adds a 100 Ω and 10 µF RC snubber across the coil. Open it from CircuitJS: inductkick-snub.txt and open the switch. Compare the spike with the first file. The snubber should lower the spike and slow the decay.

You measured the steady coil current from its supply and resistor, and you saw the switch-off spike that a flyback diode or a snubber must control. Those are the two checks you make on any relay or motor driver.

"You switch a relay with a MOSFET and the MOSFET dies after a few days. What happened and what do you change?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

The coil is inductive, so when the MOSFET turns off, the coil voltage rises until the drain avalanches. Repeated spikes eventually destroy the device. I would add a flyback diode across the coil, with its cathode to the positive supply, so the current recirculates. If the release time matters, I would use an RC snubber instead. I would also check the MOSFET's drain-source voltage rating against the supply plus the spike.
