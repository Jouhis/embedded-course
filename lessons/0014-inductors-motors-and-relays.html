Inductors, motors, and relays: inductive kickback

A relay or motor coil stores energy in a magnetic field. When you switch it off, that energy has to go somewhere, and it can destroy the switch if you have not planned for it.

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the voltage spike across a switched-off coil, measure the steady coil current in a simulator, and choose between a flyback diode and an RC snubber.

Where we are and why it matters

Lesson 13 introduced the capacitor, which stores energy in an electric field and resists a change in voltage. This lesson introduces the inductor, which does the same job for current. It stores energy in a magnetic field, and it resists a change in current. The two components are a matched pair, and a board that switches power needs to understand both.

The problem is real and common. A relay or a motor is a coil of wire, which is an inductor. A pin switches the coil on through a MOSFET, as lesson 12 showed, and the design seems to work. Then one day the MOSFET fails, or the microcontroller resets, or the relay chatters. The coil has done something to the circuit that the resistor-only calculations of lessons 5 to 9 never predicted. The rest of this lesson explains what the coil does when its current changes, why the change produces a voltage far above the supply, and how a diode or a snubber stops it.

The idea

An inductor opposes a change in current. The voltage across it is V = L × di/dt, where L is the inductance in henries (H) and di/dt is how fast the current changes. A coil with a steady current stores energy as ½ × L × I². Since the voltage depends on the rate of change, a fast switch-off can produce a very large voltage. Kickback is a problem for coils with a large inductance and a fast switch-off. A short PCB trace has too little inductance to matter here.

How a coil stores energy

An inductor is a coil of wire. When current flows through the coil, it creates a magnetic field, and the field links back through the coil's own turns. The inductance, L, measures how strongly the coil does this. It is measured in henries, H, and a coil of many turns on a magnetic core has a larger inductance than a short loop of wire. A relay coil and a motor winding both have inductance, and the sizes vary with their construction, so read the value from the part's datasheet when you need it.

The key rule is V = L × di/dt. The voltage across an inductor is its inductance times how fast its current changes. A coil with steady current has no voltage across it, because di/dt is zero, which is why a wire-wound coil looks like a short to a steady DC supply. Change the current, and the coil pushes back with a voltage that opposes the change. The more suddenly you change the current, the bigger that voltage. That is the whole of the kickback problem.

A coil with current I also stores energy. The stored energy is E = ½ × L × I². It is the magnetic-field counterpart of the ½ × C × V² that a capacitor stores in lesson 13. When the current is switched off, that energy has to go somewhere, and the coil's voltage is the mechanism it uses to dissipate it. A small coil stores a small amount, but a fast switch-off turns that energy into a short, large voltage spike.

The coil has no steady voltage and no sudden current change in a steady state, so the relation between the two is what matters in transients. A capacitor resists a change in voltage, and an inductor resists a change in current. Keep that pairing in mind, because it is why the two components appear together in filters and power supplies later in the course.

Kickback worked through

Take a 0.1 H relay coil carrying 50 mA. If the MOSFET switches off the current in 10 µs, the coil voltage is V = 0.1 × 0.05 ÷ 10×10−6 = 500 V. The 12 V supply has no say in that number. The coil forces the drain of the MOSFET to rise until something conducts, or the MOSFET breaks down. That is the kickback. It is a standard inductive-load problem, covered in the All About Circuits textbook, and it is the reason a flyback diode belongs across every coil.

Worked example: the energy and the two time constants

Use the built-in example's values, a 1 H coil and a 100 Ω series resistor on 5 V, to put numbers on the stored energy and on the time it takes to settle. Those values are the ones in the inductkick circuit.

  1. Steady current. I = V ÷ R = 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 50 mA. With no winding resistance in the model, that is the current the coil settles to.
  2. Energy stored at that current. E = ½ × L × I² = ½ × 1 H × (0.05 A)² = 1.25 mJ. That is the energy the switch-off must remove.
  3. Rise time. The coil's time constant is τ = L ÷ R = 1 H ÷ 100 Ω = 10 ms. The current reaches about 63 % of its final value after 10 ms and is settled after about 50 ms. This is the same five-τ rule as the capacitor in lesson 13, with L ÷ R in place of R × C.
  4. Switch-off with a flyback diode. The coil current now flows round through the coil, the resistor and the diode, and it decays with the same ratio, τ = L ÷ R = 10 ms. The current falls slowly, and the voltage across the coil is held near the diode drop, not at hundreds of volts. This is why the diode slows the release and protects the switch.

The energy calculation shows what the protection has to remove. Whatever path you give the current, the 1.25 mJ must be dissipated somewhere. A diode dissipates it in the coil's resistance over 10 ms. A snubber dissipates it in its resistor, and the next section gives its time constant.

Two protections

A flyback diode across the coil is the cheapest protection, and it works for DC coils only. It makes the coil current decay slowly, so the release time gets longer. An RC snubber, a resistor and capacitor in series across the coil, absorbs the energy instead of recirculating it. It is the usual choice when a faster release matters, and it also suits AC coils, where a diode would conduct on every half-cycle. A diode across the MOSFET does not protect the coil. It protects the MOSFET from the spike, so it has to be placed across the coil, not across the switch.

How the two protections differ

Use the two circuits in the simulator to compare them. In the flyback case, the current keeps circulating and decays with τ = L ÷ R. The spike is clamped, but the coil stays energised a little longer, so the release is slow. In the snubber case, the coil current charges a capacitor in series with a resistor across the coil, and the capacitor absorbs the energy. The snubber in the built-in file is 100 Ω and 10 µF, so its own time constant is R × C = 100 Ω × 10 µF = 1 ms. That is shorter than the coil's 10 ms. Compare the two built-in files in the simulator to see the release for yourself, rather than trusting the arithmetic alone.

The trade-off is simple. A diode gives the cleanest clamp and the slowest release, and it only works on a DC coil, because a diode conducts in one direction. A snubber gives a faster release and works on an AC coil as well, but it has to be sized: too small a capacitor clamps poorly, and too large a resistor does not absorb the energy fast enough. Lesson 13's RC time constant is the tool for sizing it.

Motors and relays

A DC motor is a coil with a back-EMF. When it spins, it generates a voltage that opposes the supply, so the current at full speed is lower than the stall current. A motor driven from a MOSFET needs the same flyback diode as a relay, because its winding is inductive. A relay is the same idea with a mechanical contact that switches a second circuit.

Worked example: a motor's back-EMF

Use illustrative numbers to see why a running motor draws less current than a stalled one. Suppose a DC motor's winding has a resistance of 2 Ω and is fed from 12 V. When the rotor is held still, nothing opposes the supply, and the current is I = 12 V ÷ 2 Ω = 6 A. Once the motor spins, it generates a back-EMF, a voltage that opposes the supply. Suppose it reaches 8 V at full speed.

  1. Stall current. I = 12 V ÷ 2 Ω = 6 A. The winding and the driver must handle that current, which is much larger than the running current.
  2. Running current. The voltage across the winding is the supply minus the back-EMF, 12 V − 8 V = 4 V. The current is I = 4 V ÷ 2 Ω = 2 A.
  3. What it means for the switch. The MOSFET must carry the stall current when the motor starts or jams, so its current rating and its heating from lesson 12 must cover 6 A, not only 2 A. The flyback diode must also handle the coil's switch-off energy at whatever current the motor was drawing when it stopped.

The back-EMF is the reason a motor's current depends on its speed, and it is the reason a motor driver is designed around the stall case. It is also the source of the confusion the glossary warns about. The inductive kickback spike comes from the coil's own inductance, and back-EMF is the motor's voltage as it spins. They are different effects that both appear in the same winding.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

Mistake 1: the flyback diode installed backwards

The symptom is a relay that does not pull in when the pin goes high, a supply that sags, and a diode that gets hot within seconds of power-up. A flyback diode is reverse biased while the coil is powered, so it should block. If it is placed the wrong way round, with its anode at the supply and its cathode at the switched end of the coil, it is forward biased while the coil is powered. It then bypasses the coil, and the supply is short-circuited to ground through the diode and the switch. The fix is to check the band on the diode against the schematic before you solder, and to measure the diode with a meter in diode mode. The cathode, marked with the band, must go to the positive supply end of the coil.

Mistake 2: the protection across the switch instead of the coil

The symptom is that the MOSFET still fails, or the relay releases more slowly than expected while the spike is still visible on a scope. A diode across the switch does not see the coil's current when the switch opens. The protection has to be across the coil, where the current needs a path. The fix is to move the diode to the coil, as the sidenote in the second section says.

Mistake 3: the scope's ground lead ringing the spike

The symptom is a spike that rings and oscillates at a high frequency, and a spike amplitude that changes when you move the probe's ground lead. A long ground lead on a scope probe adds inductance, and it rings with the probe's capacitance, so the picture shows oscillation that is partly the probe and partly the circuit. The fix is to use the probe's short ground spring, which sits at the probe tip, and to clip the probe's ground to the circuit's ground node, which is the MOSFET's source here, as lesson 10's ground-clip rule says.

Mistake 4: a snubber sized by guess

The symptom is a snubber that makes the relay chatter, or one that barely changes the spike. A snubber that is too small clamps poorly, and one that is far too large holds the coil current for a long time. The fix is to size it with the RC time constant from lesson 13 and compare the spike on a scope before and after.

In real projects

Coils are everywhere on a real board: relays that switch mains or a second supply, solenoids that open valves, and motors for fans and actuators. The professional design rule is simple. Every coil gets a protection element across it, chosen for the release time the application needs. Many motor driver and relay driver ICs include clamp diodes internally, and the datasheet says so, which is a reason to read the datasheet before you add a discrete diode. A reviewer checks the diode's polarity, its current rating and its voltage rating, and asks what the MOSFET's drain-source rating is against the supply plus the spike.

On a 3D printer, the fan and the motor drivers are inductive loads. The design is the same: the driver must handle the stall current, and the coil must have a protection path. When a printer's fan stops, or a stepper driver faults, the first thing a good engineer checks is the protection and the driver's current limit, not the firmware.

Interviews often ask why a MOSFET fails after days of relay use. The model answer below covers the mechanism and the fix, and it is the question to practise until you can answer it in a minute.

Summary

  • An inductor opposes a change in current. Its voltage is V = L × di/dt, and it stores energy as ½ × L × I².
  • When a coil's current is switched off quickly, the coil's voltage rises until something conducts. That spike is inductive kickback, and it can reach hundreds of volts.
  • A flyback diode across the coil, cathode to the positive supply, gives the current a path. The current then decays with τ = L ÷ R.
  • An RC snubber across the coil absorbs the energy. Its time constant is R × C, and it suits AC coils as well as DC.
  • A motor's back-EMF reduces its running current, so the driver must be designed for the stall current.
  • Check the diode's polarity against the schematic, place the protection across the coil and measure the spike with a short ground lead.

Try it in CircuitJS

The built-in example is a 5 V supply, a 1 H inductor and a 100 Ω resistor, with a switch that opens the circuit. A small capacitor across the switch is where the spike appears.

  1. Open the example: CircuitJS: inductkick.txt.
  2. Make sure the switch is closed, by clicking it if needed. Let the simulation run for about 50 ms of simulated time. The coil's time constant is L ÷ R = 1 H ÷ 100 Ω = 10 ms, so five time constants are enough to settle.
  3. Select the inductor and read its current. In steady state a coil with no winding resistance has the current set by the supply and the resistor alone: I = V ÷ R.
  4. Predict the steady current first, then read it off the element. Then open the switch and watch the scope. Compare the spike with the supply voltage. The spike is far larger, because the coil cannot change its current instantly.
  5. Open CircuitJS: inductkick-snub.txt, which has the snubber across the coil. Let it settle to 50 mA, then open the switch and compare the spike and the release with the first file. Write down which one releases sooner.
Hint

The steady current is the supply divided by the resistor, which is 5 V ÷ 100 Ω. If the reading stays at zero, the switch is still open. Click the switch to close it, then wait for the trace to settle.

The second file, inductkick-snub.txt, adds a 100 Ω and 10 µF RC snubber across the coil. Open it from CircuitJS: inductkick-snub.txt and open the switch. Compare the spike with the first file. The snubber should lower the spike and slow the decay.

You measured the steady coil current from its supply and resistor, and you saw the switch-off spike that a flyback diode or a snubber must control. Those are the two checks you make on any relay or motor driver.

Model answer

The coil is inductive, so when the MOSFET turns off, the coil voltage rises until the drain avalanches. Repeated spikes eventually destroy the device. I would add a flyback diode across the coil, with its cathode to the positive supply, so the current recirculates. If the release time matters, I would use an RC snubber instead. I would also check the MOSFET's drain-source voltage rating against the supply plus the spike.
