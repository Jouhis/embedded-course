Inductors, motors, and relays

Coils, their stored energy and the spike at switch-off

By the end of this lesson you can calculate the voltage spike across a switched-off coil, measure the steady coil current in a simulator, and choose between a flyback diode and an RC snubber.

Coils in switched circuits

Lesson 13 introduced the capacitor, which stores energy in an electric field and resists a change in voltage. The inductor does the same for current: it stores energy in a magnetic field and resists a change in current. The two are counterparts, and any board that switches power has to account for both.

The problem is real and common. A relay or a motor is a coil of wire, and therefore an inductor. A pin switches the coil on through a MOSFET, as in lesson 12, and the design appears to work until one day the MOSFET fails, the microcontroller resets or the relay chatters. The coil has done something that the resistor-only calculations of lessons 5 to 9 never predicted, and this lesson explains what a coil does when its current changes, why that change produces a voltage far above the supply, and how a diode or a snubber prevents it.

Inductance and stored energy

An inductor is a coil of wire. Current through the coil creates a magnetic field that links back through the coil's own turns, and the inductance L, measured in henries (H), expresses how strongly it does so. A coil of many turns on a magnetic core has a much larger inductance than a short loop of wire. Relay coils and motor windings both have inductance whose size depends on their construction, so read the value from the part's datasheet when you need it.

The voltage across an inductor is

V = L × di/dt(1)

where di/dt is the rate at which its current changes. With a steady current di/dt is zero, so there is no voltage across the coil, which is why a wire-wound coil looks like a short circuit to a steady DC supply. When the current changes, the coil pushes back with a voltage that opposes the change, and the more suddenly the current changes, the larger that voltage becomes. Kickback is a problem for coils with a large inductance and a fast switch-off. A short PCB trace has far too little inductance to matter here.

A coil carrying a current I also stores energy,

E = ½ × L × I²(2)

where E is in joules. This is the magnetic counterpart of the ½ × C × V² that a capacitor stores in lesson 13. When the current is switched off, the energy must go somewhere, and the coil's voltage is the mechanism by which it is released, so even a modest amount of stored energy turns into a short, large voltage spike when the switch-off is fast.

In a steady state a coil shows neither voltage nor any sudden change in current, so its behaviour matters during transients. A capacitor resists a change in voltage, whereas an inductor resists a change in current. That pairing explains why the two components appear together in filters and power supplies later in the course.

Current in a coil behaves like water moving through a long pipe. Getting it moving takes effort, and once moving it tends to keep going, so closing a valve suddenly makes the water slam into it. Plumbers call this water hammer, and the pressure spike can burst a pipe; switching off a coil does the same with voltage. A flyback diode acts as a bypass pipe around the valve, letting the water circulate and slow down gently. The comparison does not show that the coil's energy ends up as heat in the resistance of that loop.

Inductive kickback

Consider a 0.1 H relay coil on a 12 V supply, carrying 50 mA. If the MOSFET switches the current off in 10 µs, Equation 1 gives V = 0.1 × 0.05 ÷ (10 × 10−6) = 500 V. The 12 V supply does not limit this figure, because the coil forces the MOSFET's drain upwards until something conducts or the MOSFET breaks down. This is inductive kickback, a standard problem with inductive loads covered in the All About Circuits textbook, and it is why a flyback diode belongs across every coil.

Worked example: stored energy and the coil time constant

The built-in CircuitJS example uses a 1 H coil and a 100 Ω series resistor on 5 V, the values of its inductkick circuit. We use them to put numbers on the stored energy and the settling time, so that the simulation later has something to be checked against.

  1. Steady current. I = V ÷ R = 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 50 mA. The model has no winding resistance, so this is the current the coil settles to.
  2. Energy at that current. Equation 2 gives E = ½ × 1 H × (0.05 A)² = 1.25 mJ, which is the energy the switch-off must remove.
  3. Rise time. The coil's time constant is τ = L ÷ R = 1 H ÷ 100 Ω = 10 ms, so the current reaches about 63 % of its final value after 10 ms and has settled after about 50 ms. This is the five-τ rule from lesson 13, with L ÷ R in place of R × C.
  4. Switch-off with a flyback diode. The coil current now circulates through the coil, the resistor and the diode, and decays with the same ratio, τ = L ÷ R = 10 ms. The current falls slowly while the voltage across the coil stays near the diode drop instead of reaching hundreds of volts, which is how the diode slows the release and protects the switch.

The energy figure shows what any protection has to deal with. Whatever path the current is given, the 1.25 mJ must be dissipated somewhere. With a diode it is dissipated in the coil's circuit resistance over about 10 ms, and with a snubber in the snubber's resistor, whose time constant the next section gives.

  1. The MOSFET is on. 50 mA flows from the 12 V supply through the 0.1 H relay coil, and its drain sits near 0 V.
  2. The MOSFET switches off in 10 µs. The coil's current cannot stop instantly: an inductor opposes any change in its current.
  3. With nowhere to go, the coil forces the drain up until something conducts: V = L × di/dt = 0.1 × 0.05 ÷ 10 µs = 500 V. The supply does not limit it, and the MOSFET breaks down.
  4. Add a flyback diode across the coil. While the MOSFET is on, the diode is reverse biased and carries nothing.
  5. At switch-off the coil's current flows round through the diode and decays. The drain is clamped one diode drop above the supply, near 12.7 V, and the MOSFET survives.

Flyback diodes and RC snubbers

A flyback diode across the coil is the cheapest protection, and it works for DC coils only. It makes the coil current decay slowly, so the relay takes longer to release. An RC snubber, a resistor and a capacitor in series across the coil, absorbs the energy instead of recirculating it. It is the usual choice when a faster release matters, and it also suits AC coils, where a diode would conduct on every half-cycle. The flyback diode goes across the coil, with its cathode at the positive supply end, rather than across the MOSFET. Its job is to clamp the coil's switch-off spike, and that is what protects the MOSFET.

The two behave differently at switch-off. With the diode, the current keeps circulating and decays with τ = L ÷ R, so the spike is clamped but the coil stays energised a little longer. With the snubber, the coil current charges the capacitor through the resistor, and the capacitor absorbs the energy. The snubber in the built-in file is 100 Ω and 10 µF, so its own time constant is R × C = 100 Ω × 10 µF = 1 ms, shorter than the coil's 10 ms. The two simulator files let you see the release for yourself instead of relying on the arithmetic alone.

The trade-off follows from this. A diode gives the cleanest clamp and the slowest release and works only on a DC coil, since it conducts in one direction. A snubber releases faster and also works on AC coils, but it must be sized, because too small a capacitor clamps poorly and too large a resistor fails to absorb the energy quickly enough. The RC time constant from lesson 13 is the tool for sizing it.

Motors and back-EMF

A DC motor is a coil with a back-EMF: while it spins, it generates a voltage that opposes the supply, so its current at full speed is lower than its stall current. A motor driven from a MOSFET needs the same flyback diode as a relay, because its winding is inductive. A relay is the same arrangement with a mechanical contact that switches a second circuit.

Worked example: stall and running current of a DC motor

Illustrative numbers show why a running motor draws less current than a stalled one. Suppose a DC motor's winding has a resistance of 2 Ω and is fed from 12 V, and that its back-EMF reaches 8 V at full speed. Before working it through, decide which of the two currents the MOSFET must be rated for.

  1. Stall current. With the rotor held still, nothing opposes the supply, so I = 12 V ÷ 2 Ω = 6 A. The winding and the driver must handle this current, which is far larger than the running current.
  2. Running current. The voltage across the winding is now the supply minus the back-EMF, 12 V − 8 V = 4 V, so I = 4 V ÷ 2 Ω = 2 A.
  3. Consequences for the switch. The MOSFET carries the stall current whenever the motor starts or jams, so its current rating and its heating from lesson 12 must cover 6 A rather than 2 A. The flyback diode must also handle the coil's switch-off energy at whatever current the motor was drawing when it stopped.

Back-EMF is why a motor's current depends on its speed and why a motor driver is designed around the stall case. It is also the source of the confusion the glossary warns about: inductive kickback comes from the winding's inductance, whereas back-EMF is the voltage the motor generates as it spins. The two are different effects that appear in the same winding.

Try it in CircuitJS

The built-in example is a 5 V supply, a 1 H inductor and a 100 Ω resistor, with a switch that opens the circuit. A small capacitor across the switch is where the spike appears.

  1. Open the example: CircuitJS: inductkick.txt.
  2. Make sure the switch is closed, clicking it if needed, and let the simulation run for about 50 ms of simulated time. The coil's time constant is 1 H ÷ 100 Ω = 10 ms, so five time constants are enough to settle.
  3. Predict the steady current, then select the inductor and read it. A coil with no winding resistance settles to a current set by the supply and the resistor alone, I = V ÷ R.
  4. Open the switch and watch the scope, comparing the spike with the supply voltage. The spike is far larger, because the coil cannot change its current instantly.
  5. Open CircuitJS: inductkick-snub.txt, which adds a 100 Ω and 10 µF snubber across the coil. Let it settle to 50 mA, open the switch, and compare the spike and the release with the first file. The snubber should lower the spike; write down which file releases sooner.
Hint

The steady current is the supply divided by the resistor, 5 V ÷ 100 Ω. If the reading stays at zero, the switch is still open, so click it to close it and wait for the trace to settle.

You measured the steady coil current from its supply and resistor, and you saw the switch-off spike that a flyback diode or a snubber must control. Those are the two checks you make on any relay or motor driver.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

A flyback diode fitted backwards

The relay does not pull in when the pin goes high, the supply sags, and the diode gets hot within seconds of power-up. A correctly fitted flyback diode is reverse biased while the coil is powered and so blocks, but reversed, with its anode at the supply and its cathode at the switched end, it is forward biased and shorts the supply to ground through itself and the switch. Check the band on the diode against the schematic before soldering and test it with a meter in diode mode; the cathode, marked by the band, goes to the positive supply end of the coil.

Protection across the switch instead of the coil

The MOSFET still fails, or the relay releases more slowly than expected while the spike remains visible on a scope. A diode across the switch does not carry the coil's current when the switch opens, because the current needs a path around the coil itself. Move the diode across the coil, as the sidenote on flyback diodes describes.

A scope ground lead that rings

The spike rings at a high frequency, and its amplitude changes when the probe's ground lead moves. A long ground lead adds inductance that resonates with the probe's capacitance, so part of the oscillation on screen comes from the probe rather than the circuit. Use the probe's short ground spring at the tip, and clip the ground to the circuit's ground node, here the MOSFET's source, following the ground-clip rule from lesson 10.

A snubber sized by guesswork

The snubber makes the relay chatter, or it barely changes the spike. Too small a snubber clamps poorly, and one far too large holds the coil current for a long time. Size it with the RC time constant from lesson 13 and compare the spike on a scope before and after.

Inductive loads in real designs

Coils are common on real boards: relays that switch mains or a second supply, solenoids that open valves, and motors for fans and actuators. The professional rule is that every coil gets a protection element across it, chosen for the release time the application needs. Many motor and relay driver ICs include clamp diodes internally, as their datasheets state, which is one more reason to read the datasheet before adding a discrete diode. A reviewer checks the diode's polarity, current rating and voltage rating, and compares the MOSFET's drain-source rating against the supply plus the spike.

On a 3D printer, the fans and the motor drivers are inductive loads designed in the same way: the driver must handle the stall current and every coil must have a protection path. When a printer's fan stops or a stepper driver faults, the protection and the driver's current limit are the first things to check, before the firmware.

Summary

  • An inductor opposes a change in current. Its voltage is V = L × di/dt, and it stores energy as ½ × L × I².
  • When a coil's current is switched off quickly, its voltage rises until something conducts. That spike is inductive kickback, and it can reach hundreds of volts.
  • A flyback diode across the coil, cathode to the positive supply, gives the current a path, and the current then decays with τ = L ÷ R.
  • An RC snubber across the coil absorbs the energy. Its time constant is R × C, and it suits AC coils as well as DC.
  • A motor's back-EMF reduces its running current, so the driver must be designed for the stall current.
  • Check the diode's polarity against the schematic, place the protection across the coil, and measure the spike with a short ground lead.
