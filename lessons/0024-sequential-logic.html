Sequential logic

Gates alone cannot remember anything. A latch adds memory, a flip-flop adds a clock so that all the memory changes at the same moment, and every register in your MCU is a row of flip-flops.

By the end of this lesson you can explain why a clock exists, tell a latch from a flip-flop by when each one copies its input, and explain setup and hold time, then wire a D flip-flop so that the Pico's test bench prints D FLIP-FLOP OK.

Where we are and why it matters

Lesson 23 built circuits whose outputs depend only on their inputs right now. A multiplexer, a decoder and a full adder all work this way, and each one forgets everything the moment its inputs change. Lesson 22 gave you the gates they are made from. This lesson adds the one thing those circuits lack: memory.

Memory is not an optional extra in a microcontroller. The program counter, the status flags and every peripheral control register are bits that must hold their value between clock cycles. Lesson 31 shows the core registers, and lesson 38 shows the memory-mapped registers you write from C. Both are storage built from the circuits in this lesson. When a GPIO output stays high after your gpio_put() returns, a flip-flop is holding that value.

The problem this lesson solves is timing. Storage alone is not enough, because a circuit with many paths of different lengths shows wrong values for a while after its inputs change. You need a way to say "copy the new value now, and only now". That is what the clock and the flip-flop provide, and every real digital design depends on it.

The idea: state needs feedback

A sequential circuit has state. Its outputs depend on its present inputs and also on what it stored earlier. The circuits in lesson 23 had no state, so the same inputs always gave the same outputs. Here the same inputs can give different outputs, depending on history.

The simplest state is one bit, and it is stored by feedback: a gate's output is wired back into its own input, so the circuit has two stable states and stays in whichever one it is in. Two NAND gates with their outputs cross-connected form an SR latch. The letters S and R mean set and reset. Pulsing one input changes the stored bit, and when both inputs are inactive the output holds its value. The loop is what remembers. Remove the feedback and the gates forget at once. Ben Eater's series "Build an 8-bit computer" builds latches like this on breadboard, and Harris and Harris, chapter 3 (Sequential Logic Design) gives the timing rules in full.

Concept 1: the D latch copies while enable is high

An SR latch has two inputs that are awkward to use together. Most designs want one input called D (data) that says what value to store, plus an enable that says when to store it. A D latch does exactly that. While the enable is high, the output Q follows D. When the enable goes low, Q holds whatever D was at that moment.

The word for this behaviour is level-sensitive. The latch reacts to the level of the enable, high or low, and not to a change of it. That has a consequence you must plan for. If D changes at any time while the enable is high, the change reaches Q. A glitch on D, which is a short wrong pulse caused by a slow gate somewhere upstream, passes straight through to the output for as long as the enable stays high.

Concept 2: the D flip-flop copies only on an edge

A flip-flop is built so that it copies D only at one instant. That instant is an edge: a change of the clock signal. A positive-edge flip-flop copies D on the rising edge, the moment the clock changes from 0 to 1. Between edges it holds its value, whatever D does.

The usual internal structure is two latches in series, and this is the key idea. The first latch is transparent while the clock is low, so it follows D during that half of the cycle. The second latch is transparent while the clock is high, so it follows the first latch. When the clock rises, the first latch closes and freezes the value it had. The second latch then opens and passes that frozen value to Q. Q changes once per rising edge and nowhere else. Designers call this a master-slave pair: the first latch is the master, and the second is the slave that copies it. Ben Eater's series shows the same latch-based structure built from logic chips, which is a good way to see why two latches are needed.

Worked example: one clock, two latches

Take a clock with a period of 1 µs, so the clock is high from 0 to 0.5 µs, low from 0.5 to 1 µs, and rising edges fall at 0 µs, 1 µs, 2 µs and so on. Compare two circuits with the same D input: a D latch whose enable is the clock, and a D flip-flop. Suppose D follows this schedule: 0 until 0.4 µs, then 1 from 0.4 to 0.7 µs, then 0 from 0.7 to 1.5 µs, then 1 from 1.5 µs onward. Both outputs start at 0.

  1. 0 to 0.4 µs: D is 0. Both outputs are 0.
  2. 0.4 to 0.5 µs: D is 1 and the clock is high. The latch is open, so its Q becomes 1. The flip-flop is waiting for an edge, so its Q stays 0.
  3. 0.5 µs, the clock falls: the latch closes and holds 1. The flip-flop still holds 0.
  4. 0.7 to 1 µs: D is back to 0, but the clock is low, so the latch stays closed and holds 1. The flip-flop holds 0.
  5. 1 µs, the rising edge: the flip-flop samples D, which is 0, so Q stays 0. The latch opens again and follows D, so its Q falls to 0.
  6. 1.5 µs: D becomes 1 while the clock is falling. The latch is closed at 0 and holds 0. The flip-flop holds 0 too, because no edge has happened.
  7. 2 µs, the next rising edge: the flip-flop samples D, which is now 1, so Q becomes 1. The latch opens and follows D to 1.

The 0.4 to 0.5 µs pulse shows the difference clearly. The latch passed the short pulse to its output, and the flip-flop ignored it, because the flip-flop only looks at D at an edge. The flip-flop's Q changed at 2 µs, the edge where it sampled D, and not at 1.5 µs when D changed. That is the behaviour a synchronous design relies on.

The example also shows a real design habit. You decide what a register holds by asking what D is at the edge, not what D did in between. Every register in a CPU works this way, and it is why a designer can reason about the whole system one clock period at a time.

Why a clock exists

A circuit with many paths of different lengths settles at different times. After its inputs change, its outputs can pass through wrong intermediate values for a while, and the wrong values can be copied into storage if the storage is always open. If every state element copies its input at the same known instant, the designer only has to check that everything settles between two clock edges. That is a synchronous design, and nearly every digital system you will meet uses one.

The clock is a periodic signal. Its period is the time between two rising edges, and its frequency is the inverse of the period. A 1 MHz clock has a period of 1 µs, because 1 ÷ 1 MHz = 1 µs. The RP2040 datasheet uses a system clock of 125 MHz in its bus bandwidth example (RP2040 datasheet, section 2.1). At that frequency one period is 1 ÷ 125 MHz = 8 ns. Each edge of the CPU's clock is a deadline: everything must settle before the next one arrives.

The clock is also what the rest of the system waits for. A peripheral, a bus transfer or a CPU instruction completes on an edge, not in the middle of a change. When you read a register and get a value, you are reading state that was last copied on an edge.

Setup and hold: the timing rules

A flip-flop needs its input to be stable for a short time around the clock edge. Setup time is the time D must be stable before the active edge. Hold time is the time D must stay stable after the edge. Together they form a window around the edge, and the flip-flop only reads D reliably if D does not change inside that window.

Here is what goes wrong when the window is broken. The flip-flop's internal latches need a little time to close, so a change in D just before or just after the edge can leave them partly closed. The output may then settle to the wrong value. In the worst case the flip-flop enters a metastable state, an unstable middle level that lasts for a while before it falls to 0 or 1. Metastability is rare, but it is not impossible, and a design that is correct on paper can fail once in a very large number of edges.

The numbers for setup and hold are not something you guess. Every flip-flop's datasheet gives them with the test conditions, and the timing tables are read in lesson 18. The values are small, a few nanoseconds for many logic families, and they depend on voltage and temperature. The rule you carry forward is simple: the datasheet number, not the simulator, decides whether your timing is safe.

A flip-flop with a setup or hold violation does not necessarily fail loudly. It may take the right value most of the time, and the wrong one once in a million edges. That is why a signal crossing from one clock domain to another needs a synchroniser, and why a simulator that is too fast or too ideal can hide the problem.

Try it in Wokwi

The project in projects/0024-d-flip-flop drives D from GP2 and CLK from GP3, and reads Q on GP10. The test first clocks a 0 into the flip-flop, so its starting state does not matter. Then, for five values of D, it changes D while the clock is low and checks that Q does not change, then makes one rising edge and checks that Q now equals D. Each check prints one line, ok or WRONG.

  1. Open the starter folder in VS Code, build with Ctrl+Shift+B, and start the simulator with F1 → Wokwi: Start Simulator. The serial monitor prints a line for each check and then D FLIP-FLOP FAILED. The "rising edge (must copy D)" lines end in WRONG, because Q never changes when no clock reaches the flip-flop.
  2. Find the missing wire. The clock input of the flip-flop, CLK, must connect to GP3. Add this line to the connections list in diagram.json, then save:
    ["pico:GP3", "ff1:CLK", "blue", []]
  3. In main.c, write the body of pulse_clock(). It must make a rising edge: drive CLK low, then high (this is the edge), then low again, with sleep_us() between the steps. The comments say which is which.
  4. Restart the simulator. The load line should now print ok. You will know it works when the serial monitor prints D FLIP-FLOP OK after the five test steps.
Hint for the edge

The copy happens when CLK goes from 0 to 1. Setting CLK to 1 and then leaving it at 1 is not enough: the next pulse needs a 0 first, so the flip-flop sees a new rising edge. The starter's pulse_clock() ends with CLK at 0 for that reason.

The reference solution is in projects/0024-d-flip-flop/solution. Try first, compare after. The test prints one line per step, so you can see which step fails.

The flip-flop's pins are named D, CLK and Q in this project. Check the pin names on the Wokwi part's documentation page when you edit the connections. Wokwi is a digital simulator, so the test checks the logic of the edge, not the setup and hold times in nanoseconds.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

Mistake 1: the clock is not connected. This is the starter's own bug. Symptom: the "rising edge (must copy D)" lines all end in WRONG with Q unchanged, and the run ends with D FLIP-FLOP FAILED. The "must hold" lines still print ok, which is the misleading part: a flip-flop that never changes also passes the hold checks. Fix: check the connections list for a wire from GP3 to ff1:CLK. A flip-flop with no clock holds whatever it powered up with.

Mistake 2: the pulse leaves the clock high. A common first version of pulse_clock() only writes gpio_put(PIN_CLK, 1). The first call makes a rising edge and the test passes once. Symptom: the load step passes, but the "rising edge (must copy D)" lines print WRONG with Q unchanged whenever D differs from the value Q already holds, so the run ends in D FLIP-FLOP FAILED even though the first edge worked. Fix: drive CLK back to 0 before the next edge. A rising edge needs a 0 immediately before it.

Mistake 3: using a latch where the design needs a flip-flop. A latch is a shorter and cheaper circuit, and it can look right in a quick test. Symptom, on a logic analyzer or oscilloscope rather than in this simulator: Q follows D while the enable is high, so a short glitch on D shows up on Q. Fix: use a flip-flop, or make sure the latch's enable is only high when its input is stable. In firmware this is why a value read from a shared register must not change while another part of the system is copying it.

Mistake 4: changing D right at the edge. Symptom: the register sometimes takes the old value and sometimes the new one, and the failure changes from one run to the next. Fix: change D well before the edge, and never within the setup and hold window. The Wokwi simulator is too ideal to show this, so the check is a datasheet check, not a test.

In real projects

Professionals read setup and hold numbers from the datasheet for every flip-flop that sits between two blocks, and they check them with a static timing analysis tool. The tool reports the worst path between each pair of flip-flops, and the design is not signed off until every path meets its setup and hold limits. Code review does not catch these; the timing report does.

When a signal comes from outside the chip, such as a button or a sensor interrupt line, it is asynchronous. It can change at any time, so it must pass through a synchroniser, two flip-flops in series on the receiving clock. The first flip-flop may go metastable, and the second has a full clock period to settle before the logic uses the value. This is the standard fix, and it is why debouncing and input synchronisation appear in lesson 52.

In an interview, expect the question "what is the difference between a latch and a flip-flop?" and a follow-up about setup and hold. A strong answer names the edge, explains why the master-slave structure is needed, and says what a violation does. Answering only "a flip-flop is edge-triggered" is a weak answer, because it does not explain the design.

Summary

  • A sequential circuit has state. Its outputs depend on its inputs and on what it stored earlier.
  • Feedback stores a bit. Cross-connected NAND gates form an SR latch, and removing the feedback removes the memory.
  • A D latch is level-sensitive: it follows D while its enable is high, so glitches on D reach Q.
  • A D flip-flop copies D only on the rising edge of the clock. Internally it is a master latch and a slave latch in series.
  • A clock makes a design synchronous: everything settles between edges, and the state changes all happen at the same known instants.
  • Setup time is the stable time before the edge, and hold time is the stable time after it. Violating them can give a wrong value or a metastable state.
  • Asynchronous inputs need a synchroniser, two flip-flops in series, before the logic uses them.

"What is the difference between a latch and a flip-flop, and why do synchronous designs use flip-flops?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

A latch is level-sensitive: it follows its input for as long as its enable is high. A flip-flop is edge-triggered: it copies its input only at one clock edge and holds otherwise, built from a master latch and a slave latch. Synchronous designs use flip-flops so that all state changes happen at known instants, and the designer can check that logic settles between edges. The setup and hold times around the edge are the timing rules that must hold for the copy to be reliable.
