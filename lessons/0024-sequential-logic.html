Sequential logic

Latches, flip-flops and the clock that times them

By the end of this lesson you can explain why a clock exists, tell a latch from a flip-flop by when each one copies its input, and explain setup and hold time, then wire a D flip-flop so that the Pico's test bench prints D FLIP-FLOP OK.

Circuits with memory

Lesson 23 built circuits whose outputs depend only on their present inputs. A multiplexer, a decoder and a full adder all work this way, forgetting everything the moment their inputs change, and lesson 22 supplied the gates they are made of. This lesson adds what those circuits lack, which is memory.

Memory is essential in a microcontroller. The program counter, the status flags and every peripheral control register are bits that must hold their value between clock cycles. Lesson 31 shows the core registers and lesson 38 the memory-mapped registers you write from C, and both are storage built from the circuits in this lesson. When a GPIO output stays high after gpio_put() returns, a flip-flop is holding that value.

The real problem here is timing. Storage alone is not enough, because a circuit with paths of different lengths shows wrong values for a while after its inputs change, so the design needs a way to say "copy the new value now, and only now". The clock and the flip-flop provide exactly that.

Feedback and the SR latch

A sequential circuit has state: its outputs depend on its present inputs and also on what it stored earlier. The circuits of lesson 23 had no state, so the same inputs always produced the same outputs, whereas here the same inputs can produce different outputs depending on history.

The simplest state is a single bit, stored by feedback. A gate's output is wired back into its own input, so the circuit has two stable states and stays in whichever it is in. Two NAND gates with cross-connected outputs form an SR latch, where S and R stand for set and reset: pulsing one input changes the stored bit, and with both inputs inactive the output holds its value. The loop is what remembers, and without the feedback the gates forget immediately. Ben Eater's series "Build an 8-bit computer" builds latches like this on a breadboard, and Harris and Harris, chapter 3 (Sequential Logic Design) gives the timing rules in full.

The D latch

An SR latch has two inputs that are awkward to use together. Most designs want a single D (data) input stating what to store, plus an enable stating when to store it, and a D latch provides exactly that. While the enable is high, the output Q follows D, and when the enable goes low, Q holds whatever D was at that moment.

This behaviour is called level-sensitive, because the latch responds to the level of the enable rather than to a change in it. The consequence must be planned for: any change of D while the enable is high reaches Q. A glitch on D, a short wrong pulse caused by a slow gate somewhere upstream, therefore passes straight through to the output for as long as the enable stays high.

The edge-triggered D flip-flop

A flip-flop is built to copy D at a single instant, an edge, which is a change in the clock signal. A positive-edge flip-flop copies D on the rising edge, the moment the clock changes from 0 to 1, and between edges it holds its value whatever D does.

A latch resembles an open window: while the enable is high, whatever moves outside is seen inside, and the last thing seen when the window closes remains. A flip-flop resembles a camera, which records one instant, the clock edge, and ignores everything between shots. The camera picture hides one detail, though, since a real flip-flop needs its subject to hold still briefly around the shot, which is the setup and hold time described later.

The usual internal structure is two latches in series. The first is transparent while the clock is low and so follows D during that half of the cycle, while the second is transparent while the clock is high and follows the first. When the clock rises, the first latch closes and freezes its value, and the second opens and passes that frozen value to Q, so Q changes once per rising edge and at no other time. Designers call this a master-slave pair, with the first latch as the master and the second as the slave that copies it. Ben Eater's series shows the same latch-based structure built from logic chips, which is a good way to see why two latches are needed.

Worked example: a latch and a flip-flop on one clock

Take a clock with a period of 1 µs, high from 0 to 0.5 µs and low from 0.5 to 1 µs, with rising edges at 0 µs, 1 µs, 2 µs and so on. We compare a D latch whose enable is the clock with a D flip-flop, both fed the same D: 0 until 0.4 µs, 1 from 0.4 to 0.7 µs, 0 from 0.7 to 1.5 µs, and 1 from 1.5 µs onwards. Both outputs start at 0, and before reading on it is worth predicting what each output does with the short pulse at 0.4 µs.

  1. 0 to 0.4 µs: D is 0, and both outputs are 0.
  2. 0.4 to 0.5 µs: D is 1 while the clock is high. The latch is open, so its Q becomes 1, whereas the flip-flop is waiting for an edge, so its Q stays 0.
  3. 0.5 µs, the clock falls: the latch closes and holds 1, and the flip-flop still holds 0.
  4. 0.7 to 1 µs: D returns to 0, but the clock is low, so the latch stays closed holding 1, and the flip-flop holds 0.
  5. 1 µs, the rising edge: the flip-flop samples D, which is 0, so its Q stays 0. The latch opens again and follows D, so its Q falls to 0.
  6. 1.5 µs: D becomes 1 at the moment the clock falls. The latch closes at 0 and holds it, and the flip-flop also holds 0, because no rising edge has occurred.
  7. 2 µs, the next rising edge: the flip-flop samples D, now 1, so its Q becomes 1, and the latch opens and follows D to 1.

The pulse from 0.4 to 0.5 µs shows the difference clearly. The latch passed the short pulse to its output, while the flip-flop ignored it, because it looks at D only at an edge. The flip-flop's Q changed at 2 µs, the edge where it sampled D, rather than at 1.5 µs when D changed, and this is the behaviour a synchronous design relies on.

The example also illustrates a real design habit. You decide what a register holds by asking what D is at the edge, not what D did in between. Every register in a CPU works this way, which is why a designer can reason about a whole system one clock period at a time.

The worked example can be run below. The latch and the flip-flop share the same D and the same C, which is the latch's enable and the flip-flop's clock, and each click adds a step to the timing diagram. Set C to 1, then pulse D to 1 and back to 0 while C stays at 1, and the latch output follows the pulse while the flip-flop output does not. Then set D to 1 with C at 0 and raise C, and the flip-flop takes the new value only at that rising edge.

Clocks and synchronous design

A circuit with many paths of different lengths settles at different times. After its inputs change, its outputs can pass through wrong intermediate values, and storage that is always open would capture them. If every state element copies its input at the same known instant, the designer only has to check that everything settles between two clock edges. This is called a synchronous design, and nearly every digital system you will meet uses one.

The clock is a periodic signal whose period T is the time between two rising edges and whose frequency is

f = 1 ÷ T(1)

where f is in hertz when T is in seconds, so a 1 MHz clock has a period of 1 µs. The RP2040 datasheet uses a system clock of 125 MHz in its bus bandwidth example (RP2040 datasheet, section 2.1), for which one period is 1 ÷ 125 MHz = 8 ns. Each edge of the CPU's clock is a deadline by which everything must have settled.

The clock is also what the rest of the system waits for. A peripheral, a bus transfer or a CPU instruction completes on an edge rather than in the middle of a change, so a value read from a register is state that was last copied on an edge.

Setup time, hold time and metastability

A flip-flop needs its input to be stable for a short time around the clock edge. Setup time is how long D must be stable before the active edge, and hold time how long it must stay stable after the edge. Together they form a window around the edge, and the flip-flop reads D reliably only if D does not change inside it.

Setup and hold return to the camera. The subject must stand still for a moment before the shutter opens and a moment after, or the photo blurs. A flip-flop whose input moves inside that window can catch a blurred value, neither a clean 0 nor a clean 1, for a while, a state called metastability. A blurred photo stays blurred, however, whereas a metastable flip-flop eventually settles to 0 or 1, only without any guarantee of which or when.

The failure mechanism is internal. The flip-flop's latches need a little time to close, so a change in D just before or after the edge can leave them partly closed, and the output may then settle to the wrong value. In the worst case the flip-flop enters a metastable state, an unstable middle level that persists for a while before falling to 0 or 1. Metastability is rare but not impossible, so a design that is correct on paper can still fail once in a very large number of edges.

Setup and hold values are never guessed. Every flip-flop's datasheet gives them with their test conditions, read in the way lesson 18 described for timing tables. The values are small, a few nanoseconds for many logic families, and they depend on voltage and temperature, so the datasheet number decides whether a design's timing is safe.

A flip-flop with a setup or hold violation does not necessarily fail loudly. It may take the right value most of the time and the wrong one once in a million edges, which is why a signal crossing from one clock domain to another needs a synchroniser, and why a simulator that is too fast or too ideal can hide the problem.
  1. A rising clock edge at the dashed line, and D going from 0 to 1 well before it.
  2. Around the edge is a window: D must be stable for the setup time before the edge and the hold time after it. The datasheet gives both numbers, with their conditions.
  3. D was stable through the whole window, so Q takes the new value cleanly, a short delay after the edge.
  4. Now D changes inside the window. The internal latches are caught half-closed, and Q can hover at a middle level, metastable, before it falls to a value. Here it falls to 0, the old value.
  5. The same violation on another edge can settle to 1. Which one, and how long it takes, is not predictable. That is why a signal from another clock domain goes through a synchroniser before the logic uses it.

Try it in Wokwi

The project in projects/0024-d-flip-flop drives D from GP2 and CLK from GP3 and reads Q on GP10. The test first clocks a 0 into the flip-flop, so that its starting state does not matter. Then, for five values of D, it changes D while the clock is low and checks that Q stays unchanged, makes one rising edge, and checks that Q now equals D. Each check prints one line ending in ok or WRONG.

  1. Open the starter folder in VS Code, build with Ctrl+Shift+B, and start the simulator with F1 → Wokwi: Start Simulator. The serial monitor prints a line for each check and then D FLIP-FLOP FAILED. The "rising edge (must copy D)" lines end in WRONG, because Q never changes when no clock reaches the flip-flop.
  2. Add the missing wire. The flip-flop's clock input, CLK, must connect to GP3, so add this line to the connections list in diagram.json and save:
    ["pico:GP3", "ff1:CLK", "blue", []]
  3. In main.c, write the body of pulse_clock(). It must make a rising edge by driving CLK low, then high (this is the edge), then low again, with sleep_us() between the steps; the comments mark which is which.
  4. Restart the simulator. The load line should now print ok, and it works when the serial monitor prints D FLIP-FLOP OK after the five test steps.
Hint for the edge

The copy happens when CLK goes from 0 to 1. Setting CLK to 1 and leaving it there is not enough, because the next pulse needs a 0 first so that the flip-flop sees a new rising edge, which is why the starter's pulse_clock() ends with CLK at 0.

The reference solution is in projects/0024-d-flip-flop/solution; try first and compare afterwards. The test prints one line per step, so you can see which step fails.

The flip-flop's pins are named D, CLK and Q in this project, so check the pin names on the Wokwi part's documentation page when editing the connections. Wokwi is a digital simulator, so the test checks the logic of the edge, not the setup and hold times in nanoseconds.
You built a D flip-flop test that separates the two behaviours that matter, holding between edges and copying on an edge. You can now explain why a clock makes synchronous design possible and what setup and hold times mean for the datasheet numbers that lesson 18 taught you to read.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

An unconnected clock

This is the starter's own bug. The "rising edge (must copy D)" lines all end in WRONG with Q unchanged, and the run ends with D FLIP-FLOP FAILED, while the "must hold" lines still print ok, which is the misleading part, because a flip-flop that never changes also passes the hold checks. Check the connections list for a wire from GP3 to ff1:CLK, since a flip-flop with no clock holds whatever it powered up with.

A pulse that leaves the clock high

A common first version of pulse_clock() only writes gpio_put(PIN_CLK, 1), so the first call makes a rising edge and the test passes once. After that, the load step passes but the "rising edge (must copy D)" lines print WRONG with Q unchanged whenever D differs from the value Q already holds, and the run ends in D FLIP-FLOP FAILED even though the first edge worked. Drive CLK back to 0 before the next edge, since a rising edge needs a 0 immediately before it.

A latch where the design needs a flip-flop

A latch is a smaller and cheaper circuit and can look correct in a quick test. On a logic analyzer or oscilloscope, though not in this simulator, Q follows D while the enable is high, so a short glitch on D appears on Q. Use a flip-flop, or make sure the latch's enable is high only while its input is stable. In firmware the same principle explains why a value read from a shared register must not change while another part of the system is copying it.

D changed right at the edge

The register sometimes takes the old value and sometimes the new one, and the failure changes from run to run. Change D well before the edge and never within the setup and hold window. The Wokwi simulator is too ideal to show this, so the check is made against the datasheet.

Timing analysis and synchronisers in real designs

Professionals read setup and hold numbers from the datasheet for every flip-flop that sits between two blocks, and check them with a static timing analysis tool. The tool reports the worst path between each pair of flip-flops, and a design is not signed off until every path meets its setup and hold limits, so the timing report is what catches violations.

A signal from outside the chip, such as a button or a sensor's interrupt line, is asynchronous and can change at any time, so it must pass through a synchroniser, two flip-flops in series on the receiving clock. The first flip-flop may go metastable, and the second gives it a full clock period to settle before the logic uses the value. This is the standard remedy, and it is why debouncing and input synchronisation appear in lesson 52.

Summary

  • A sequential circuit has state, so its outputs depend on its inputs and on what it stored earlier.
  • Feedback stores a bit. Cross-connected NAND gates form an SR latch, and removing the feedback removes the memory.
  • A D latch is level-sensitive and follows D while its enable is high, so glitches on D reach Q.
  • A D flip-flop copies D only on the rising edge of the clock, and internally it is a master latch and a slave latch in series.
  • A clock makes a design synchronous: everything settles between edges, and all state changes happen at the same known instants.
  • Setup time is the stable time before the edge and hold time the stable time after it, and violating either can give a wrong value or a metastable state.
  • Asynchronous inputs need a synchroniser, two flip-flops in series, before the logic uses them.
