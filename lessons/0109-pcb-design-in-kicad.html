PCB design in KiCad

From schematic to manufacturing files

By the end of this lesson you can outline a KiCad workflow for a small two-layer board, explain what ERC and DRC each check, place a decoupling capacitor and route a ground return correctly, and review a schematic for the mistakes a first board usually has.

Circuits that need a printed board

Every circuit in Parts 1 to 8 ran on a breadboard, a Wokwi diagram or a dev board, whereas a product needs the same circuit on a printed board that someone has to draw. Lesson 17 taught you to read a schematic and lesson 18 to read the datasheet giving a part's pins and package, and this lesson turns those two skills around, so that you draw the schematic, pick each part's package from its datasheet and lay the copper out so that the circuit works on a real board.

The job comes up in the capstone, Part 10 building a filament-dryer controller (lesson 113) whose heater driver, thermistor input and Pico socket need a board, and a firmware engineer who can read a layout can spot a bad heater return, a noisy ADC ground or a missing decoupling capacitor in a colleague's board, a review that catches many hardware bugs before a board is ordered.

This lesson is optional, being useful if you want to design your own breakout boards or to read a hardware team's schematics and layout reviews, and since KiCad is not installed in this course and no hardware is needed, the exercises are concept questions, although the review skill is worth having either way.

From schematic to copper

A printed circuit board is a schematic turned into copper, the schematic saying which pins connect (the netlist) and the layout where the parts sit and how the copper runs. KiCad is a free, open-source suite that does both, adding the rule checks and the output files a manufacturer needs, with Eeschema as its schematic editor and Pcbnew as its board editor.

The workflow has six stages, each producing something the next stage uses.

StageWhat you doWhat it checks or produces
1. Schematic capturePlace symbols, wire them, label power and groundThe circuit, in the form lesson 17 teaches you to read
2. Symbols and footprintsGive each symbol a footprint: the copper pads and outline for that partThe part's package and pad layout, from its datasheet (lesson 18)
3. ERCRun the electrical rules checkUnconnected pins, conflicting outputs, missing power flags
4. NetlistExport the list of connectionsThe connection list the board must match
5. LayoutPlace parts, route copper, add a ground pourThe physical board, the design rules applied
6. DRC and outputRun the design rules check, then export Gerbers and the drill fileClearance and width violations, and the files the factory builds from

The Eeschema manual for KiCad 9 describes the schematic stages, choosing symbols, assigning footprints, the electrical rules check, the netlist export and annotation of reference designators KiCad 9.0 documentation, Eeschema. ERC checks the schematic against electrical rules, not the board. A clean ERC does not mean the layout is right, and a DRC pass does not mean the circuit is right. Run both.

Schematics, footprints and Gerber files

A schematic is the drawing of a circuit as symbols joined by wires, one symbol per part, the circuit's connections forming its netlist, the list of which pins are joined, while a PCB (printed circuit board) is the physical board, a stack of copper layers bonded to an insulating core with the parts soldered to it. Each symbol is linked to a footprint, the pattern of copper pads and outline the part is soldered onto, which comes from the package drawing in the datasheet, so a wrong footprint is a datasheet-reading error.

On the board, a trace is a strip of copper carrying a signal and a ground pour a large area of copper filled with ground, while a Gerber file describes one layer of the board in a format the factory can read, terms the rest of the lesson uses and the glossary defines.

The capstone breakout board

The capstone (Part 10) is a filament-dryer controller, and this breakout board is a small piece of it, a Pico socket, a thermistor input and a heater driver, which makes a good first design, because each block uses something from Part 1.

  • NTC input divider. A fixed resistor from 3V3 to the ADC pin, and the NTC from the pin to ground. This is the divider of lesson 8 with the thermistor as the lower arm of lesson 58. A pull-up of 4.7 kΩ is what Klipper's own thermistor configuration assumes Klipper Config Reference, so it is a sensible starting value for a printer-style sensor. The ADC pin should carry only the divider, with a 100 nF capacitor across it to filter fast noise (lesson 13).
  • MOSFET heater driver. A logic-level N-channel MOSFET, low-side, switches the heater's return to ground (lesson 12). The gate gets a series resistor against ringing and a pull-down so the heater stays off during reset. Check the MOSFET's RDS(on) at the gate voltage the Pico gives (3.3 V), not at the datasheet's headline number.
  • Flyback diode. A heater is resistive, so it does not need a flyback diode: no energy is stored in a magnetic field that must go somewhere. A fan, a solenoid or a relay on the same board is inductive, and it does need one (lesson 14). Put it across the load, cathode to the positive supply, as close to the load as the layout allows. Adding the diode to a resistive heater is harmless, but it is not the reason the board works.
  • Pico header. Two rows of pin sockets at the Pico's 2.54 mm pitch, so the board takes the Pico as a plug-in module. Check the Pico datasheet's pin names, not the silkscreen of a clone.

Worked example: the NTC input, with numbers

The thermistor input from the breakout list has a 4.7 kΩ fixed resistor from 3V3 to the ADC pin and a 100 kΩ NTC from the pin to ground, realistic numbers, since Klipper's sensor list includes 100 kΩ NTC thermistors (Klipper Config Reference). The NTC is the lower arm of the divider from lesson 8, so the pin voltage is

Vpin = 3.3 V × RNTC ÷ (Rfixed + RNTC)(1)

where Rfixed is the 4.7 kΩ pull-up and RNTC the thermistor's present resistance. Before following the steps, predict the ADC codes for an unplugged and a shorted NTC.

  1. Room temperature. At 25 °C the NTC is 100 kΩ, so the pin voltage is 3.3 × 100 ÷ 104.7 = 3.15 V, which the ADC converts to a code of 3.15 ÷ 3.3 × 4095 = 3911, using the 3.3 V reference and the 4095 top code from lesson 58.
  2. Warming. As the NTC warms, its resistance falls, so the pin voltage falls and the code with it.
  3. The two extremes. With the NTC unplugged, the pin is pulled to 3.3 V and the code is 4095, and with the NTC shorted, the pin is at ground and the code is 0, a good bench test before any firmware runs.

A board reading anything else at those two extremes has a fault in the divider or the connections, which a multimeter (lesson 10) can find before the board ever reaches the firmware.

Decoupling capacitors at the power pins

A decoupling capacitor is a local reservoir of charge, supplying the short current bursts a chip takes when its transistors switch, so that the supply does not sag, and it must sit close to the pin it serves, because the trace between them adds inductance and makes the burst arrive late. The RP2040 hardware design guide asks for 100 nF per power pin placed close to the pins, plus 1 µF on each side of its internal regulator, stressing that placement is what matters, so the capacitor must sit close to its pin Hardware design with RP2040, section 2.1.2 and 2.1.3.

A decoupling capacitor placed 10 mm from its pin does little against fast edges. Put it on the same side of the board as the pin, with the shortest trace to the power and ground pads.

Two-layer layout rules

A two-layer board has a top and a bottom copper layer and no inner planes, the RP2040 guide's own minimal design being such a board, whose section on making a PCB notes that the design rules are relaxed to keep fabrication cheap Hardware design with RP2040, section 2.7, and the rules that matter most on such a board are the following.

  1. Pour ground on the bottom layer. A solid ground pour gives every signal a low-impedance return path directly beneath it. Route signals on the top layer, and keep the bottom layer clear of signal tracks where you can. The RP2040 guide's minimal design does this: it states that almost the entire bottom copper layer is ground (Hardware design with RP2040, section 2.4.1).
  2. Keep the heater current loop separate. The heater's high current flows from the supply, through the heater, through the MOSFET and back to ground. Make that loop short and wide, and keep the ADC divider's ground connection away from it, so the heater's current does not add a voltage error to the thermistor reading.
  3. Route high-speed or noisy lines over solid copper. A trace that crosses a gap in the ground pour has a long return path and radiates. Move the trace or fill the gap.
  4. Keep the crystal and its load capacitors close to the chip. The guide's section 2.3 shows the crystal with two load capacitors, C2 and C3, each from one crystal terminal to ground, and it asks for them to be placed with the crystal.

The first and third rules rest on the return current, because every current that goes out also comes back, and on a board the return runs in the ground plane right under its trace.

The return current behaves like a shadow of its trace, following it through a solid plane, whereas a slot cut through the plane forces the shadow to go round it, so the loop grows, and a bigger loop radiates and picks up noise like an antenna. The comparison breaks at low frequencies, though, because a slow or steady current does not hug its trace but spreads through the plane along the path of least resistance, so the shadow picture holds only for fast edges.

Design rules set the minimum track width and clearance for the fabricator, numbers that come from your manufacturer's capability sheet rather than KiCad's defaults, and DRC checks the layout against the rules you set as well as checking that every pad is connected as the netlist says, so a missing connection is a DRC error as well as a clearance problem (KiCad 9.0 documentation, Pcbnew, design rules checking). A rule that is wrong for your factory passes a board the factory rejects, so read the capability sheet before tuning DRC.

Gerber files and ordering

The factory does not take the KiCad project but Gerber files, one per copper layer, mask and silkscreen, plus a drill file, together with a bill of materials and a pick-and-place file if you want assembly, all exported from the board editor's plot dialog. Check the files in a Gerber viewer before ordering, because a missing layer or a shifted drill file is the most common first-board mistake, while panelisation, copper weight and board thickness are chosen at ordering time.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

A first board fails in a few predictable ways, each mistake below showing a symptom in one of the checks or the bench test and each fix being a short step in the workflow.

A power net without a power flag

ERC flags the 3V3 pin of the Pico socket even though the wire is there, the Eeschema manual noting that ERC often reports "Input Power pin not driven by any Output Power pins" on power pins that look connected, because the power arrives through a connector or a part not marked as a power output. Place a power flag on the rail, which tells ERC that the net has a source, remembering that the flag creates no voltage, so the regulator still has to be wired correctly.

A wire crossing another wire

A net is named on one sheet but has a gap, or a pin that the drawing seems to join stays unconnected, because, as the Eeschema manual says, only wire ends create connections and a wire crossing the middle of another makes none. End the wires on the pins or on each other and place a junction where a joint is meant to be, looking for the small dot as well as the lines.

A footprint that does not match the datasheet

DRC shows a pad that is not on the netlist as unconnected, or at the bench a pin wired to the heater does not reach its pad, because a footprint with a mirrored or renumbered pad passes ERC, which checks the schematic's electrical rules rather than whether the footprint's pads match the part. Compare the footprint's pad numbers with the package drawing in the datasheet, the reading skill from lesson 18.

Default design rules instead of the factory's

The factory rejects a board that passed DRC for a track or drill below its minimum, or the fabricator asks for a change before starting, because a pass with KiCad's default track width and clearance proves nothing for your factory. Copy the minimum track width, clearance and drill sizes from the capability sheet into the board setup before the first DRC run.

A decoupling capacitor far from its pin

The board runs and then resets intermittently when a heater or a motor switches on, because a capacitor on a long trace delivers its charge late, so the supply still dips when a burst of switching arrives, which is why the RP2040 guide asks for 100 nF close to each power pin and lesson 13 explained the reason. Place the capacitor on the same side of the board as the pin, with the shortest trace to the power and ground pads.

Board reviews in a hardware team

A hardware team reviews a board in a fixed order before ordering it, first the schematic, with ERC clean and every power net, every connector and every heater path checked by hand, then the layout against the same schematic, with DRC clean against the factory's rules and the decoupling, the heater return and the ADC ground checked by eye, and only then are the Gerbers exported and checked in a viewer. Each step catches a different class of error, and no step replaces the others.

Firmware engineers take part in the review, because the board is where firmware assumptions are made, the pin the firmware uses as an ADC input having to stay clear of any trace carrying heater current and the pin driving a MOSFET having to match the logic-level part the schematic shows (lesson 12), so a reviewer who reads the netlist against the firmware's pin map catches a whole class of bugs that no simulator sees.

Summary

  • A schematic is the circuit as symbols and wires, and its netlist is the list of connected pins that the board must match.
  • ERC checks the schematic's electrical rules, such as unconnected pins, conflicting outputs and missing power flags, while DRC checks the layout against the board's rules and the netlist.
  • A two-layer board routes signals on the top and pours ground on the bottom, keeping the heater return away from the ADC ground.
  • Each 100 nF decoupling capacitor sits on the same side of the board as its pin, with the shortest trace to power and ground.
  • The design rules come from the factory's capability sheet before DRC can be trusted.
  • The Gerber files are checked in a viewer before ordering, because a missing layer is the most common first-board mistake.
