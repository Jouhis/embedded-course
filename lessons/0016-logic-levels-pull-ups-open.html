Logic levels, pull-ups, open-drain, level shifting

Input thresholds and the resistors that set a pin's default level

By the end of this lesson you can state the RP2040's input thresholds at 3.3 V, enable an internal pull-up with the Pico SDK, and explain why a floating input and a 5 V signal on a 3.3 V pin are both dangerous.

Voltages at a GPIO pin

Lesson 5 introduced voltage and current, lesson 8 showed how two resistors set a voltage, lesson 9 why a series resistor limits an LED's current, and lesson 11 how a transistor works as a fully on or fully off switch. This lesson brings those ideas together at a microcontroller pin. A GPIO pin is a digital input or output: the chip reads the voltage on it as 0 or 1 and drives it as 0 or 1, and both depend on the resistors and the supply around the pin, whose 3.3 V source lesson 15 described.

The trouble starts with the first real switch. A pushbutton on a pin reads 1 one moment and 0 the next even when nobody touches it, a bug you will meet in a 3D printer's endstop, a door sensor or a rotary encoder. The second problem is voltage, since a sensor with a 5 V output cannot be wired straight to a pin powered at 3.3 V. Both problems come down to logic levels, and both have a standard fix.

Logic levels and input thresholds

A logic level is the range of voltages a device reads as 0 or as 1. A digital input has two thresholds: VIH, the minimum voltage it reads as high, and VIL, the maximum it reads as low. Between them lies a band where the input is not guaranteed to read either value. The subscripts are standard datasheet notation, with IH meaning input high and IL input low.

The RP2040's supply for its digital pins is called IOVDD, which is 3.3 V on a Pico, and the datasheet gives the input thresholds for each IOVDD voltage. At IOVDD = 3.3 V, VIH is at least 2 V and VIL at most 0.8 V, and the hysteresis, the extra margin a Schmitt-trigger input adds, is 0.2 V (RP2040 datasheet, Table 625, Digital IO characteristics). The thresholds are fractions of the IO supply only at some voltages. The datasheet gives a formula, 0.65 × IOVDD for VIH, in the 1.8 V row, but at 3.3 V the table gives a fixed minimum of 2 V instead, so read the row for your supply.

A Schmitt trigger is an input circuit with two thresholds, one for a rising signal and one for a falling one, and the hysteresis is the gap between them. A slow or noisy edge that crosses one threshold cannot flip the input back and forth, because it must cross the other threshold to change it again. A button read through a Schmitt input therefore gives one clean change.

Output levels and noise margins

A driven output has limits of its own. At IOVDD = 3.3 V the RP2040 guarantees a high output of at least 2.62 V and a low output of at most 0.5 V (RP2040 datasheet, Table 625), and these are the figures that matter when one chip drives another. Since the receiving input needs at least 2.0 V for a high, a 2.62 V output leaves 0.62 V of margin, and a 0.5 V low stays under the 0.8 V threshold with 0.3 V to spare. A signal within these margins works on the bench and in the field, whereas one that sits between them does not.

Floating inputs and pull-up resistors

A floating input is connected to nothing that sets its level, as happens with an open pushbutton or a loose wire. Its voltage is then determined by stray charge, leakage and nearby noise, so it wanders, and it can sit in the undefined band of Figure 1 and read 0 or 1 at random.

A pull-up resistor connects the line to the supply through a resistance, so when nothing else drives the line, it holds it high and the input reads 1. A button wired from the pin to ground overpowers the pull-up when pressed and pulls the line to 0. The pull-up must therefore be weak enough for the button to win and strong enough that noise cannot pull the line low by accident. The RP2040 has internal pull-ups of 50 to 80 kΩ and internal pull-downs of the same size (RP2040 datasheet, Table 625, Pull-Up and Pull-Down Resistance).

The current involved is small. With a pressed button holding the pin at 0 V, the current through the strongest case, a 50 kΩ pull-up, is

I = VIOVDD ÷ Rpull = 3.3 V ÷ 50 kΩ = 66 µA(1)

where Rpull is the pull-up resistance. That small current is why an internal pull-up suits a button but not a long wire to a distant sensor, which needs a stronger external resistor.

A pull-down resistor does the opposite job and holds a line low. The reset state of a GPIO matters here: the pins listed in Table 615, GPIO0 to GPIO27, all reset with a pull-down enabled (RP2040 datasheet, Table 615, GPIO pins). An unconfigured pin therefore reads low rather than floating, but that is a property of reset rather than a design decision, so set the pull you need in the firmware instead of relying on it.

A floating input is like a door with no latch, swung by every draught so that you cannot tell whether it is open or shut. A pull-up is a door spring that holds the door shut until someone pushes and closes it again as soon as they let go. The spring is deliberately weak so that a push, the button, easily wins. The comparison stops at the electrical cost, since a pressed button keeps a small current flowing through the pull-up for as long as it is held.

Open-drain outputs

An open-drain output can only pull a line low or let go and leave it high impedance, meaning it neither drives nor sinks current, and a pull-up resistor supplies the high level. Many shared buses use this arrangement, including I2C. Because each device only ever pulls low, two devices cannot fight: if one pulls low, the line is low for both, and if both release, the pull-up brings it high (NXP UM10204, the I2C-bus specification).

Picture the shared line as a rope held up by a spring, the pull-up. Anyone on the line can pull the rope down, but nobody can push it up, only let go, so if two people pull at once nothing breaks and the rope is simply down. With push-pull outputs, one side could push up while another pulled down, and the two drivers would fight, which is why shared buses such as I²C use open-drain. The picture leaves out the time the spring takes to lift the rope again, which is the rise time discussed next.

Take a line with a 4.7 kΩ pull-up to 3.3 V. With every device released, the pull-up holds the line at 3.3 V and only the small leakage of the inputs flows. When one device pulls the line low, the pull-up current is 3.3 V ÷ 4.7 kΩ = 0.70 mA and the line sits near 0 V. No device ever has to supply a high level, so none can be damaged by the others. The price is that the line rises slowly through the pull-up, which limits the bus speed, a real design trade-off for which the bus specification gives the limits.

  1. Every device has released the line: their outputs are open switches. Only the pull-up connects to the line, so it holds the line at 3.3 V and the reader sees 1. No current flows.
  2. Device A closes its switch to ground. The line falls near 0 V, and 3.3 V ÷ 4.7 kΩ = 0.70 mA flows through the pull-up and into A.
  3. A releases and B pulls low instead. The line is low again: any one device can make it 0.
  4. Both pull low at once. Nothing fights: two switches to ground just share the 0.70 mA. Two push-pull outputs driving opposite levels would short the supply.
  5. When the last device lets go, the pull-up brings the line back to 3.3 V. It rises at a rate set by the pull-up and the line's capacitance, which is why the resistor value matters on a fast bus.

5 V signals and level shifting

A PC's USB port supplies 5 V, and many sensors output 5 V. The RP2040's absolute maximum rating for any digital pin is IOVDD + 0.5 V, which is 3.8 V at an IOVDD of 3.3 V (RP2040 datasheet, Table 622, absolute maximum ratings). An absolute maximum is a stress limit rather than an operating value, and a 5 V signal on a 3.3 V pin exceeds it and can damage the pin.

The datasheet also describes fault-tolerant pins, marked FT, into which very little current flows when the pin is below 3.63 V and IOVDD is 0 V (RP2040 datasheet, Table 614, pin types). That protects the pin while the chip is unpowered, which is a different case, and it does not make a 5 V signal safe while the chip is running, because Table 622 still limits every pin to IOVDD + 0.5 V. Every RP2040 pin should therefore be treated as a 3.3 V pin.

A 5 V signal needs a level shifter, which may be a resistor divider, a bidirectional MOSFET shifter or a translator IC. A simple divider works for a one-way signal from a source to the pin. With 2.2 kΩ on top and 3.3 kΩ to ground, the pin sees

Vpin = 5 V × 3.3 kΩ ÷ (2.2 kΩ + 3.3 kΩ) = 3.0 V(2)

when the source is at 5 V, which is above the 2.0 V high threshold and below the 3.8 V absolute maximum, so the pin reads a clean high and is safe. The divider draws 5 V ÷ 5.5 kΩ = 0.9 mA while the source is high, and that current is part of the design. Lesson 8 covers the divider, and lesson 9 explains why a series resistor also limits current.

A divider suits only a one-way input. A bidirectional line such as I2C needs a shifter that can drive in both directions, which no resistor pair can do, so a translator IC or a MOSFET shifter is the right part there.

Setting the pulls in C

The pull setting in the solution is one small function, which translates the hardware idea into an SDK call. 

static void configure_pulls(bool pull_up) {
    if (pull_up) {
        gpio_pull_up(BUTTON_PIN);
    } else {
        gpio_disable_pulls(BUTTON_PIN);
    }
}
  • static makes the function private to this file, which is how the course writes helpers, so nothing outside main.c can call it.
  • bool pull_up selects the phase. The function acts on the decision without keeping any state of its own, so main() owns the timing.
  • gpio_pull_up(BUTTON_PIN) is the Pico SDK call that enables the internal pull-up on that pin. In the SDK it is a small inline function that calls gpio_set_pulls(gpio, true, false), which sets the up enable and clears the down enable (Pico SDK 2.3.1, hardware/gpio.h).
  • gpio_disable_pulls(BUTTON_PIN) calls gpio_set_pulls(gpio, false, false), clearing both enables so that the pin has no internal pull at all, which is the state this phase is meant to show.

The rest of main is the usual GPIO sequence. gpio_init claims the pin for software control, and gpio_set_dir(BUTTON_PIN, GPIO_IN) makes it an input so the RP2040 does not drive it. gpio_get returns the logic level as a bool, which the code prints as 1 for true, and sleep_ms(SAMPLE_MS) slows the loop to one reading every 250 ms so that the serial monitor can be read by eye.

You enabled an internal pull-up from C, read a button through it, and switched the pin's pulls at runtime while watching the logic level. That is the check to make before wiring any input.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

A button wired to 3.3 V with the pull-up on

The serial log reads 1 all the time, pressed or not. The pull-up holds the pin high, and the pressed button connects it to 3.3 V, the same level, so nothing changes. Wire the button to ground as the project does, or switch to a pull-down with the button on the supply side, and check the wiring first.

A floating pin because no pull was set

The log flips between 0 and 1 with no button press, and the flicker changes when you touch the wires. The pin has nothing setting its level. Add a pull, either the internal one set in code or an external resistor, chosen so that the default state is the one you want.

Relying on the reset state

A spurious button event appears at start-up. An unconfigured pin reads low after reset because of the reset pull-down, so a button that should read high when released reads low until the firmware sets a pull-up, and the first milliseconds of the program look like a press. Set the pull in main before the loop, as the solution does.

A 5 V sensor wired straight to a 3.3 V pin

The pin reads wrong or stops working, and the damage is permanent, although the code may work on the bench for a while, which makes the mistake easy to miss. Use the divider from this lesson or a shifter, and check the sensor's output voltage with a meter before connecting it.

Input configuration in real projects

Professionals set every pull explicitly, in the firmware or on the schematic, instead of relying on reset states or on a pin that happens to work. A code reviewer looks for an explicit pull on every input, a comment explaining why it is needed, and a check that any external resistor value matches the bus. A printer's endstop switch is a good example, since its pin needs a pull set in software and its wiring is checked against the schematic when the board is built.

Summary

  • At IOVDD = 3.3 V, the RP2040 reads a high at 2.0 V or more and a low at 0.8 V or less, and the band from 0.8 V to 2.0 V is not guaranteed.
  • A floating input has no defined level and reads 0 or 1 at random, and a pull-up or pull-down sets its default.
  • The RP2040's internal pulls are 50 to 80 kΩ, and a GPIO resets with a pull-down.
  • An open-drain output only pulls low, so a shared line with a pull-up is safe when several devices drive it.
  • The absolute maximum pin voltage is IOVDD + 0.5 V, or 3.8 V at 3.3 V, so a 5 V signal exceeds the limit.
  • A resistor divider level-shifts a one-way signal, for example from 5 V to 3.0 V with 2.2 kΩ over 3.3 kΩ.
  • The SDK's gpio_pull_up and gpio_disable_pulls set the internal pulls from C.
