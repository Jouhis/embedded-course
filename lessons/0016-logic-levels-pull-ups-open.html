Logic levels, pull-ups, and open-drain outputs

A button on a floating pin reads 0 or 1 at random. A pull-up resistor, or the chip's internal one, fixes that, and it also sets the logic level that the pin sees.

By the end of this lesson you can state the RP2040's input thresholds at 3.3 V, enable an internal pull-up with the Pico SDK, and explain why a floating input and a 5 V signal on a 3.3 V pin are both dangerous.

The idea

A logic level is the voltage range a device reads as 0 or 1. An input has two thresholds: VIH, the minimum voltage that reads as high, and VIL, the maximum voltage that reads as low. Between them is a region where the input is not guaranteed to read either value. For the RP2040 at IO voltage 3.3 V, the Schmitt-trigger inputs have VIH of at least 0.65 × 3.3 V = 2.15 V and VIL of at most 0.35 × 3.3 V = 1.16 V (RP2040 datasheet, Table 625, Digital IO characteristics). The thresholds are fractions of the IO supply, not fixed volts. A 1.8 V IO supply moves them down.

Floating inputs and pull-ups

A floating input is connected to nothing that sets its level. A button that is open, or a wire that is not connected, is floating. Its voltage wanders with noise and leakage, so the reading flips between 0 and 1. A pull-up resistor ties the line to the supply, so the line reads 1 when nothing else drives it. Pressing the button connects the line to ground and pulls it to 0. The RP2040 has an internal pull-up, which the Pico SDK enables with gpio_pull_up(). Its resistance is 50 to 80 kΩ (RP2040 datasheet, Table 625). A pull-up is weak on purpose. Pressing the button must win against it easily, and the pull-up's current is 3.3 V ÷ 50 kΩ = 66 µA at most.

Pins also have a reset state. Most GPIOs reset to a pull-down (RP2040 datasheet, Table 615), so a pin you never configure is not floating in the normal case. Do not rely on that. Set the pull you need.

Open-drain outputs

An open-drain output can only pull a line low, or let go and leave it high impedance. A pull-up resistor supplies the high level. Many shared lines, such as I2C, use this so that two devices cannot fight: each one only pulls low. The pull-up's supply sets the high level the line reaches, so the pull-up must go to a supply that every receiving pin tolerates. The section on 5 V tolerance explains why 5 V is not safe on a 3.3 V pin.

5 V tolerance and level shifting

Your PC's USB port gives 5 V, and many sensors output 5 V. The RP2040's absolute maximum rating for a pin voltage is IOVDD + 0.5 V, which is 3.8 V at IOVDD 3.3 V (RP2040 datasheet, Table 622). A 5 V signal on a 3.3 V pin is therefore over the limit, and the pin can be damaged. The RP2040 pins are not 5 V tolerant. Its fault-tolerant pins only tolerate 3.63 V with IOVDD off.

To connect a 5 V signal, use a level shifter: a resistor divider, a bidirectional MOSFET shifter, or a dedicated translator IC. A simple divider for a one-way 5 V input is two resistors whose ratio gives the pin a safe voltage. Lesson 8 covers the divider, and lesson 9 covers why a series resistor also limits current.

Hint for the phases

Keep a counter of the readings and a bool that says which phase you are in. When the counter reaches 20, flip the bool, call the matching pull function, and reset the counter. At 250 ms per reading, 20 readings take 5 seconds. Do not put sleep_ms inside the phase logic, because the loop already sleeps once per reading.

"Why is a floating input a bug, even if it sometimes reads the right value?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

A floating input has no defined voltage, so it can sit in the undefined band between VIL and VIH, where the input may read either value and may draw extra current. It also picks up noise, so the reading changes with the environment. A test that passes on the bench can fail in the field. The fix is a pull-up or pull-down, or a driven level, set in the design and in the firmware.

RP2040 datasheet, Table 625 (Digital IO characteristics) and Table 622 (Absolute maximum ratings): read the VIH and VIL rows at 3.3 V, the pull resistance, and the pin voltage limit. Then compare them with the Pico SDK GPIO reference for gpio_pull_up and gpio_disable_pulls.
