Logic levels, pull-ups, and open-drain outputs

A button on a floating pin reads 0 or 1 at random. A pull-up resistor, or the chip's internal one, fixes that, and it also sets the logic level that the pin sees.

By the end of this lesson you can state the RP2040's input thresholds at 3.3 V, enable an internal pull-up with the Pico SDK, and explain why a floating input and a 5 V signal on a 3.3 V pin are both dangerous.

Where we are, and why this matters

Lesson 5 introduced voltage and current, and lesson 8 showed how two resistors set a voltage. Lesson 9 showed why a resistor in series with an LED limits the current, and lesson 11 showed a transistor used as a switch, fully on or fully off. This lesson joins those ideas at the pin of a microcontroller. A GPIO pin is a digital input or output: the chip reads the voltage on it as a 0 or a 1, and it drives the voltage on it as a 0 or a 1. Those two facts depend on the resistors and the supply around the pin, and lesson 15 showed where the 3.3 V supply comes from.

The problem appears with the first real switch. A pushbutton on a pin reads 1 one moment and 0 the next, even when nobody touches it, and that is the bug you will meet in a 3D printer's endstop, a door sensor, or a rotary encoder. The second problem is voltage: a sensor that outputs 5 V cannot be wired straight to a pin that is powered at 3.3 V. Both problems come down to the logic level, and both have a standard fix, which is the subject of this lesson.

Logic levels and thresholds

A logic level is the range of voltages that a device reads as 0 or as 1. A digital input has two thresholds. VIH is the minimum voltage that the input reads as high, and VIL is the maximum voltage that it reads as low. Between them is a band where the input is not guaranteed to read either value. The subscripts are standard datasheet notation: IH means "input, high" and IL means "input, low".

The RP2040 supply for its digital pins is called IOVDD, and it is 3.3 V on a Pico. Its datasheet gives the input thresholds for each IOVDD voltage. At IOVDD = 3.3 V, VIH is at least 2 V, and VIL is at most 0.8 V. Its hysteresis, the extra margin that a Schmitt-trigger input adds, is 0.2 V (RP2040 datasheet, Table 625, Digital IO characteristics). The thresholds are fractions of the IO supply only at some voltages. The datasheet lists a formula, 0.65 × IOVDD for VIH, for the 1.8 V row. At 3.3 V the table gives a fixed minimum of 2 V instead, so read the row for your supply.

A Schmitt trigger is an input circuit with two different thresholds, one for rising and one for falling. The hysteresis is the gap between them. A slow or noisy edge that crosses one threshold does not flip the input back and forth, because it has to cross the other threshold to change again. So a button read through a Schmitt input gives one clean change.

Outputs must clear the thresholds too

A driven output has its own limits. At IOVDD = 3.3 V, the RP2040 guarantees a high output of at least 2.62 V and a low output of at most 0.5 V (RP2040 datasheet, Table 625). Those are the figures that matter when one chip drives another. The receiving input needs at least 2.0 V to read a high, so a high output of 2.62 V leaves 0.62 V of margin. A low output of 0.5 V stays under the 0.8 V low threshold with 0.3 V to spare. A signal inside these margins works on the bench and in the field. A signal that sits between them does not.

Floating inputs and pull-ups

A floating input is connected to nothing that sets its level. An open pushbutton, or a wire that has come loose, leaves a pin floating. Its voltage is set by stray charge, leakage and nearby noise, so it wanders, and a floating pin can sit in the undefined band of Figure 1 and read 0 or 1 at random.

A pull-up resistor connects the line to the supply through a resistor. When nothing else drives the line, the pull-up holds it high, so the input reads 1. Pressing a button wired from the pin to ground overpowers the pull-up and pulls the line to 0. The pull-up has to be weak enough that a button can win, and strong enough that noise cannot pull the line low by accident. The RP2040 has an internal pull-up of 50 to 80 kΩ, and an internal pull-down of the same size (RP2040 datasheet, Table 625, Pull-Up and Pull-Down Resistance).

Here is the current arithmetic. The weakest pull-up is 50 kΩ. With the pin held at 0 V by a pressed button, the current through the pull-up is 3.3 V ÷ 50 kΩ = 66 µA at most. That small current is why an internal pull-up is fine for a button and is not fine for a long wire to a far-away sensor, which needs a stronger external resistor.

A pull-down resistor does the opposite job, holding a line low. The reset state of a GPIO matters here. The GPIOs in the pin list of Table 615, GPIO0 to GPIO27, all reset to a pull-down (RP2040 datasheet, Table 615, GPIO pins). So a pin you never configure reads low, not floating, but that is a fact about reset, not a plan. Set the pull you need in the firmware, and do not rely on the reset state.

Open-drain outputs

An open-drain output can only pull a line low, or let go and leave it high impedance, which means it neither drives nor sinks current. A pull-up resistor supplies the high level. Many shared buses use this, including I2C. Each device on the line only ever pulls low, so two devices cannot fight: if one pulls low, the line is low for both, and if both release, the pull-up brings the line high (NXP UM10204, the I2C-bus specification).

Take a line with a 4.7 kΩ pull-up to 3.3 V. When every device releases the line, the pull-up holds it at 3.3 V and no current flows in the pull-up beyond the small leakage of the inputs. When one device pulls the line low, the current through the pull-up is 3.3 V ÷ 4.7 kΩ = 0.70 mA, and the line sits near 0 V. No device has to supply a high level, so no device is damaged by the others. The price is that the line rises slowly through the pull-up, which limits the bus speed. That is a real design trade-off, and the bus specification gives the limits.

5 V tolerance and level shifting

Your PC's USB port gives 5 V, and many sensors output 5 V. The RP2040's absolute maximum rating for any digital pin voltage is IOVDD + 0.5 V, which is 3.8 V at IOVDD of 3.3 V (RP2040 datasheet, Table 622, absolute maximum ratings). An absolute maximum is a stress limit, not an operating value. A 5 V signal on a 3.3 V pin is over that limit, and it can damage the pin.

The datasheet also describes fault-tolerant pins, which the RP2040 calls FT. On those pins very little current flows in when the pin is below 3.63 V and IOVDD is 0 V (RP2040 datasheet, Table 614, pin types). That protects the pin when the chip is unpowered, which is a different case. It does not make a 5 V signal safe while the chip is powered, because Table 622 still limits every pin to IOVDD + 0.5 V. So treat every RP2040 pin as a 3.3 V pin.

To connect a 5 V signal, use a level shifter: a resistor divider, a bidirectional MOSFET shifter, or a translator IC. A simple divider works for a one-way signal from a source to the pin. Take 2.2 kΩ on top and 3.3 kΩ to ground. The pin sees 5 V × 3.3 kΩ ÷ (2.2 kΩ + 3.3 kΩ) = 3.0 V when the source is at 5 V. That is above the 2.0 V high threshold and below the 3.8 V absolute maximum, so the pin reads a clean high and is safe. The divider draws 5 V ÷ 5.5 kΩ = 0.9 mA while the source is high, and that current is part of the design. Lesson 8 covers the divider, and lesson 9 explains why a series resistor also limits current.

A divider is only for a one-way input. A bidirectional line, such as I2C, needs a shifter that can drive in both directions, and a single resistor pair cannot do that. The translator IC or MOSFET shifter is the right part there.

The code, line by line

The solution's pull setting is one small function. Read it as the translation of the hardware idea into a C call: 

static void configure_pulls(bool pull_up) {
    if (pull_up) {
        gpio_pull_up(BUTTON_PIN);
    } else {
        gpio_disable_pulls(BUTTON_PIN);
    }
}
  • static makes the function private to this file, which is how the course writes helpers. Nothing outside main.c can call it.
  • bool pull_up chooses the phase. The function takes the decision and does not keep state of its own, so main() owns the timing.
  • gpio_pull_up(BUTTON_PIN) is the Pico SDK call that enables the internal pull-up on that pin. In the SDK it is a small inline function that calls gpio_set_pulls(gpio, true, false), which sets the up enable and clears the down enable (Pico SDK 2.3.1, hardware/gpio.h).
  • gpio_disable_pulls(BUTTON_PIN) calls gpio_set_pulls(gpio, false, false), which clears both enables, so the pin has no internal pull at all. That is the state the phase is meant to show.

The rest of main is the usual GPIO sequence. gpio_init claims the pin for software control. gpio_set_dir(BUTTON_PIN, GPIO_IN) makes it an input, so the RP2040 does not drive it. gpio_get returns the logic level as a bool, and the code prints 1 for true. The sleep_ms(SAMPLE_MS) call slows the loop to one reading every 250 ms, so the serial monitor can be read by eye.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

The first mistake is wiring a button to 3.3 V instead of ground, and leaving the pull-up on. The pull-up holds the pin high, and a pressed button connects it to 3.3 V, which is the same level. The symptom is a serial log that reads 1 all the time, pressed or not. The fix is to wire the button to ground, as the project does, or to switch to a pull-down with the button on the supply side. Check the wiring first.

The second mistake is to leave a pin floating while the board is wired, because no pull was set. The symptom is a log that flips between 0 and 1 with no button press, and the flicker changes when you touch the wires. The fix is a pull, either the internal one set in code or an external resistor, chosen so the default state is the one you want.

The third mistake is to assume the reset state is the one you need. A pin that is never configured reads low after reset, because the reset state is a pull-down. A button that should read high when released reads low until the firmware sets a pull-up, so the first few milliseconds of the program look like a press. The symptom is a spurious event at start-up. The fix is to set the pull in main before the loop, as the solution does.

The fourth mistake is to connect a 5 V sensor straight to a 3.3 V pin. The code works on the bench for a while, which makes the mistake easy to miss. The symptom is a pin that reads wrong, or one that stops working, and the damage is permanent. The fix is the divider from this lesson, or a shifter, and a check of the sensor's output voltage on a meter before you connect it.

In real projects

Professionals set every pull explicitly, in the firmware or on the schematic, and do not rely on reset states or on a pin that happens to work. A code reviewer looks for an explicit pull on every input, a comment that says why it is needed, and a check that the external resistor value matches the bus. On a printer control board, an endstop switch is a good example: its pin needs a pull that is set in software, and its wiring is checked against the schematic when the board is built.

Interviewers ask about this because it shows whether a candidate has wired hardware as well as written code. A good answer names the threshold band, explains why a floating input is random, gives the pull's purpose and the current it draws, and describes the open-drain idea that makes a shared bus safe. The last step in a real project is a measurement: the logic analyzer from lesson 10 or a meter on the pin, with the button released and pressed.

Summary

  • At IOVDD = 3.3 V, the RP2040 reads a high at 2.0 V or more and a low at 0.8 V or less, with a 0.8 V to 2.0 V band that is not guaranteed.
  • A floating input has no defined level, so it reads 0 or 1 at random. A pull-up or pull-down fixes its default.
  • The RP2040's internal pulls are 50 to 80 kΩ, and a GPIO resets to a pull-down.
  • An open-drain output only pulls low, so a shared line with a pull-up is safe when several devices drive it.
  • Absolute maximum pin voltage is IOVDD + 0.5 V, which is 3.8 V at 3.3 V, so a 5 V signal is over the limit.
  • A resistor divider level-shifts a one-way signal, for example 5 V to 3.0 V with 2.2 kΩ over 3.3 kΩ.
  • The SDK's gpio_pull_up and gpio_disable_pulls set the internal pulls from C.

Model answer

A floating input has no defined voltage, so it can sit in the undefined band between VIL and VIH, where the input may read either value and may draw extra current. It also picks up noise, so the reading changes with the environment. A test that passes on the bench can fail in the field. The fix is a pull-up or pull-down, or a driven level, set in the design and in the firmware.
