Storing data in flash

A setpoint, a calibration value or a counter has to survive the power going off, and on most microcontrollers the only place that keeps data without power is the flash that holds the program. Flash has its own rules: you erase it in big blocks, you can only clear bits, and each block wears out. You will build a small settings store that follows those rules and keeps a good copy even when the power fails in the middle of a save.

By the end of this lesson you can save a setting in flash as a log of records with a sequence number and a CRC, so that it survives resets, spreads the wear over many saves, and keeps the last good copy through a power cut at any moment.

Where we are and why it matters

Lesson 104 split the flash into slots and kept the old image until the new one had proven itself. Lesson 103 used a CRC to catch a frame that was damaged on the way. This lesson uses both ideas again, at a much smaller scale: a 12-byte record instead of a firmware image.

Almost every product keeps a few values across power cycles. The capstone's filament dryer has a setpoint that the user picks with two buttons. Other devices keep a sensor's calibration offset, a count of operating hours, or a network address. The user expects the value to be there after the plug is pulled, and nobody pulls the plug at a convenient moment. The code that saves the value must assume the power can go between any two instructions.

The rules of flash

The Pico's program lives in a 2 MB Winbond W25Q16JV chip next to the RP2040 (Pico datasheet, chapter 1). It is NOR flash, and it does not behave like RAM. Three rules decide everything in this lesson (W25Q16JV datasheet Rev I, section 1, and the SDK's hardware/flash.h):

  1. Erase sets bits to 1, a sector at a time. The smallest erase is a 4 KB sector (4096 bytes, 16 pages). After it, every byte reads 0xFF. The chip has 512 such sectors.
  2. Program can only clear bits, a page at a time. A program operation writes up to one 256-byte page, and it can only change bits from 1 to 0. Writing a byte that is not 0xFF gives the AND of the old and the new value. The only way back to 1 is to erase the whole sector.
  3. Each sector wears out. The datasheet promises at least 100,000 program-erase cycles and more than 20 years of data retention (section 2). After that, bits may stop erasing or may lose their value.

Rule 2 is the one that surprises programmers. Here is one byte, programmed twice without an erase between: 

after erase              1111 1111   0xFF
program 0x5A             0101 1010   0xFF AND 0x5A = 0x5A   (correct)
program 0xA5, no erase   0000 0000   0x5A AND 0xA5 = 0x00   (neither value)

So changing a single saved byte really means: copy the sector somewhere, erase it, and program it again. That is slow, it wears the sector, and between the erase and the program the old value no longer exists anywhere.

Flash on the Pico: offsets, XIP and interrupts

The RP2040 runs its program straight from this chip, which is called execute in place (XIP). The chip is mapped at XIP_BASE, 0x10000000, so reading saved data is a plain memory read: the byte at flash offset off is at address XIP_BASE + off. The SDK's write functions take the offset, not the address: flash_range_erase(off, count) needs a sector-aligned offset and a whole number of sectors, and flash_range_program(off, data, count) needs a page-aligned offset and a whole number of pages.

While the chip erases or programs, it cannot be read, so for that time nothing may run from flash. The SDK's functions run from RAM, but an interrupt handler or the vector table in flash would be fetched in the middle of the operation. The SDK header says to disable interrupts around the calls, and to stop the other core if both are running (multicore_lockout, or flash_safe_execute() from pico/flash.h, which does both). The data you program must also be in RAM, not in flash.

The program starts at offset 0 and grows upwards, so settings go at the other end. The last two sectors of the 2 MB chip start at 0x200000 − 2 × 0x1000 = 0x1FE000. The linker symbol __flash_binary_end marks where the program ends; the demo below prints both, so you can see the gap.

Two problems with the obvious save

The obvious way to save a setting is to keep it in one place: erase its sector, then program the new value. The starter project does exactly this. It works on the bench, and it has two problems that show only in the field.

Wear. Every save erases the same sector. If the firmware saves once a minute, 100,000 cycles last 100,000 / 1440 saves a day = 69 days. A product that saves only when the user changes something lasts much longer, but a counter of operating hours or a "last state" that is saved on every change will wear out the sector within a year.

Power loss. A 4 KB sector erase takes 45 ms typically and up to 400 ms (datasheet section 9.6). For that whole time, and until the program finishes, the old value is gone and the new one is not written yet. A power cut in that window leaves an erased sector, and the next boot has no setting at all. The same happens if the cut comes halfway through the program: the record is only partly written, and nothing tells the next boot that it is incomplete.

The fix: a log of records

Never overwrite the only copy. Write each new value into a fresh, already erased place, and leave the old one alone until the new one is complete. Three small pieces make that work:

  1. Slots. The two sectors are split into 32 slots of one page each. Each save writes one record into the next free slot, so one erase serves 16 saves.
  2. A sequence number. Each record carries a number one higher than the record before it. At boot, the store reads all 32 slots and takes the valid record with the highest number. No pointer to "the current slot" is saved anywhere, so there is nothing else to keep up to date.
  3. A CRC. A record torn by a power cut, or a bit that went bad, fails its CRC and is ignored, so the next older record wins. This is the CRC-16/CCITT-FALSE from lesson 103.

The log erases a sector only when it moves into that sector's first slot. At that moment the newest record is in the other sector, so the erase destroys only older copies. A power cut during the erase leaves the newest record untouched. This is the same rule as lesson 104's update: the old copy stays until the new one exists.

The record

A record is 12 bytes at the start of its slot, and the rest of the page stays 0xFF. The fields are ordered so that the struct has no padding (lesson 48), and a _Static_assert checks that it is 12 bytes.

BytesFieldWhy it is there
0 to 3magic, 0x53455454 ("SETT")Erased flash reads 0xFFFFFFFF, so a blank slot is rejected at once
4 to 7seqWhich record is newest. 32 bits never wrap in practice: at one save a minute, that takes over 8,000 years
8 to 9setpoint_cThe value itself
10 to 11crcCRC-16/CCITT-FALSE of bytes 0 to 9: a torn or damaged record fails it

Worked example: a power cut, byte by byte

Start where Figure 1 starts: 21 saves are done, and slot 20 holds sequence 21 with a setpoint of 80 °C. The RP2040 is little-endian, so each field is stored low byte first: 

slot 20   54 54 45 53 | 15 00 00 00 | 50 00 | 09 E1
          magic SETT    seq 21        80      CRC 0xE109

The user sets 81 °C. The store finds slot 20 as the newest, so the new record gets sequence 22 and goes into slot 21. Its CRC is 0x1CD8. Slot 21 is not the first slot of a sector, so no erase is needed. The program starts, and the power fails after the first 10 bytes: 

slot 21   54 54 45 53 | 16 00 00 00 | 51 00 | FF FF
          magic OK      seq 22        81      CRC not written

At the next boot, the scan reads all 32 slots. Slot 21 has the right magic and the highest sequence number, but the CRC of its first 10 bytes is 0x1CD8, not 0xFFFF, so it is rejected. The newest valid record is slot 20, and the device starts with 80 °C, the last value that was saved completely. The user's change to 81 °C is lost, which is the best any design can do when the power goes before the save finishes.

Now the user sets 82 °C. The newest valid record is still slot 20, so the obvious next slot is slot 21 again. But slot 21 is not blank, and programming over it would AND the new bytes into the torn ones. The setpoint byte would become 0x51 AND 0x52 = 0x50, which is 80 again. The CRC bytes were still 0xFF, so they would take the new record's CRC exactly. The slot would say 80 °C under the CRC for 82 °C, the check would reject it, and the new save would be lost too. The store therefore checks that a slot is blank before it uses it, skips slot 21, and writes sequence 22 with 82 °C into slot 22.

The wear has changed too. The two sectors are erased in turn, each once per 32 saves, so 100,000 cycles allow 3.2 million saves. At one save a minute, that is 3,200,000 / 1440 = 2,222 days, about six years instead of 69 days. Saving only when the value changes stretches it much further.

The code, line by line

The store is src/settings.c. It keeps nothing in RAM: every call reads the flash, so a reset changes nothing. It reaches the flash only through three functions in flash_port.h, so the same file runs on the Pico and in the host tests, which is the hardware abstraction from lesson 76. Loading is a scan: 

static bool record_valid(const settings_record_t *r) {
    return r->magic == RECORD_MAGIC && record_crc(r) == r->crc;
}

static int newest_slot(settings_record_t *newest) {
    int best = NO_SLOT;
    for (unsigned i = 0; i < SLOT_COUNT; i++) {
        settings_record_t r;
        memcpy(&r, flash_port_read(slot_offset(i)), sizeof r);
        if (record_valid(&r) && (best == NO_SLOT || r.seq > newest->seq)) {
            *newest = r;
            best = (int)i;
        }
    }
    return best;
}

The magic check comes first, because it rejects the blank slots cheaply. The comparison r.seq > newest->seq keeps the highest sequence number wherever it is, so the scan works after the log has wrapped from slot 31 back to slot 0. The memcpy copies the bytes into a local struct, which avoids reading a struct through a pointer into flash.

Saving picks the slot after the newest record: 

unsigned next = (slot == NO_SLOT) ? 0u : ((unsigned)slot + 1u) % SLOT_COUNT;
for (;;) {
    if (next % SLOTS_PER_SECTOR == 0u) {
        flash_port_erase_sector(slot_offset(next));   /* the log moves into this sector */
        break;
    }
    if (slot_blank(next)) {
        break;
    }
    next = (next + 1u) % SLOT_COUNT;   /* a record torn by a power cut: skip its slot */
}
/* then: build a 256-byte page of 0xFF, copy the record in, program it */

The % SLOT_COUNT makes the log a ring, like the ring buffer of lesson 70, but in flash. Reaching slot 0 or slot 16 means the log moves into a sector, so that sector is erased first. The skip loop always stops, because at most 15 slots lie between the newest record and the next sector's first slot. The page buffer is static: it must be in RAM for the Pico's program call, and 256 bytes is a lot of stack.

On the Pico, flash_port_pico.c is all the hardware-specific code there is: 

void flash_port_erase_sector(uint32_t offset) {
    uint32_t irq = save_and_disable_interrupts();
    flash_range_erase(offset, FLASH_SECTOR_SIZE);
    restore_interrupts(irq);
}

const uint8_t *flash_port_read(uint32_t offset) {
    return (const uint8_t *)(XIP_BASE + offset);
}

Common mistakes and how to spot them

Mistake 1: programming without an erase. The code writes a new value over an old one and reads back something that is neither. Symptom: a saved value comes back with bits missing: the AND of the old and the new value, never larger than either. Fix: program only slots you know are blank, and check with a read-back in a test.

Mistake 2: interrupts left on during an erase. An interrupt fires while the chip is busy, and its handler in flash cannot be fetched. Symptom: the device hangs or faults now and then during a save, and never on the bench where no interrupts are running. Fix: wrap every erase and program in save_and_disable_interrupts() and restore_interrupts(), and use flash_safe_execute() when the second core is running.

Mistake 3: an address where an offset belongs. flash_range_erase(XIP_BASE + off, ...) passes 0x101FE000 instead of 0x1FE000, far past the end of a 2 MB chip. Symptom: the save works or fails depending on how the chip treats an address it doesn't have, which is not something to rely on. Fix: keep offsets and addresses apart in the names, as flash_port_read(offset) does, and add XIP_BASE only when reading.

Mistake 4: trusting a record without a CRC. The magic matches, so the code believes the rest. Symptom: after a power cut the device starts with a value that was never completely saved, such as the 81 °C in the starter's failing test, or with a mix of old and new bits. Fix: check the CRC on every load, and fall back to the next older record.

In real projects

Products store more than one value, so the record usually holds a version number and a whole settings struct, and the firmware knows how to read the older versions after an update. Teams often use a library instead of their own log: littlefs, a small file system, says it "is designed to handle random power failures" and provides wear leveling. Whatever the library, the review questions are the ones in this lesson: what happens if the power fails here, and how many erases does each save cost? A test with a fake flash that can cut the power answers both on the PC.

Summary

  • NOR flash erases a 4 KB sector to 0xFF and programs a 256-byte page by clearing bits. Writing over data without an erase gives the AND of the two values.
  • The W25Q16JV on the Pico promises at least 100,000 program-erase cycles. Saving in one place once a minute wears it out in 69 days.
  • On the Pico, flash is read at XIP_BASE + offset and written by offset. Interrupts must be off during an erase or program, and the other core stopped.
  • A log of records, each with a sequence number and a CRC, writes new values into fresh slots and never overwrites the only copy.
  • Erasing a sector only when the log moves into it keeps the newest record safe in the other sector, and divides the wear by the number of slots.
  • A record torn by a power cut fails its CRC, so the store loads the previous one, and the next save skips the torn slot.
You built a settings store that survives resets and a power cut at any moment, spreads the wear over 32 slots, and proved both with tests that cut the power on purpose.
