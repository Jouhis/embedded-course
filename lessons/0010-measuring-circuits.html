Measuring circuits

A meter only tells you the truth if you connect it correctly. The wrong connection can change the circuit, or break the meter, or both.

By the end of this lesson you can connect a voltmeter in parallel and an ammeter in series, read a value from each in CircuitJS, explain why a meter can change the thing it measures, and say which instrument records digital signals over time.

Where we are and why it matters

Over the last nine lessons you learned the quantities that every circuit is made of. Lesson 5 defined voltage, current and resistance and tied them together with Ohm's law. Lesson 6 added power and heat. Lesson 7 gave you Kirchhoff's laws for nodes and loops, and lesson 8 showed how two resistors split a supply into a fraction. Lesson 9 used all of that to choose a series resistor for an LED. Every one of those calculations assumed you knew the numbers in the circuit. This lesson is about how you find out.

The problem comes up on the first day of any hardware job. A board arrives from the assembler, and the supply rail reads 3.2 V instead of 3.3 V. A heater draws more current than the datasheet says it should. A motor driver works on the bench and fails in the enclosure. In each case you have to put an instrument into a live circuit, read a number, and trust it. A wrong connection can give a wrong number, and it can also blow a fuse, damage a supply, or change the behaviour you were trying to observe.

On a working bench you use three kinds of instrument. A multimeter gives one value at a time, for voltage, current or resistance. An oscilloscope draws voltage against time, so you can see a signal change. A logic analyzer, which the course uses in lesson 60, records which digital pins are high or low over time. Each one answers a different question, and each one has to be connected in a particular way.

The idea: a meter is part of the circuit

The first idea to hold onto is that every meter is a component. It has a resistance of its own, and when you connect it, that resistance joins the circuit. A good meter is designed so that its effect is negligible, and you can predict when it is not. The rest of the lesson follows from that one fact.

Start with a voltage. Voltage is always a difference between two points, so a voltage measurement compares two nodes. A voltmeter is therefore connected in parallel with the part you care about, across the two points whose difference you want. Recall from lesson 7 that components in parallel share the same two nodes and therefore the same voltage. The voltmeter sits across those nodes and sees the same voltage as the part. Its input resistance is very high, so it takes almost no current from the circuit, and the circuit barely notices it is there.

Now take a current. Current is the amount of charge that passes a point each second, and the meter has to make the current flow through it. An ammeter is therefore connected in series, in the path the current already takes. Lesson 7 showed that components in series carry the same current, so an ammeter placed anywhere in the path reads the same current as every other point in that path. The trade-off is that the ammeter must have a very low resistance. If it did not, it would add a large resistance to the path and change the current it is trying to measure.

That is why the two connections are opposite. A voltmeter wants to be a near-open circuit, so it can sit across a part without drawing current. An ammeter wants to be a near-short circuit, so it can sit in a path without adding resistance. Put the voltmeter in series and it breaks the circuit, so the reading is near zero and the circuit stops. Put the ammeter across a part and it becomes a short across that part, so the current runs far higher than it should. Many multimeters protect their current inputs with a fuse. That is a sign of how easily the wrong connection ends the meter's life. Set the function and the range before you connect the probes.

Checking a bicycle tyre with a pressure gauge lets out a puff of air, so the reading is a little lower than the pressure was before you measured. A good gauge lets out so little that nobody cares. A meter does the same to a circuit: it takes a little current, or adds a little resistance, and a good meter keeps that small next to what it measures. The next section puts numbers on "small".

Voltage, current and what a meter does to them

Before doing any arithmetic, it helps to name the two kinds of error a meter can cause. The first is loading: the meter's own resistance draws current that the circuit would not otherwise draw, and the voltage you read is lower or higher than the true value. The second is burden: the ammeter's small internal resistance drops a small voltage across itself, which slightly lowers the current in the path. Both errors are small when the meter is well designed and correctly connected. Both become large when the connection is wrong.

Loading is easiest to see on a voltage divider, which you met in lesson 8. Two equal 10 kΩ resistors across a 10 V supply give a midpoint of 5 V. Now put a voltmeter across the lower resistor. Suppose that meter has an input resistance of 10 kΩ, which is a plausible value for a cheap meter on a low range. The lower resistor is now in parallel with the meter, and the parallel combination of two 10 kΩ resistors is 5 kΩ. The divider now has 10 kΩ on top and 5 kΩ on the bottom, so the voltage across the 5 kΩ part is 10 V × 5 kΩ ÷ (10 kΩ + 5 kΩ) = 3.33 V. The meter reads 3.33 V, not 5 V. The circuit has not changed, only the meter has, and the reading is wrong by a third.

The same arithmetic with a meter whose input resistance is 10 MΩ gives almost no error. The parallel combination is 9.99 kΩ, and the reading is 4.998 V. This is why the high input resistance of a good voltmeter matters. A rule of thumb is that the meter's resistance should be at least a hundred times the resistance you are measuring across. A meter that is only ten times larger will pull the reading down by about a tenth.

Loading also explains a common surprise on a board. You measure a divider output with a meter, you get the expected value, then you connect the next stage and the voltage drops. The next stage is a load on the divider, and its input resistance acts like a meter in parallel with the bottom resistor. Lesson 8 called this the source resistance of a divider. The meter and the load behave the same way, and the cure is the same: keep the parallel resistance much larger than the divider's own resistance.

Current: the ammeter in series

An ammeter reads current, and it must be in the path. Suppose a 5 V USB supply feeds a 100 Ω resistor. Ohm's law gives I = 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 0.05 A, which is 50 mA. Put the ammeter in series with the resistor, and it reads 50 mA. Its resistance is small, so the total resistance is still close to 100 Ω, and the reading is close to the true value.

Now make the mistake. Put the ammeter across the supply instead. The supply is now connected to the ammeter with nothing in between, so the only resistance in the path is the ammeter's own. Suppose that is 0.1 Ω, which is a plausible value for a meter's current shunt on a low range. The current is 5 V ÷ 0.1 Ω = 50 A. A meter rated for milliamps cannot survive that. Its fuse blows if it has one, its internal shunt may burn, and the USB supply may shut down or be damaged. Nothing about the circuit has been measured.

A voltmeter goes across a part and must not be in series. An ammeter goes in the path and must never be across a supply. Check the connection before you turn anything on.

  1. A voltmeter measures a difference, so its two probes go across the part. Its input resistance is very high, so it draws almost no current and reads the full 5 V.
  2. An ammeter measures the current that passes through it, so you break the path and put it in series. Its resistance is tiny, so it hardly changes the 50 mA.
  3. The classic mistake: the ammeter across the supply. Its 0.1 Ω shunt is the only thing in the path, so 5 V ÷ 0.1 Ω = 50 A tries to flow. The fuse blows and nothing has been measured.
  4. Now a divider: 10 kΩ on 10 kΩ across 10 V. Its midpoint is at 5.00 V.
  5. Measure it with a meter whose input resistance is only 10 kΩ. The meter is in parallel with the bottom resistor, the bottom becomes 5 kΩ, and the meter reads 3.33 V. The meter changed the circuit; a 10 MΩ meter would read 5.00 V.

The oscilloscope: voltage against time

A multimeter gives one number, but many signals do not sit still. A PWM output switches between 0 V and 3.3 V many thousands of times a second, and a multimeter reading of "1.6 V" tells you almost nothing about it. An oscilloscope, usually called a scope, draws the voltage on the vertical axis against time on the horizontal axis. Its trace shows the shape of the signal: how fast it rises, whether it overshoots, how much it ripples.

A scope has a few settings you must understand before you read it. The volts per division sets the vertical scale, and the time per division sets the horizontal scale. Both are set on the instrument, and the trace is only meaningful when you know them. The scope also has a ground clip. Its voltage readings are measured relative to that clip, so the clip must be on a node that is truly the circuit's reference. If you clip it to a point that sits 2 V above the real ground, every trace moves up by 2 V. The scope is correct for the reference it was given.

The scope's probe is a connection in parallel, like a voltmeter, and it loads the circuit in the same way. Many probes have a 10× setting. In that setting the probe divides the signal by ten before it reaches the scope, and its input resistance is much higher, so it loads the circuit less. The cost is that you must tell the scope that the probe is in 10× mode, or every reading is out by a factor of ten. For probe guidance, see Tektronix on probes.

The logic analyzer: which pins were high, in what order

A scope answers "how does this voltage change?" A logic analyzer answers a different question: "which pins were high or low, and in what order?" It does not draw the analogue shape. It samples a set of digital inputs at a fixed rate, records a 1 or a 0 on each, and stores the record. A decoder then reads a protocol from the record. In lesson 60 you will decode I²C from a logic analyzer capture, and the decoder finds the start condition, the address and the acknowledge bits.

The Wokwi part is wokwi-logic-analyzer. It has eight inputs, D0 to D7, and it saves the recording as a VCD file. You cannot read a logic analyzer on a bench display the way you read a multimeter, because the information is in the sequence, not in one value. That is why the lesson introduces it here: it is the right instrument for a digital bus and the wrong one for a supply rail. Wokwi: Logic Analyzer part

Lesson 16 will use the same digital view for logic levels and pull-up resistors. A logic analyzer or a scope on a pin tells you whether the pin reads high or low, but the threshold between them comes from the logic family, not from the instrument.

Worked example with real numbers

Take the 5 V supply and 100 Ω resistor from above, and walk through one complete measurement. You want to know the voltage across the resistor and the current through it, and you want to know whether the meters have changed the circuit.

  1. Predict first. The current is I = V ÷ R = 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 0.05 A. The voltage across the resistor is the full 5 V, because it is the only load on the supply.
  2. Measure the voltage. Set the multimeter to DC volts with a range above 5 V. Put the red probe on the supply side and the black probe on the ground side of the resistor. The reading should be 5.0 V, or very close to it.
  3. Measure the current. Move the red probe to the current input and set the function to DC current with a range above 50 mA. Break the wire at one end of the resistor, and connect the meter in series so that the current flows through it. The reading should be 50 mA.
  4. Check the loading. A 10 MΩ voltmeter in parallel with a 100 Ω resistor adds almost nothing, because the parallel value is still very close to 100 Ω. A meter with a 0.1 Ω shunt in series changes the total from 100 Ω to 100.1 Ω, which changes the current by about 0.1 %. Both errors are far below the precision of a bench meter, so the measurement is trustworthy.

The point of the example is the order of the steps. You predicted the answer before you measured it, so a wrong reading is easy to notice. You also checked, in principle, that the meter did not disturb the circuit. Those two habits apply to any measurement.

Before you measure current, check the meter's current terminals, its function dial, and its range. Start with the highest range if you do not know the value, and never move the probes while the circuit is powered.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

Each of these mistakes is common, and each has a recognisable symptom. Learn the symptoms, because on a real board you will see them before you think to check the connection.

Mistake 1: the ammeter across the supply

The symptom is a fuse that blows the moment you connect the meter, or a meter that reads "OL" or flashes a warning, or a supply that shuts down. The fix is to remove the meter from the supply, check the current terminals and the fuse rating, and put the meter in series with the load. Before you power a circuit with a current meter in it, trace the path the current will take and confirm that the meter is in that path and nowhere else.

Mistake 2: the voltmeter in series

The symptom is a circuit that stops working when you add the meter. An LED goes out, a motor stops, or a reading is close to zero when you expect the full supply. The voltmeter has a very high resistance, so in series it breaks the path. The fix is to move the probes across the part, not into its path. If the reading is zero and the circuit is dead, look at the connection before you look at the part.

Mistake 3: the wrong range or function

The symptom is a reading that is exactly 1,000 times too large or too small. A current of 5 mA shows as 5 A, or a voltage of 3.3 V shows as 0.0033 V. The cause is a function or range setting that does not match the quantity or the size of the signal. Start on the highest range when you do not know the size, and read the unit on the display before you read the number.

Mistake 4: a scope with the wrong probe setting

The symptom is a waveform that looks ten times too large or too small, with no obvious cause. The probe is in 10× mode, but the scope is set to 1×, or the other way round. The fix is to match the probe attenuation setting on the scope to the switch on the probe. Check the setting every time you change probes.

Mistake 5: the ground clip on the wrong node

The symptom is a trace that sits at a voltage other than zero when the signal should be at zero, or a shorted circuit when the ground clip touches a live rail. Clip the scope's ground to the circuit's ground node, the same point the supply returns to. If you need to measure across a part, use a differential probe or move the clip, not force the reading.

In real projects

On a professional bench, measuring a board's supply current is routine. The usual method is to break the supply line at a jumper or a test point, insert a current meter in series, and set the range high enough for the largest current you expect. Many engineers add a small series resistor, often a fraction of an ohm, and measure the voltage across it instead, because a voltage is easier to log and to show on a scope than a current. That is the same idea as Ohm's law in lesson 5. Measure the drop across a known resistance, then divide.

A clamp meter measures current without breaking the circuit. It senses the magnetic field around a wire, so it does not load the circuit in series. It is the right choice for a live board or a motor cable, but it is a poor choice for a small current, because the field is weak and the reading has more noise. Know which tool you are using, and what its limit is.

Experienced engineers also keep a habit of checking the instrument before the circuit. They confirm that the probe is in the right mode, the meter is on the right function, and the scope's ground is on the right node. Code review works the same way. A reviewer does not trust the output until the setup is checked, and a careful engineer does not trust a reading until the setup is known.

Summary

  • Every meter is a component. Its resistance joins the circuit, so a good meter is designed to make that effect negligible.
  • A voltmeter is connected in parallel across the part, and its input resistance is very high. An ammeter is connected in series, and it must have a very low resistance.
  • A voltmeter in series breaks the circuit, and an ammeter across a supply draws a huge current. Check the connection before you turn anything on.
  • Loading is the error caused by a meter's own resistance. With a 10 kΩ meter across the lower 10 kΩ of a divider, the reading is 3.33 V instead of 5 V, and the meter's resistance should be at least a hundred times the resistance you measure across.
  • An oscilloscope draws voltage against time, and its ground clip sets the zero reference. A probe in 10× mode must be matched by the setting on the scope, or every reading is out by a factor of ten.
  • A logic analyzer records which digital pins were high or low, and in what order. The Wokwi part wokwi-logic-analyzer has eight inputs, D0 to D7.
  • Predict the reading before you measure. A 5 V supply across a 100 Ω resistor gives 50 mA, so a reading far from that points to a fault in the setup or the circuit.
