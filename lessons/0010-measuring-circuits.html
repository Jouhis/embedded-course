Measuring circuits

Meters, oscilloscopes and logic analyzers on a live circuit

By the end of this lesson you can connect a voltmeter in parallel and an ammeter in series, read a value from each in CircuitJS, explain why a meter can change the thing it measures, and say which instrument records digital signals over time.

Finding the numbers in a real circuit

The last nine lessons built up the quantities that every circuit is made of. Lesson 5 defined voltage, current and resistance and linked them with Ohm's law, lesson 6 added power and heat, lesson 7 gave Kirchhoff's laws for nodes and loops, and lesson 8 showed how two resistors divide a supply. Lesson 9 then used all of it to choose a series resistor for an LED. Every one of those calculations assumed the numbers in the circuit were known, and this lesson is about how you actually find them.

The need arises on the first day with any new board. The supply rail reads 3.2 V instead of 3.3 V, a heater draws more current than its datasheet suggests, or a motor driver works on the bench and fails inside the enclosure. In each case you have to put an instrument into a live circuit, read a number and decide whether to trust it. A wrong connection can produce a wrong number, and it can also blow a fuse, damage a supply or change the very behaviour you were trying to observe.

A working bench has three kinds of instrument. A multimeter gives one value at a time, for voltage, current or resistance. An oscilloscope plots voltage against time, so you can watch a signal change. A logic analyzer, which the course uses in lesson 60, records which digital pins were high or low over time. Each answers a different question and each must be connected in its own way.

The meter as part of the circuit

The central idea of this lesson is that every meter is itself a component. It has a resistance of its own, and connecting it adds that resistance to the circuit. A good meter is designed so that its effect is negligible, and with a little arithmetic you can predict when it is not. Everything else in the lesson follows from this.

Checking a bicycle tyre with a pressure gauge lets out a puff of air, so the reading is slightly lower than the pressure before you measured. A good gauge releases so little that nobody cares. A meter does the same to a circuit, drawing a little current or adding a little resistance, and a good one keeps that small compared with what it measures. The comparison stops at the numbers, which the next sections supply, since a gauge's error cannot be calculated from its design the way a meter's can.

Consider voltage first. A voltage is always a difference between two points, so measuring one means comparing two nodes, and a voltmeter is therefore connected in parallel with the part of interest. Lesson 7 showed that parallel components share the same two nodes and hence the same voltage, so the voltmeter sees exactly what the part sees. Its input resistance is very high, which means it draws almost no current and the circuit barely notices it.

Current works the other way round. Current is the charge passing a point each second, so the meter has to make that current flow through itself, and an ammeter is therefore connected in series, in the path the current already takes. Since series components carry the same current, an ammeter anywhere in the path reads the current of the whole path. The ammeter must, however, have a very low resistance, because otherwise it would add a large resistance to the path and change the current it is trying to measure.

The two connections are therefore opposites. A voltmeter behaves almost like an open circuit, so it can sit across a part without drawing current, and an ammeter behaves almost like a short circuit, so it can sit in a path without adding resistance. Placed in series, a voltmeter breaks the circuit, so the reading is near zero and the circuit stops. Placed across a part, an ammeter shorts it, and the current rises far above its normal value. Many multimeters protect their current inputs with a fuse, which shows how easily a wrong connection ends a meter's life. Set the function and the range before connecting the probes.

Loading and burden errors

Before doing any arithmetic it helps to name the two errors a meter can introduce. Loading occurs when the meter's own resistance draws current the circuit would not otherwise supply, so the voltage you read differs from the true value. Burden occurs when an ammeter's small internal resistance drops a small voltage across itself and slightly lowers the current in the path. Both are small with a well-designed, correctly connected meter, and both become large when the connection is wrong.

Loading is easiest to see on the voltage divider from lesson 8. Two equal 10 kΩ resistors across a 10 V supply give a midpoint of 5 V. Now place a voltmeter across the lower resistor, and suppose it has an input resistance of 10 kΩ, a plausible figure for a cheap meter on a low range. The lower resistor and the meter are now in parallel, so the bottom of the divider becomes

Rbottom = (R2 × Rm) ÷ (R2 + Rm)(1)

where R2 is the lower resistor and Rm the meter's input resistance. With both at 10 kΩ, the bottom becomes 5 kΩ, and the divider formula gives 10 V × 5 kΩ ÷ (10 kΩ + 5 kΩ) = 3.33 V. The meter reads 3.33 V instead of 5 V, an error of a third, even though nothing in the circuit itself has changed.

Repeating the calculation with a 10 MΩ meter gives almost no error, since Equation 1 then yields 9.99 kΩ and the reading is 4.998 V. This is why a good voltmeter's high input resistance matters. As a rule of thumb, the meter's resistance should be at least a hundred times the resistance it is measuring across. A meter only ten times larger lowers the bottom resistance by about 9 %, which on an equal-resistor divider reads about 5 % low, as lesson 8 calculated.

Loading also explains a common surprise on a board. A divider output measures correctly with a meter, but the voltage drops as soon as the next stage is connected. That stage is a load whose input resistance sits in parallel with the bottom resistor exactly as the meter did, which lesson 8 described in terms of the divider's source resistance. The cure is the same for both: keep the parallel resistance much larger than the divider's own.

Current measurement with an ammeter

An ammeter must sit in the current path. Suppose a 5 V USB supply feeds a 100 Ω resistor, so that Ohm's law gives

I = V ÷ R = 5 V ÷ 100 Ω = 0.05 A(2)

where V is the supply voltage and R the resistance in the path, giving 50 mA. With the ammeter in series with the resistor it reads 50 mA, because its small resistance leaves the total close to 100 Ω.

Now suppose the ammeter is placed across the supply instead. Nothing then lies between the supply terminals except the ammeter, so the only resistance in the path is its own, perhaps 0.1 Ω for a meter's current shunt on a low range. Equation 2 now gives 5 V ÷ 0.1 Ω = 50 A, which no milliamp-rated meter survives. Its fuse blows if it has one, its internal shunt may burn and the USB supply may shut down or be damaged, and nothing about the circuit has been measured.

The rule is therefore short: a voltmeter goes across a part and never in series, and an ammeter goes in the path and never across a supply. Check the connection before switching anything on.

  1. A voltmeter measures a difference, so its two probes go across the part. Its input resistance is very high, so it draws almost no current and reads the full 5 V.
  2. An ammeter measures the current that passes through it, so you break the path and put it in series. Its resistance is tiny, so it hardly changes the 50 mA.
  3. The classic mistake: the ammeter across the supply. Its 0.1 Ω shunt is the only thing in the path, so 5 V ÷ 0.1 Ω = 50 A tries to flow. The fuse blows and nothing has been measured.
  4. Now a divider: 10 kΩ on 10 kΩ across 10 V. Its midpoint is at 5.00 V.
  5. Measure it with a meter whose input resistance is only 10 kΩ. The meter is in parallel with the bottom resistor, the bottom becomes 5 kΩ, and the meter reads 3.33 V. The meter changed the circuit; a 10 MΩ meter would read 5.00 V.

The oscilloscope and voltage over time

A multimeter gives one number, but many signals do not stay still. A PWM output switches between 0 V and 3.3 V thousands of times a second, and a multimeter reading of 1.6 V says almost nothing about it. An oscilloscope, usually called a scope, plots voltage on the vertical axis against time on the horizontal axis, so its trace shows the shape of the signal: how fast it rises, whether it overshoots and how much it ripples.

A few settings must be understood before a scope can be read. The volts per division sets the vertical scale and the time per division the horizontal one, and the trace means nothing until you know both. The scope also has a ground clip, and every reading is measured relative to it, so the clip must sit on the circuit's true reference node. Clipped to a point 2 V above the real ground, every trace shifts up by 2 V, even though the scope is correct for the reference it was given.

The scope's probe connects in parallel, like a voltmeter, and loads the circuit in the same way. Many probes have a 10× setting, in which the probe divides the signal by ten before it reaches the scope and presents a much higher input resistance, so it loads the circuit less. The scope must then be told that the probe is in 10× mode, or every reading is out by a factor of ten. For probe guidance, see Tektronix on probes.

The logic analyzer and digital timing

Where a scope shows how a voltage changes, a logic analyzer shows which pins were high or low and in what order. It ignores the analogue shape and samples a set of digital inputs at a fixed rate, recording a 1 or a 0 on each and storing the record. A decoder can then reconstruct a protocol from it. In lesson 60 you decode I²C from a logic analyzer capture, and the decoder finds the start condition, the address and the acknowledge bits.

The Wokwi part is wokwi-logic-analyzer, which has eight inputs, D0 to D7, and saves its recording as a VCD file. A logic analyzer cannot be read at a glance like a multimeter, because the information lies in the sequence rather than in any single value. That makes it the right instrument for a digital bus and the wrong one for a supply rail, which is why it is introduced here alongside the others. Wokwi: Logic Analyzer part

Lesson 16 uses the same digital view for logic levels and pull-up resistors. A logic analyzer or a scope on a pin shows whether the pin reads high or low, but the threshold between the two comes from the logic family rather than from the instrument.

Worked example: voltage and current in one resistor

Take the 5 V supply and 100 Ω resistor from above and walk through one complete measurement. We want the voltage across the resistor, the current through it, and confidence that the meters have not changed the circuit.

  1. Predict first. Equation 2 gives 0.05 A. The voltage across the resistor is the full 5 V, because it is the only load on the supply.
  2. Measure the voltage. Set the multimeter to DC volts on a range above 5 V, then place the red probe on the supply side and the black probe on the ground side of the resistor. The reading should be 5.0 V or very close to it.
  3. Measure the current. Move the red lead to the current input and set the function to DC current on a range above 50 mA. Break the wire at one end of the resistor and connect the meter in series so that the current flows through it. The reading should be 50 mA.
  4. Check the loading. A 10 MΩ voltmeter in parallel with 100 Ω leaves the parallel value very close to 100 Ω. A meter with a 0.1 Ω shunt in series raises the total from 100 Ω to 100.1 Ω, changing the current by about 0.1 %. Both errors are far below a bench meter's precision, so the measurement can be trusted.

The order of the steps is the point of the example. Because the answer was predicted before measuring, a wrong reading stands out immediately, and the loading check confirms in principle that the meter did not disturb the circuit. Both habits apply to every measurement you make.

Before measuring current, check the meter's current terminals, its function dial and its range. Start on the highest range when the value is unknown, and never move the probes while the circuit is powered.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

Each of these mistakes is common and has a recognisable symptom. On a real board you usually see the symptom before you think to check the connection, so it pays to know them.

An ammeter across the supply

A fuse blows the moment the meter is connected, the meter shows "OL" or a warning, or the supply shuts down. The meter has shorted the supply. Remove it, check the current terminals and the fuse rating, and put the meter in series with the load; before powering a circuit with an ammeter in it, trace the current path and confirm the meter is in that path and nowhere else.

A voltmeter in series

The circuit stops working when the meter is added: an LED goes out, a motor stops, or the reading is near zero where the full supply was expected. The voltmeter's very high resistance has broken the path. Move the probes across the part instead of into its path, and when a reading is zero and the circuit is dead, look at the connection before the part.

The wrong range or function

The reading is exactly 1000 times too large or too small, so 5 mA appears as 5 A or 3.3 V as 0.0033 V. The function or range setting does not match the quantity or its size. Start on the highest range when the size is unknown, and read the unit on the display before the number.

A scope with the wrong probe setting

A waveform looks ten times too large or too small for no obvious reason. The probe is in 10× mode while the scope is set to 1×, or the other way round. Match the attenuation setting on the scope to the switch on the probe, and check it every time you change probes.

The ground clip on the wrong node

A trace sits away from zero when the signal should be at zero, or the circuit shorts when the clip touches a live rail. Clip the scope's ground to the circuit's ground node, the same point the supply returns to, and when you need to measure across a part, use a differential probe or move the clip rather than forcing the reading.

Current and supply measurements in real projects

On a professional bench, measuring a board's supply current is routine. The usual method is to break the supply line at a jumper or test point, insert a current meter in series, and set the range high enough for the largest current expected. Many engineers instead fit a small series resistor, often a fraction of an ohm, and measure the voltage across it, because a voltage is easier to log and display on a scope than a current. This is Ohm's law from lesson 5 again: measure the drop across a known resistance and divide.

A clamp meter measures current without breaking the circuit, since it senses the magnetic field around a wire and so adds nothing in series. It suits a live board or a motor cable, but it is a poor choice for small currents, because the field is weak and the reading noisy. Knowing which tool you are using, and where its limit lies, is part of the measurement.

Experienced engineers also check the instrument before the circuit, confirming that the probe is in the right mode, the meter on the right function and the scope's ground on the right node. Code review follows the same principle, since a reviewer does not trust an output until the setup has been checked, and a careful engineer does not trust a reading until the setup is known.

Summary

  • Every meter is a component whose resistance joins the circuit, so a good meter is designed to make that effect negligible.
  • A voltmeter connects in parallel across the part and has a very high input resistance, while an ammeter connects in series and must have a very low resistance.
  • A voltmeter in series breaks the circuit, and an ammeter across a supply draws a huge current, so check the connection before switching on.
  • Loading is the error caused by a meter's own resistance. A 10 kΩ meter across the lower 10 kΩ of a divider reads 3.33 V instead of 5 V, so the meter's resistance should be at least a hundred times the resistance it measures across.
  • An oscilloscope plots voltage against time, and its ground clip sets the zero reference. A probe in 10× mode must be matched by the scope setting, or every reading is out by a factor of ten.
  • A logic analyzer records which digital pins were high or low and in what order. The Wokwi part wokwi-logic-analyzer has eight inputs, D0 to D7.
  • Predict the reading before measuring. A 5 V supply across 100 Ω gives 50 mA, so a reading far from that points to a fault in the setup or the circuit.
