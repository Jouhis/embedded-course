Measuring circuits

A meter only tells you the truth if you connect it correctly. The wrong connection can change the circuit, or break the meter, or both.

By the end of this lesson you can connect a voltmeter in parallel and an ammeter in series, read a value from each in CircuitJS, and say which instrument records digital signals over time.

The idea

A multimeter measures one quantity at a time. For voltage, it is connected in parallel with the part, across the two points whose difference you want. Its input resistance is very high, so it draws almost no current and barely changes the circuit. For current, the meter is connected in series, so the current you want flows through it. An ammeter has a very low resistance, which is why it must never go across a supply: the two would form a near short circuit. Many meters protect their current inputs with a fuse, which is a sign of how easily the wrong connection ends the meter's life. Set the function and the range before you connect the probes.

An oscilloscope plots voltage against time. It is the instrument for anything that changes: a PWM signal, the rise of a button press, the ripple on a supply. A scope probe connects in parallel, like a voltmeter. Many probes have a 10× setting that divides the signal before it reaches the scope, which loads the circuit less. For probe guidance, see Tektronix on probes.

A logic analyzer records the high and low levels on several digital pins over time, and a viewer decodes the protocols from the record. It answers a different question from a scope: not "how does this voltage change?" but "which pins were high, in what order?" You will use Wokwi's logic analyzer in lesson 60 to decode I²C. Its part is wokwi-logic-analyzer, with eight inputs, D0 to D7, and it saves the recording as a VCD file. Wokwi: Logic Analyzer part

Worked example with real numbers

A 5 V USB supply feeds a 100 Ω resistor. The voltmeter across the resistor reads 5 V, and the ammeter in series reads 50 mA. Now suppose someone connects the ammeter across the supply instead. The ammeter's resistance is only a fraction of an ohm, so the current would be the supply voltage divided by almost nothing, which could be amperes or more. The meter would overheat, its fuse would blow, and the supply could be damaged.

Before you measure current, check the meter's current terminals, its function dial, and its range. Start with the highest range if you do not know the value, and never move the probes while the circuit is powered.

Try it in CircuitJS

  1. Open the voltage divider example. The right-hand ladder has four 10 kΩ resistors in series across 10 V, with three output probes on its taps. Read the top output probe. The reading should be a fraction of 10 V.
  2. Open Ohm's law in CircuitJS. Add an ammeter in series with the 1 kΩ resistor: break the wire at one end of the resistor, and place the ammeter element between the broken ends. Read the current. It should match 5 V ÷ 1 kΩ.
  3. Add a voltmeter across the 1 kΩ resistor as well, and check that it reads 5 V. Then type the ammeter reading into the question below.
Hint

The CircuitJS voltmeter and ammeter are elements you add from the element menu. Their names may differ slightly by version. Look for the entries labelled as a probe or a meter, and place the ammeter so the current has no other way to go.

You read a voltage in parallel and a current in series, and you saw what happens when the connection is wrong. You now know which probe goes where on a bench, and why.

"How would you measure the current a board draws from its supply, and what could go wrong?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

Put an ammeter in series in the supply line, in the current-range terminals, with the range set high enough. Check the fuse rating. Never put the meter across the supply, because a low-resistance meter across a source draws a large current. For a live board where breaking the line is not practical, a clamp meter measures the field around the wire without any connection.
