Checkpoint 1: Electronics for Programmers

Sixteen questions across Part 1, mixed on purpose, then three prompts to explain out loud. Answer from memory before you check the reasons.

By the end of this checkpoint you can apply Ohm's law, power, dividers, LEDs, transistor and MOSFET switching, RC timing, inductive kickback, regulators, logic levels and datasheet limits to a single small design, and you know which lessons to revisit for each gap.

Instructions

Each question mixes topics from lessons 5 to 18, so you must choose the method yourself. Try each one on paper first. Some questions are numeric. Type the value in the units shown, and SI prefixes such as k and m are accepted. Each answer gives a reason, which also shows the tempting wrong answer. For any pin threshold, check the RP2040 datasheet rather than trusting your memory. If you miss a question, the reason names the lesson to revisit. The table at the end maps each topic to its lessons.

Explain out loud

Answer each prompt in about 60 seconds, out loud, without notes. Then open the model answer and compare.

1. "A 3.3 V microcontroller pin must switch a 12 V heater. Explain which MOSFET and which side, and why."

Model answer

A logic-level N-channel MOSFET on the low side, between the heater and ground. The pin drives the gate directly. A high-side N-channel would need its gate above 12 V, which a 3.3 V pin cannot give. I would check the threshold and on-resistance at 3.3 V on the datasheet, and calculate the dissipation as I² times RDS(on).

2. "A motor starts and the microcontroller resets. Walk through what you check, and what you add."

Model answer

I would look at the supply at the chip's pins on a scope during the start, to confirm a dip. Then I would check for a 100 nF decoupling capacitor at each supply pin and a larger bulk capacitor nearby. If the motor shares the rail, I would separate it with a filter or its own regulator, and check that its inductive kickback is clamped by a flyback diode.

3. "Why is a floating input a bug, and what do you do about it? Include the RP2040's thresholds at 3.3 V."

Model answer

A floating input has no defined voltage. At 3.3 V the RP2040 reads a high only above about 2.15 V and a low only below about 1.16 V, so a floating pin can sit in the band between them and read either value. The fix is a pull-up or pull-down, set in the design and in the firmware, with the pin's pull enabled by code. A 5 V signal is not a fix: the pin's limit is 3.8 V, so a level shifter or divider is needed.
