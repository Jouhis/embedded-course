PID control

Feedback from error, accumulated error and rate

By the end of this lesson you can write a discrete PID update with output limits and an anti-windup clamp, make it avoid derivative kick by differentiating the measurement, and tune a simulated heater to a settling time and overshoot that a test checks.

Holding a heater at its target temperature

Lesson 59 showed PWM, the microcontroller switching a pin on and off so that the fraction of each period the pin is high, the duty cycle, sets the average power, while lesson 58 showed how an ADC reads a thermistor and lesson 107 how to smooth that reading. This lesson closes the loop, the reading going in, a controller choosing the duty cycle and the heater's temperature responding, so with the sensor, the actuator and the filter already in place, the controller is the decision in the middle.

The concrete job is a heater that must hold a temperature, as printer hotends, filament dryers and sous-vide cookers all do, and a heater simply switched fully on until it reads the target keeps warming after the switch-off and overshoots, whereas one held at a fixed half power stops short, because the room keeps drawing heat away and the balance point sits below the target. A controller changes the power continuously in response to the error, which is what holds the temperature at the target.

Klipper, the firmware running many printers, shows the same choice, its heater control being either pid or watermark (a simple on-off rule), with the PID settings as three gains, pid_Kp, pid_Ki and pid_Kd (Klipper Config Reference), the three gains this lesson tunes.

Feedback from the error

A feedback controller measures the output, compares it with the setpoint and changes the input to shrink the difference, the error, error = setpoint − measurement, and the PID controller computes its output from three properties of that error, its present size (P), its running sum (I) and its rate of change (D). The output is the power sent to the heater, as a fraction from 0 to 1.

A PID loop runs on a fixed clock, once every sample period dt, and since the controller never sees the future but only the past and the present, tuning is a trade between speed and stability.

Setpoint, plant and closed loop

Five terms carry the whole lesson, the setpoint being the value the loop is trying to reach, here 60 °C, the measurement what the sensor reports right now and the error the setpoint minus the measurement, positive when the heater is too cold. The plant is the physical process being controlled, the heater element, its thermal mass and the room that cools it, and the closed loop is the arrangement in which the output is set from a measurement, the loop being closed through the sensor.

The controller runs on a fixed clock with a sample period dt, here one second, reading the measurement every sample, computing the error and writing a new output, a fraction from 0 to 1 of full power. Two measures judge the response, the overshoot, how far the temperature goes past the setpoint, and the settling time, the time after which the temperature stays within a stated tolerance of the setpoint, and a good loop keeps both small.

The P, I and D terms

  • P (proportional) reacts to the error now. Large error, large push. Alone it leaves an offset: a heater with P only settles where its heat loss balances kp × error, not at the setpoint.
  • I (integral) sums the error over time, so any leftover error keeps pushing until it is gone. It removes the offset, and it is the term that causes windup.
  • D (derivative) reacts to how fast the measurement is changing. It slows the approach and damps overshoot, and it amplifies noise, so it needs care.

The three terms resemble holding a car at 80 km/h with the pedal, P pressing harder the further below 80 you are, I noticing that you have been a little slow for a while on a long hill and adding a bit more pedal that stays, and D seeing the speed rise quickly and easing off early, so that you do not shoot past 80, each term answering a different question, how far off, for how long and how fast it is changing. The comparison breaks at the limits, though, because a driver knows that the pedal is already on the floor, whereas the integral term keeps adding up error behind a saturated output unless the code clamps it.

The standard tutorial on these terms and their implementation is Brett Beauregard's "Improving the Beginner's PID" series Beauregard, Improving the Beginner's PID. The same three terms appear in PID libraries and in industrial controllers. The names are fixed; the implementation details are where engineers differ.

Worked example: the first two seconds

The project's reference gains are kp = 0.04, ki = 0.0005 and kd = 0.5, with the setpoint at 60 °C, the room at 25 °C, a full-power rise of 80 °C and a time constant of 60 s, the plant updating once per second with T ← T + dt × (P × 80 − (T − 25)) ÷ 60, where P is the power from 0 to 1. Figure 1 showed the parts, and the numbers below show how they add up. Before following the steps, predict whether the heater runs at full power in the first second, and which term pulls the output down in the second.

  1. Second 1, the terms. The measurement is the room temperature, 25 °C, so the error is 35, P = 0.04 × 35 = 1.40, the integral starting at zero gains 0.0005 × 35 × 1 = 0.0175, and since there is no previous measurement yet, D is zero.
  2. Second 1, the output. The sum, 1.4175, is clamped by the output limit to 1.0, so the heater runs at full power, and the plant warms by (1.0 × 80 − 0) ÷ 60 = 1.333 °C, to 26.333 °C.
  3. Second 2, the terms. The measurement is 26.333 °C, so the error is 33.667 and P = 0.04 × 33.667 = 1.347, the integral grows by 0.0005 × 33.667 = 0.0168 to 0.0343, and D = −0.5 × (26.333 − 25) ÷ 1 = −0.667, because the temperature rose and the derivative pulls the output down.
  4. Second 2, the output. The sum is 1.347 + 0.034 − 0.667 = 0.714, inside the limits, so the power is 0.7143, and the plant warms by (0.7143 × 80 − 1.333) ÷ 60 = 0.930, to 27.264 °C.

The two rows match the simulator's CSV output exactly and show the three terms at work, P giving most of the push, I still small but growing, and D, the only term reacting to the rise, already taking a visible bite out of the output in the second second.

Discretisation of the controller and the plant

The controller runs once per sample period dt, so the integral becomes a sum and the derivative a difference, the output at sample n being

un = kp en + ki Σ ej dt − kd (yn − yn−1) ÷ dt(1)

where en is the error, yn the measurement, the sum runs over all samples so far and un is then clamped to the actuator's range, which the code writes as follows. 

e  = setpoint - measurement;
P  = kp * e;
I += ki * e * dt;                       /* rectangle rule: the integral over one sample */
D  = -kd * (measurement - last_meas) / dt;   /* derivative on measurement */
u  = clamp(P + I + D, out_min, out_max);

The first line is the error the controller acts on and the second the proportional term, simply the gain times the error, while the third adds to the integral the error times the gain times the sample period, the area of one rectangle under the error curve. The fourth is the derivative, the change in measurement since the last sample divided by dt to make it a rate, with a minus sign so that a rising temperature lowers the output, and the last line adds the three and clamps the result to the actuator's range. The project keeps the same arithmetic in pid_update, with the integral clamped before it is added, as the next section explains.

The plant is discretised the same way, the heater model being first order,

dT/dt = (P·k − (T − Tamb)) ÷ τ(2)

where T is the heater temperature, P the power from 0 to 1, k the full-power rise, Tamb the room temperature and τ the time constant, and Euler's step gives T ← T + dt·(P·k − (T − Tamb)) ÷ τ, accurate when dt is small compared with τ, which the simulation satisfies with dt = 1 s and τ = 60 s.

The sample period dt is in the code for a reason, because the gains are defined per second, so the sample period converts each step into seconds, and a controller sampled every 100 ms with the same gains is a different controller whose gains must be retuned. Keep dt in the same unit as the plant's time constant, here seconds, and check it whenever the sample rate changes.

Integral windup and the anti-windup clamp

If the output is clamped at 100 % while the error stays positive, the integral keeps growing, so that when the setpoint is reached it is far too large and the output stays high until the error has been negative for a long time, an overshoot called integral windup. The fix is to clamp the integral itself to the output range, so that it cannot store more than the actuator can use, which the code in this lesson does by clamping the I term to [out_min, out_max] on every step.

The effect is easy to see, because with ki = 1 and the output limited to 0 to 1, a hundred steps of error +10 leave the integral at 1 rather than 1000, so one step of error −10 brings the output straight to 0, whereas without the clamp the output would stay at 1 for about another hundred steps.

The clamp matters most in a real heater, which saturates, and the simulator tests this, removing the integral clamp in a scratch copy of the project with the starter gains (kp 0.05, ki 0.002, kd 0) raising the overshoot from 6.18 °C to 7.48 °C, while with the tuned gains the overshoot stayed at 0.63 °C either way, because the integral never reached its limit. The clamp therefore protects the loop when the output saturates and changes nothing when it does not.

Tuning the gains

Tuning proceeds in order, first P alone until the loop responds quickly without too much overshoot, then I until the offset goes away and finally D to damp the approach. The classic closed-loop rule is Ziegler and Nichols's method, raising kp until the loop oscillates steadily with period Tu at gain Ku and then setting kp = 0.6 Ku, Ti = Tu/2 and Td = Tu/8, which gives ki = kp/Ti and kd = kp·Td, a starting point that tends to overshoot, since a physical process rarely matches the model it assumes.

Set the output limits before you tune. A loop that saturates at 100 % during tuning looks unstable, when the real problem is windup.

The heater is slow and forgiving, a poor gain costing a few degrees of overshoot over several minutes, whereas a pole balanced on a cart is the opposite, falling in about a second when left alone, so the controller runs a hundred times a second and every term shows its job at once. The demo below uses the same update as the heater, the error being the pole's lean and the output the motor's force on the cart, limited as the heater's power is, with D taken on the measurement, and like real hardware the motor takes about 40 ms to reach the requested force while the tilt sensor is noisy. These two are why turning every gain up to the maximum fails, a large kp chasing the lagging motor into oscillation and a large kd turning the sensor noise into force that slams against the limit.

Hint for the I term

Add ki × error × dt to the stored integral, clamp the stored integral to the output range, and only then add it to the output. If you clamp only the final output, the integral still winds up behind the clamp.

Hint for the overshoot

The integral gain causes most overshoot in this model. Lowering ki from 0.002 to 0.0005 is a good first step, and the derivative gain can damp what is left.

To print the response, run projects/0108-pid-host/solution/build/pid_sim.exe > response.csv from the workspace root after building, the program app/pid_sim.c printing a CSV row per simulated second and its metrics on stderr, so the terminal shows final=60.00 C overshoot=0.63 C settle=83 s and response.csv has 1201 lines, the header and 1200 rows. Open response.csv in a spreadsheet, select the columns t_s, setpoint_c and temp_c and insert a line chart with t_s on the horizontal axis, whose curve should rise, touch the setpoint and settle.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

Only the output clamped

The overshoot is larger than the clamped version gives, 7.48 °C against 6.18 °C in the experiment above, because the final output was clamped while the integral kept growing, so the output looks correct but the integral grows far larger than the actuator can use and the loop overshoots when the error changes sign. The host test test_anti_windup_stops_the_integral_at_the_limit catches this, since after 100 saturated steps one step of −10 error must bring the output to zero, which an unclamped integral prevents, so clamp the stored integral before adding it to the output.

The wrong unit for dt

The power sits at the limit for the first several rows and the settling time looks suspiciously good, 32 s, because the controller took dt in milliseconds while the gains expect seconds, so passing 1000 as dt with the reference gains makes the integral grow a thousand times too fast, driving the power to 1.0000 on the second row where the correct loop reads 0.7143 and pinning the integral at its limit. Keep dt in seconds, the unit of the plant's time constant, and check the first few rows of the CSV against your hand calculation.

The wrong sign on the derivative

The settling time grows to 147 s with an overshoot of 0.00 °C, against the correct 83 s and 0.63 °C, while the three loop tests stay green, because 147 s is inside the 600 s limit, so only the unit test test_derivative_reacts_to_the_measurement_change fails, the cause being a dropped minus sign in the derivative term, which a learner tuning against the loop tests alone would miss. Check the derivative sign against the rule that a rising measurement must lower the output, and always compare the settling time with the reference rather than only with the pass limit.

No first-sample handling in the derivative

The first row of the CSV shows power 0.0000 although the room is cold and the setpoint is 60 °C, the opposite of what the controller should do, because without a previous measurement on the first call the derivative differentiates against zero. Use the first flag in pid_update, which makes the first derivative zero, as the code comment says.

Derivative on measurement, not on error

If the derivative uses the error, a setpoint step is a sudden jump in the error, so the D term kicks the output for one sample, whereas the measurement does not jump when the setpoint does, so differentiating the measurement avoids the kick, the minus sign being needed because a rising measurement must reduce the output, while the first sample, having no previous measurement, gets a D term of zero.

The kick can be put in numbers, a derivative of the error with kd = 0.5 and a setpoint step of 35 °C adding 0.5 × 35 = 17.5 to the output for one sample, whereas a derivative of the measurement adds nothing on that sample, because the temperature has not moved yet. The first sample is the one exception, handled in the code with a first flag, without which the previous measurement starts at zero and the derivative of a 25 °C reading against zero is −0.5 × 25 = −12.5, clamping the output to zero in the very first second.

PID loops in industrial and printer controllers

Industrial and printer controllers are PID loops with the same three terms and the same four traps, engineers tuning on the real plant rather than only in a model and logging the response as this lesson's CSV does, because a settling time and an overshoot are the two numbers a reviewer asks for first. Klipper exposes its heater gains in the printer configuration, and its documentation says that temperature measurements are smoothed over a time window for the PID derivative (pid_deriv_time), the same smoothing idea as the filter in lesson 107.

A code reviewer checks in a controller that the integral is clamped, that the derivative is on the measurement, that the first sample is handled and that dt has the right unit and is the real sample period, each point having a host test in this project, which is why the controller is written in plain C and tested on the PC before it runs on the Pico, where the same pid_update runs unchanged once a real sensor feeds it.

Summary

  • A PID controller sums three terms, P reacting to the error now, I removing the leftover error over time and D reacting to how fast the measurement changes.
  • The discrete form is one line per term per sample, with dt in seconds to match the plant's time constant.
  • The stored integral is clamped to the output range, so that it cannot store more than the actuator can use.
  • The measurement rather than the error is differentiated, so that a setpoint step does not kick the output, and a flag handles the first sample.
  • P is tuned first, then I to remove the offset and then D to damp the approach, checking the settling time as well as the overshoot.
  • A tuned loop is compared against a reference set, because a passing test can hide a slower or wrong loop.
