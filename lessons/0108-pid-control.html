PID control

A heater that is told "60 degrees" and simply turned on half-way will never get there. A PID controller reads the error, adds up the past error, and watches how fast the temperature is moving, then picks the power. Each term has one job and one way to go wrong.

By the end of this lesson you can write a discrete PID update with output limits and an anti-windup clamp, make it avoid derivative kick by differentiating the measurement, and tune a simulated heater to a settling time and overshoot that a test checks.

Where we are, and why a heater needs a controller

Lesson 59 showed PWM: the microcontroller switches a pin on and off, and the fraction of each period that the pin is high, the duty cycle, sets the average power. Lesson 58 showed how an ADC reads a thermistor, and lesson 107 showed how to smooth that reading. This lesson closes the loop. The reading goes in, a controller chooses the duty cycle, and the heater's temperature responds. The sensor, the actuator and the filter are already in place; the controller is the decision in the middle.

The concrete job is a heater that must hold a temperature. Printer hotends, filament dryers and sous-vide cookers all do it. A heater that is simply switched fully on until it reads the target keeps warming after the switch-off and overshoots. A heater held at a fixed half power stops short, because the room keeps drawing heat away and the balance point sits below the target. A controller changes the power continuously in response to the error, and that is what holds the temperature at the target.

Klipper, the firmware that runs many printers, shows the same choice. Its heater control is either pid or watermark (a simple on-off rule), and the PID settings are three gains, pid_Kp, pid_Ki and pid_Kd (Klipper Config Reference). Those are the three gains this lesson tunes.

The idea

A feedback controller measures the output, compares it with the setpoint, and changes the input to shrink the difference. The difference is the error: error = setpoint − measurement. The PID controller computes its output from three things about that error: its present size (P), its running sum (I), and its rate of change (D). The output is the power you send to the heater, as a fraction from 0 to 1.

A PID loop runs on a fixed clock, once every sample period dt. The controller never sees the future. Everything it knows is the past and the present, which is why tuning is a trade between speed and stability.

The loop, one word at a time

Five terms carry the whole lesson, and each one is defined here before it is used. The setpoint is the value the loop is trying to reach, here 60 °C. The measurement is what the sensor reports right now. The error is the setpoint minus the measurement, so it is positive when the heater is too cold. The plant is the physical process being controlled: the heater element, its thermal mass and the room that cools it. The closed loop is the arrangement in which the output is set from a measurement, so the loop is closed through the sensor.

The controller runs on a fixed clock with a sample period dt, here one second. Every sample it reads the measurement, computes the error, and writes a new output. The output in this lesson is a fraction from 0 to 1 of full power. Two measures judge the response. The overshoot is how far the temperature goes past the setpoint. The settling time is the time after which the temperature stays within a stated tolerance of the setpoint. A good loop has both small.

The P, I and D terms

  • P (proportional) reacts to the error now. Large error, large push. Alone it leaves an offset: a heater with P only settles where its heat loss balances kp × error, not at the setpoint.
  • I (integral) sums the error over time, so any leftover error keeps pushing until it is gone. It removes the offset, and it is the term that causes windup.
  • D (derivative) reacts to how fast the measurement is changing. It slows the approach and damps overshoot, and it amplifies noise, so it needs care.

Picture holding a car at 80 km/h with the pedal. P presses harder the further below 80 you are. I notices that you have been a little slow for a while, on a long hill, and adds a bit more pedal that stays. D sees the speed rising quickly and eases off early, so you do not shoot past 80. Each term answers a different question: how far off, for how long, and how fast it is changing.

The standard tutorial on these terms and their implementation is Brett Beauregard's "Improving the Beginner's PID" series Beauregard, Improving the Beginner's PID. The same three terms appear in PID libraries and in industrial controllers. The names are fixed; the implementation details are where engineers differ.

Worked example: the first two seconds

Use the reference gains from the project, kp = 0.04, ki = 0.0005 and kd = 0.5, with the setpoint at 60 °C, the room at 25 °C, a full-power rise of 80 °C and a time constant of 60 s. The plant updates once per second with T ← T + dt × (P × 80 − (T − 25)) ÷ 60, where P is the power from 0 to 1. Figure 1 showed the parts; the numbers below show how the parts add up.

Second 1. The measurement is the room temperature, 25 °C, so the error is 35. P = 0.04 × 35 = 1.40. The integral starts at zero and gains 0.0005 × 35 × 1 = 0.0175. There is no previous measurement yet, so D is zero. The sum is 1.4175, which the output limit clamps to 1.0, so the heater runs at full power. The plant then warms by (1.0 × 80 − 0) ÷ 60 = 1.333 °C, and the temperature becomes 26.333 °C.

Second 2. The measurement is 26.333 °C, so the error is 33.667. P = 0.04 × 33.667 = 1.347. The integral grows by 0.0005 × 33.667 = 0.0168, to 0.0343. D = −0.5 × (26.333 − 25) ÷ 1 = −0.667, because the temperature rose and the derivative pulls the output down. The sum is 1.347 + 0.034 − 0.667 = 0.714, inside the limits, so the power is 0.7143. The plant warms by (0.7143 × 80 − 1.333) ÷ 60 = 0.930, and the temperature becomes 27.264 °C.

The two rows match the simulator's CSV output exactly, and they show the three terms at work. P gives most of the push. I is small so far, and it will grow. D is the only term that reacts to the rise, and it already takes a visible bite out of the output in the second second.

Discretisation: one line per sample

The controller runs once per sample period dt, so the integral becomes a sum and the derivative a difference. The forms that code uses are: 

e  = setpoint - measurement;
P  = kp * e;
I += ki * e * dt;                       /* rectangle rule: the integral over one sample */
D  = -kd * (measurement - last_meas) / dt;   /* derivative on measurement */
u  = clamp(P + I + D, out_min, out_max);

The plant itself is discretised the same way. The heater model is first order: dT/dt = (P·k − (T − Tamb)) / τ. Euler's step gives T ← T + dt·(P·k − (T − Tamb))/τ. That step is accurate when dt is small compared with τ, which the simulation in this lesson satisfies with dt = 1 s and τ = 60 s.

Line by line: the first line is the error the controller acts on. The second is the proportional term, which is just the gain times the error. The third adds the integral, the error times the gain times the sample period, which is the area of one rectangle under the error curve. The fourth is the derivative: the change in measurement since the last sample, divided by dt to make it a rate, with a minus sign so that a rising temperature lowers the output. The last line adds the three and clamps the result to the actuator's range. In the project the same arithmetic lives in pid_update, with the integral clamped before it is added, which the next section explains.

The sample period dt is in the code for a reason. The gains are defined per second, so the sample period converts each step into seconds. A controller sampled every 100 ms with the same gains is a different controller, and the gains must be retuned. Keep dt in the same unit as the plant's time constant, here seconds, and check it when you change the sample rate.

Windup and the anti-windup clamp

If the output is clamped at 100 % while the error stays positive, the integral keeps growing. When the setpoint is reached, the integral is far too large, and the output stays high until the error has been negative for a long time. That overshoot is integral windup. The fix is to clamp the integral itself to the output range, so it cannot store more than the actuator can use. The code in this lesson clamps the I term to [out_min, out_max] on every step.

Windup is pressing the pedal to the floor while stuck behind a lorry on the hill, and writing yourself another "press harder" note every second. When the road clears, you must work through all those notes before you ease off, so the car overshoots. The anti-windup clamp stops writing notes while the pedal is already on the floor.

The effect is easy to see. With ki = 1 and the output limited to 0 to 1, a hundred steps of error +10 leave the integral at 1, not 1000. One step of error −10 then brings the output straight to 0. Without the clamp, the output would stay at 1 for about another hundred steps.

The clamp matters most in a real heater, because a real heater saturates. The simulator tests this. In a scratch copy of the project, with the starter gains (kp 0.05, ki 0.002, kd 0), removing the integral clamp raised the overshoot from 6.18 °C to 7.48 °C. With the tuned gains the overshoot stayed at 0.63 °C either way, because the integral never reached its limit with those gains. The clamp protects the loop when the output saturates, and it changes nothing when the loop does not saturate.

Tuning

Tune in this order: first P alone until the loop responds quickly without too much overshoot, then add I until the offset goes away, then add D to damp the approach. The classic closed-loop rule is Ziegler and Nichols's method: raise kp until the loop oscillates steadily with period Tu at gain Ku, then set kp = 0.6 Ku, Ti = Tu/2 and Td = Tu/8, which gives ki = kp/Ti and kd = kp·Td. The rule is a starting point. It tends to overshoot, and a physical process rarely matches the model it assumes.

Set the output limits before you tune. A loop that saturates at 100 % during tuning looks unstable, when the real problem is windup.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

Mistake 1: clamping only the output

The most common windup bug is to clamp the final output but let the integral keep growing. The output looks correct, but the integral is far larger than the actuator can use, and the loop overshoots when the error changes sign. The symptom is an overshoot larger than the clamped version gives, as in the experiment above, 7.48 °C against 6.18 °C. The host test test_anti_windup_stops_the_integral_at_the_limit catches it: after 100 saturated steps, one step of −10 error must bring the output to zero, and an unclamped integral keeps it high. The fix is the clamp on the stored integral, before it is added to the output.

Mistake 2: the wrong unit for dt

If the controller takes dt in milliseconds while the gains expect seconds, the integral grows a thousand times too fast. In the simulator, passing 1000 as dt with the reference gains drives the power to 1.0000 on the second row, where the correct loop reads 0.7143. The response reaches the setpoint in 32 s, but only because the integral is pinned at its limit and the output stays saturated. The symptom is power at the limit for the first several rows, and a settling time that looks suspiciously good. The fix is to keep dt in seconds, the same unit as the plant's time constant, and to check the first few rows of the CSV against your hand calculation.

Mistake 3: the wrong sign on the derivative

The minus sign in the derivative term is easy to drop. In the simulator, removing it with the reference gains changes the response to an overshoot of 0.00 °C and a settling time of 147 s, against the correct 0.63 °C and 83 s. The three loop tests still pass, because 147 s is inside the 600 s limit, so only the unit test test_derivative_reacts_to_the_measurement_change fails. A learner who tunes the gains against the loop tests alone would not see it. The symptom is a settling time much worse than the reference, with the loop tests green. The fix is to check the derivative sign against the rule in the text: a rising measurement must lower the output. Always compare the settling time with the reference, not only with the pass limit.

Mistake 4: no first-sample handling in the derivative

If the derivative has no previous measurement on the first call, it differentiates against zero. The symptom is the first row of the CSV showing power 0.0000 when the room is cold and the setpoint is 60 °C, which is the opposite of what the controller should do. The fix is the first flag in pid_update, which makes the first derivative zero, as the code comment says.

Derivative on measurement, not on error

If the derivative uses the error, a setpoint step is a sudden jump in the error, and the D term kicks the output for one sample. The measurement does not jump when the setpoint does, so differentiating the measurement avoids the kick. The minus sign is needed because a rising measurement must reduce the output. The first sample has no previous measurement, so its D term is zero.

Put numbers on the kick. With kd = 0.5 and a setpoint step of 35 °C, a derivative of the error would add 0.5 × 35 = 17.5 to the output for one sample. A derivative of the measurement adds nothing on that sample, because the temperature has not moved yet. The first sample is the one exception, and the code handles it with a first flag. Without that flag, the previous measurement starts at zero, and the derivative of a 25 °C reading against zero is −0.5 × 25 = −12.5, which clamps the output to zero on the very first second.

In real projects

Industrial and printer controllers are PID loops with the same three terms, and the same four traps. Engineers tune on the real plant, not only in a model, and they log the response the way this lesson's CSV does, because a settling time and an overshoot are the two numbers a reviewer asks for first. Klipper exposes its heater gains in the printer configuration, and its documentation says that temperature measurements are smoothed over a time window for the PID derivative (pid_deriv_time), which is the same smoothing idea as the filter in lesson 107.

A code reviewer checks four things in a controller: that the integral is clamped, that the derivative is on the measurement, that the first sample is handled, and that dt has the right unit and is the real sample period. Each of them has a host test in this project, which is why the controller is written in plain C and tested on the PC before it runs on the Pico. The same pid_update runs unchanged in the firmware, once a real sensor feeds it.

Summary

  • A PID controller sums three terms: P reacts to the error now, I removes the leftover error over time, and D reacts to how fast the measurement changes.
  • The discrete form is one line per term per sample, with dt in seconds to match the plant's time constant.
  • Clamp the stored integral to the output range, so it cannot store more than the actuator can use.
  • Differentiate the measurement, not the error, so a setpoint step does not kick the output; handle the first sample with a flag.
  • Tune P first, then I to remove the offset, then D to damp the approach, and check the settling time as well as the overshoot.
  • Compare a tuned loop against a reference set: a passing test can hide a slower or wrong loop.
