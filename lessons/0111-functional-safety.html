Functional safety: IEC 61508 and ISO 26262

A car's brake controller or a heater's thermal cut-off must still do the safe thing when a chip fails. Functional safety is the discipline of deciding how much that matters, and then building and proving that the firmware is safe enough.

By the end of this lesson you can explain what SIL and ASIL measure, sketch the V-model, name three firmware safety mechanisms and what each detects, and run a March C- RAM test that finds a stuck-at bit.

This lesson is optional. Functional safety is a career path of its own. The standards are paid documents, so this lesson cites them by title and uses freely available overviews. The hands-on part is a host-test project that you can run without hardware.

Where we are and why it matters

The earlier lessons gave you the tools a safety-critical product needs: interrupts and timers from lesson 57, the watchdog and HardFault analysis from lesson 84, the static analysis and MISRA C rules from lesson 83, and the unit tests with fakes from lessons 80 and 81. Each of those makes the firmware more reliable. None of them, on its own, says how reliable the product must be, or how anyone would prove it.

That question comes up on a real product. A brake controller, a steering motor, an airbag squib driver, a medical pump or the thermal cut-off on a 3D printer's heater all have a way to cause harm when they fail. A company that sells such a product must show a regulator or a customer that the chance of that harm is acceptably small, and it must show how it got there. Functional safety is the name for that discipline. This lesson introduces its vocabulary, one idea at a time, and then builds one of its firmware mechanisms.

Concept 1: a failure is not the same as a hazard

Reliability asks how often a system fails. Functional safety asks a narrower question: when the system fails, can it hurt someone, and how often would that harmful failure happen? A thermostat that stops working is annoying. A heater that keeps heating when it should stop is a hazard, because the room can catch fire. Both are failures, but only the second needs the safety machinery.

The formal term for the harm is a functional safety concern, and the standard that covers electrical, electronic and programmable electronic systems in general is IEC 61508. The automotive standard built on it is ISO 26262, which adapts the same ideas to road vehicles. Both are paid documents. A free overview from Freescale, now part of NXP, summarises them. Its white paper is titled Addressing the Challenges of Functional Safety in the Automotive and Industrial Markets (document FCTNLSFTYWP, revision 1, 2011 and 2013). Read the overview for the vocabulary, then read the standards through your employer or a certified course. The white paper is a vendor document from 2011 and 2013, so use its numbers as an overview and check the current standards before you rely on a level.

Concept 2: integrity levels, SIL and ASIL

Once a harmful failure is identified, the standards assign it an integrity level. The level is a target for how rarely that failure may happen, and it rises with the severity of the harm. IEC 61508 defines four Safety Integrity Levels (SIL), from SIL 1 to SIL 4, with SIL 4 the most stringent. ISO 26262 defines four Automotive Safety Integrity Levels (ASIL), from ASIL A to ASIL D, with ASIL D the most stringent.

The two scales are not the same. The white paper states that there is no direct correlation between SIL and ASIL levels, so an ASIL D function is not automatically a SIL 4 function. Keep the scales apart in your notes and in your interview answers. The white paper's table gives the random hardware failure targets: for SIL 4, a rate from 10−9 up to but not including 10−8 per hour, and for ASIL D, below 10−8 per hour Freescale (NXP) white paper FCTNLSFTYWP, failure measures table.

A level is a target, not a property of the chip. A higher level means more evidence: more reviews, more independent checks, more traceability and more testing of the safety mechanisms. Buying a chip with a safety label does not make the product meet a level by itself.

Concept 3: the V-model, development on the left and proof on the right

Safety standards follow a lifecycle that looks like a V. On the left side you go down from the requirements, to the system architecture, to the software design, to the code. On the right side you go back up, testing each level against the matching requirement: unit tests against the design, integration tests against the architecture, and system tests against the requirements. Each arm of the V has its own evidence, and the safety case collects it.

The V is useful because it makes the tests a planned output of the design. A requirement without a test on the right side of the V is a gap that a reviewer will find. The model is general practice; the standards spell out the steps in detail, and the details differ between IEC 61508 and ISO 26262.

Concept 4: HARA, the hazard analysis that comes first

The hazard analysis and risk assessment (HARA) starts before any design. For each function, you list the ways it can fail in a harmful way. Then you rate three things for each hazard: the severity of the harm, how often the situation occurs (exposure), and how controllable it is by the driver or operator. Those ratings give the ASIL, or in IEC 61508 the SIL. The glossary entry for HARA gives the short form.

Take the heater from the subtitle. One hazard is that the heater keeps heating when the controller asks it to stop. Severity is high, because a heater can start a fire. Exposure depends on how often the printer runs unattended. Controllability is low if the user cannot see the heater working. Those three ratings point to a high integrity level, and so to more than one independent protection. The rating sets how much evidence the design must carry.

Concept 5: faults, safe states and safety mechanisms

Three terms come up in every safety discussion. A stuck-at fault is a bit or line that always reads 0 or always reads 1, whatever was written. A safe state is the state the system moves to when a fault is detected, chosen so that no harm results, such as the heater off or the brake applied. A safety mechanism is the piece of hardware or software that detects the fault and moves the system to the safe state.

Three mechanisms are common on a small MCU. Each detects a different class of fault, so a product usually uses more than one:

  • Watchdog. A timer that resets the chip unless the code kicks it on time. It detects a program that has stopped making progress. Lesson 84 covers the independent and window watchdogs, which are different on the STM32.
  • RAM and flash self-tests. A March test writes and reads patterns over every byte of RAM, and detects stuck-at bits, which is the case you build below. A CRC over the flash image, computed at startup and compared with a stored value, detects a corrupted program. The CRC is the same idea as the checksums in lesson 103.
  • Plausibility checks. A plausibility check tests whether a value is physically possible, given its last value and the physics of the process. A heater's temperature cannot rise 40 °C in one second; a thermistor reading of 0 counts is a broken wire. The check runs on the sensor data, not on the hardware.

Redundancy (two channels that must agree) and a safe state (heater off, brake applied) complete the picture. Coding rules such as MISRA C (lesson 83) reduce the faults you put in, and the mechanisms above catch the faults that remain. Hardware and software both have to be covered, and a mechanism that only watches another mechanism is not enough on its own.

Concept 6: the safety case

A safety case is the argument, with evidence, that the system is acceptably safe. It names the hazards, the safety goals, the mechanisms that meet each goal, and the tests that show the mechanisms work. The evidence is the test results, the reviews and the traceability from requirement to test. A reviewer who cannot follow that chain will reject the case, however good the code is.

Worked example: a March test that finds a stuck bit

The RAM self-test is the mechanism you can build without hardware. A March test walks the memory in both directions, writing and reading 0 and 1 patterns, so every bit is seen in both states. The version in this project is March C-, which the Microchip SRAM diagnostic documentation describes as detecting stuck-at faults, addressing faults, transition faults and coupling faults Microchip SRAM diagnostic, DIAG_SRAM_MarchCMinus.

The March C- sequence has six passes. The first writes 0 to every address. The second walks up, reading 0 and writing 1. The third walks up, reading 1 and writing 0. The fourth and fifth repeat that pattern walking down. The sixth walks up once more, reading 0. Counting the operations per byte: pass 1 has one write, passes 2 to 5 have one read and one write each, and pass 6 has one read. That is 10 operations per byte, as the quiz below checks.

Now trace the project's simulated 64-byte RAM with a fault planted by the first test. The test sticks bit 3 of address 17 at 0, so that bit reads 0 whatever was written. Pass 1 writes 0x00 to address 17, and pass 2 reads it back: the stored value is 0x00, the stuck bit is already 0, so the read is 0x00 and the test expects 0x00. Pass 2 then writes 0xFF there, which is stored as 0xFF. In pass 3, the test reads address 17 expecting 0xFF. The stuck bit forces bit 3 to 0, so the read returns 0xF7. That is not 0xFF, so the function returns 17 at once.

The same reasoning shows the other fault. The second test sticks bit 0 of address 40 at 1. Pass 1 writes 0x00, but the read in pass 2 returns 0x01, because the stuck bit forces bit 0 high. The expected value is 0x00, so the function returns 40 in pass 2. A stuck-at-1 bit is caught earlier than a stuck-at-0 bit in this sequence, because pass 2 is the first to read a stored 0.

Code, line by line

The solution's march_c_minus() is short. Its helper and its first two passes look like this: 

static int check(const mem_iface_t *m, int addr, uint8_t expected) {
    return m->read(m->ctx, (uint8_t)addr) == expected;
}

int march_c_minus(const mem_iface_t *mem) {
    int n = mem->size;

    for (int a = 0; a < n; a++) {                 /* pass 1 */
        mem->write(mem->ctx, (uint8_t)a, 0x00u);
    }
    for (int a = 0; a < n; a++) {                 /* pass 2 */
        if (!check(mem, a, 0x00u)) return a;
        mem->write(mem->ctx, (uint8_t)a, 0xFFu);
    }

Line by line. The mem_iface_t argument is a struct with a context pointer, a read function and a write function. The March code calls read and write through those pointers and never touches the RAM directly. That is why the same function can test the real RAM on the chip or the simulated RAM in the host test, which is the callback pattern from lesson 49.

The check() helper reads one address and compares the result with the expected pattern. It returns 1 when they match. The comparison uses the expected pattern, not the value written earlier, and that choice is what makes stuck bits visible. The cast to uint8_t keeps only the low 8 bits of the address, so it wraps at 256. The 64-byte RAM is well inside that range.

The first loop is pass 1. It writes 0x00 to every address. The second loop is pass 2. It first checks that each address reads 0x00, which returns the address on failure, and then writes 0xFF. The check comes before the write, so the function always reads what the previous pass left behind. The remaining passes follow the same pattern, with the expected value and the direction changed as the sequence requires.

Try it: a March test that finds stuck bits

  1. Open projects/0111-march-host/starter in VS Code, or run python -I tools/build.py projects/0111-march-host/starter --test from the workspace root. The starter compiles, and its tests fail, because the function reports every RAM as healthy.
  2. Your task: write the six passes of march_c_minus() in src/march.c, following the comment at the top of the file. Return the address of the first wrong read, or -1 if every pass succeeds.
  3. You will know it works when the test summary reports 0 failures. Four tests plant a stuck bit and check that the function returns that address, one checks that healthy RAM passes, and one checks that the simulator itself shows the stuck bit on a plain read.

The reference solution is in projects/0111-march-host/solution; it passes all 6 tests.

The four fault tests are the ones that matter. A self-test deserves trust only after it has found faults you planted on purpose.

Hint for the passes

Each pass reads what the previous pass wrote and then writes the next value. Passes 2 and 3 walk up, passes 4 and 5 walk down, and pass 6 is a final read that walks up. Remember that the test writes to every address, so it destroys the RAM contents: that is why a real device runs it at startup.

Hint for the stuck-bit tests

The RAM simulator applies a stuck bit on every read, so the fault is visible only through a read. A pass that writes a cell but never reads it cannot see the fault at all. Each pass must read the cell and compare the result with the value its sequence expects.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

The first mistake is checking a pass against the wrong expected value. Pass 2 must expect 0x00, because pass 1 wrote 0x00, but a developer who copies the pass 3 check into pass 2 expects 0xFF. A healthy RAM then fails at address 0, and the healthy-RAM test reports 0 where it expects -1. The fix is to read the sequence from the comment at the top of the file, and to check each pass against the value the previous pass left behind.

The second mistake is returning the wrong thing from the function. A common version returns the value read, or the loop counter after the loop has finished, instead of the address that failed. The symptom is a fault test that reports the right failure, but with the wrong number, often one more than the address. The fix is to return a at the moment of the failed check, and nothing else.

The third mistake is running the test while the RAM holds live data. A developer adds the test to main after the stack and the globals are in use. The March test writes every byte, so it overwrites the stack and the variables, and the program crashes or behaves strangely at random. The symptom looks like a random fault that moves when the code changes. The fix is the comment in the solution's header: run the test at startup, before the RAM holds anything you need, or test a region that is not in use.

The fourth mistake is treating a passing self-test as a proof of safety. A team runs the March test at boot, sees it pass on every unit, and writes the safety case as finished. A stuck bit can appear later in the product's life, and the test only runs at boot, so the product can run for weeks with a failing cell. The fix is to state the test's coverage and interval in the safety case, and to add a runtime check or a plausibility check where the standard requires one. The test is one mechanism, not the whole case.

In real projects

Microcontroller vendors that sell into automotive and industrial markets publish safety manuals and self-test libraries for their parts. A team usually adopts those libraries, runs them at startup, and reuses the vendor's evidence where it applies. The team then adds its own requirements and tests, because the vendor cannot know the product's hazards. Reviewers ask for the traceability from each hazard to a mechanism and from each mechanism to a test.

In a code review of safety firmware, the checks are stricter than in ordinary firmware. Reviewers look for a coding standard, a documented set of deviations from it, static analysis with a clean report, and tests that fail on the injected fault before the fix. They also look for what the code does when a mechanism fails: the safe state must be entered on purpose, not by accident.

In interviews, the questions test the vocabulary and the reasoning. Expect to define SIL and ASIL without mixing them, to name a safety mechanism and what it detects, and to explain why a plausibility check is not a range check. The model answer below covers the ASIL question. Learn the vocabulary first, then the reasoning.

You wrote a RAM self-test that finds stuck-at faults at the right address, and you can explain the integrity levels, the V-model and the safety mechanisms that a safety engineer would discuss with you in an interview.

"What is ASIL D, and what does it change in your firmware?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

ASIL D is the most stringent automotive integrity level, set by the hazard analysis from severity, exposure and controllability. It demands a stricter failure target, usually below 10−8 per hour, and more evidence. In firmware that means independent safety mechanisms such as a watchdog, RAM and flash self-tests, plausibility checks and a defined safe state; a coding standard such as MISRA C; traceability from each requirement to a test; and a safety case that ties the evidence together. The level does not change the code's syntax; it changes how much of the code must be checked, reviewed and tested.

Summary

  • Functional safety asks how often a harmful failure may happen, not how often the system fails.
  • IEC 61508 uses SIL 1 to 4; ISO 26262 uses ASIL A to D. The scales do not correspond directly.
  • The HARA rates severity, exposure and controllability for each hazard, and that rating sets the level.
  • The V-model pairs each level of design with a test on the right side; the safety case collects the evidence.
  • A safety mechanism detects a fault and moves the system to a safe state: watchdogs, self-tests, plausibility checks.
  • A March C- test uses six passes and finds a stuck-at-0 bit in pass 3 and a stuck-at-1 bit in pass 2.
  • Compare the read with the expected pattern, run the test before the RAM is in use, and never count a passing test as the whole case.
