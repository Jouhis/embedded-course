Functional safety: IEC 61508 and ISO 26262

A car's brake controller or a heater's thermal cut-off must still do the safe thing when a chip fails. Functional safety is the discipline of deciding how much that matters, and then building and proving that the firmware is safe enough.

By the end of this lesson you can explain what SIL and ASIL measure, sketch the V-model, name three firmware safety mechanisms and what each detects, and run a March C- RAM test that finds a stuck-at bit.

This lesson is optional. Functional safety is a career path of its own. The standards are paid documents, so this lesson cites them by title and uses freely available overviews. The hands-on part is a host-test project that you can run without hardware.

The idea: risk, not perfection

Functional safety asks a narrower question than reliability. It asks whether a malfunction of an electrical or electronic system could cause harm, and how often that must be prevented. A system does not need to be perfect; it needs its dangerous failures to be rare enough, and detected or controlled when they happen. The generic standard is IEC 61508, titled "Functional safety of electrical/electronic/programmable electronic safety-related systems", and the automotive standard built on it is ISO 26262, titled "Road vehicles: Functional safety". Both are paid documents; cite them by title, and read a free overview first.

The free overview used here is NXP's white paper on the two standards. It says that IEC 61508 defines four Safety Integrity Levels (SILs), with SIL 4 the most stringent, and that ISO 26262 defines four Automotive Safety Integrity Levels (ASILs), with ASIL D the most stringent. It also states that there is no direct correlation between the two scales NXP, Functional safety white paper. The white paper is a 2013 vendor document, so read its numbers as an overview and check the current standards before you rely on a level.

SIL and ASIL: how much risk is allowed

A level is a target for how often a dangerous failure may occur, with the level set by how bad the harm could be. The white paper gives the random hardware failure targets: for SIL 4 about 10−9 to 10−8 per hour, and for ASIL D below 10−8 per hour NXP, Functional safety white paper. A higher level means more evidence, more reviews and more independent checks, not just a better chip.

The V-model: development on the left, proof on the right

Safety standards follow a lifecycle that looks like a V. On the left side you go down from the requirements, to the system architecture, to the software design, to the code. On the right side you go back up, testing each level against the matching requirement: unit tests against the design, integration tests against the architecture, and system tests against the requirements. Each arm of the V has its own evidence, and the safety case collects it. The model is general practice; the standards spell out the steps in detail.

HARA: find the hazards first

The hazard analysis and risk assessment (HARA) starts before any design. For each function, list the ways it can fail in a harmful way, rate the severity of the harm, how often the situation occurs, and how controllable it is by the driver or operator. That rating gives the ASIL, or in IEC 61508 the SIL. A heater that can keep heating when it should stop is a hazard; the rating decides how many independent protections it needs.

Safety mechanisms in firmware

A safety mechanism is code or hardware that detects a fault and moves the system to a safe state. Three are common on a small MCU:

  • Watchdog. A timer that resets the chip unless the code kicks it on time. It detects a program that has stopped making progress. Lesson 84 covers the independent and window watchdogs.
  • RAM and flash self-tests. A March test writes and reads patterns over every byte of RAM, and detects stuck-at bits, which is the case you build below. A CRC over the flash image, computed at startup and compared with a stored value, detects a corrupted program. The CRC is the same idea as the checksums in lesson 101.
  • Plausibility checks. A value that the physics forbids is a fault. A heater's temperature cannot rise 40 °C in one second; a thermistor reading of 0 counts is a broken wire. The check runs on the sensor data, not on the hardware.

Redundancy (two channels that must agree) and a safe state (heater off, brake applied) complete the picture. Coding rules such as MISRA C (lesson 83) reduce the faults you put in, and the mechanisms above catch the faults that remain. Hardware and software both have to be covered.

The safety case

A safety case is the argument, with evidence, that the system is acceptably safe. It names the hazards, the safety goals, the mechanisms that meet each goal, and the tests that show the mechanisms work. The evidence is the test results, the reviews and the traceability from requirement to test. A reviewer who cannot follow that chain will reject the case, however good the code is.

Try it: a March test that finds stuck bits

A March test is the RAM self-test from the safety-mechanism list. It walks the memory in both directions, writing and reading 0 and 1 patterns, so every bit is seen in both states. A bit stuck at 0 fails the read of 1, and a bit stuck at 1 fails the read of 0. Microchip's SRAM diagnostic documentation describes March C- as detecting stuck-at faults among others Microchip SRAM diagnostic, DIAG_SRAM_MarchCMinus.

  1. Open projects/0109-march-host/starter in VS Code, or run python -I tools/build.py projects/0109-march-host/starter --test from the workspace root.
  2. Your task: write the six passes of march_c_minus() in src/march.c, following the comment at the top of the file. Return the address of the first wrong read, or -1 if every pass succeeds.
  3. You will know it works when the test summary reports 0 failures. The three stuck-at tests check that the fault is found at the right address. The reference solution is in projects/0109-march-host/solution; it passes all 6 tests.
Hint for the passes

Each pass reads what the previous pass wrote and then writes the next value. Passes 2 and 3 walk up, passes 4 and 5 walk down, and pass 6 is a final read. Remember that the test writes to every address, so it destroys the RAM contents: that is why a real device runs it at startup.

Hint for the stuck-bit tests

The simulator forces a bit on every read, so the test must compare the read-back with the expected pattern, not with what was written. If you compare with the write, the stuck bit goes unnoticed.

"What is ASIL D, and what does it change in your firmware?" Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

ASIL D is the most stringent automotive integrity level, set by the hazard analysis from severity, exposure and controllability. It demands a stricter failure target, usually below 10−8 per hour, and more evidence. In firmware that means independent safety mechanisms such as a watchdog, RAM and flash self-tests, plausibility checks and a defined safe state; a coding standard such as MISRA C; traceability from each requirement to a test; and a safety case that ties the evidence together. The level does not change the code's syntax; it changes how much of the code must be checked, reviewed and tested.
