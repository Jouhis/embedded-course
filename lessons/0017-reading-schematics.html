Reading schematics

A schematic is the board's specification in drawing form. Learning to read it quickly is the difference between debugging the hardware and guessing.

By the end of this lesson you can identify the parts, reference designators and nets of a small schematic, follow a Pico GPIO pin to its net, and predict the voltage at a labelled net by reading the schematic.

Where we are, and why this matters

The last four lessons were about parts in isolation: how a resistor drops voltage (lesson 8), how a pull-up holds a pin high (lesson 16), how a regulator wastes power (lesson 15), and how a MOSFET switches a load (lesson 12). A real board puts all of them on one drawing, with dozens of parts and a few hundred connections. The drawing is called a schematic, and it is the document that every other step on a board depends on. The layout comes from it, the parts list comes from it, and the first person who debugs a board uses it to decide what to measure.

The job this lesson prepares you for is the first hour with a board you did not draw yourself. A product engineer hands you a schematic and a board that does not work, and you have to say which pin should read what, and where the signal should be. If you cannot read the drawing, you will guess and probe at random. If you can, you will predict the voltage on a net before you pick up the meter, and the meter will either agree or show you the fault.

What a schematic shows, and what it does not

A schematic shows electrical connections. It does not show where anything sits on the board, how long a wire is, or how thick a track must be. Those belong to the layout, which is the next design step. Each part is drawn as a symbol, which is a simplified picture with pins at its ends. A resistor is a box or a zig-zag, a capacitor is a pair of parallel plates, and a connector is a set of numbered pins. The symbol conventions are described in the KiCad 9 schematic editor manual, which is the reference for the drawing tool used on most open-source boards (KiCad 9.0 documentation, Eeschema, Electrical Connections). A schematic tells you what is connected to what. It does not tell you how long a wire is or where the parts sit. Those belong to the PCB layout.

Two drawn lines that cross are not automatically connected. The KiCad manual is explicit that wires which cross are not joined, and that a connection is made only where a wire ends on another wire or where a junction dot is placed (KiCad 9.0 documentation, Eeschema, Wire Junctions). That convention is why a missing dot is a real bug in the drawing, and why you check every crossing on a critical net.

Reference designators and values

Each part has a reference designator: a letter for its type and a number, such as R1 for a resistor, C3 for a capacitor, U1 for an integrated circuit, SW1 for a switch and J2 for a connector. The value, such as 10 kΩ or 100 nF, sits next to it. The designator is how the bill of materials and the board refer to the part, so when you debug a board, write the designator in your notes. A designator is not the part's manufacturer number, which is a separate field in the parts list.

Nets, net labels and power symbols

A net is a set of connected points that are all at one voltage. A net can be one wire, or many wires and labels joined into one electrical node. You trace a net, not a wire, because the net is what has a voltage. A net label such as ADC_IN is a name written on a net. The KiCad manual distinguishes three kinds. A local label connects only within one sheet. A global label connects anywhere in the schematic, even on another sheet, and a hierarchical label joins a sub-sheet to its parent (KiCad 9.0 documentation, Eeschema, Labels). So two sheets that both say ADC_IN are connected only if that label is global, and reading the label type is part of reading the net.

A power symbol such as 3V3 or GND is a label for a supply net. It joins every point with that name, the same way a global label does. A power flag (KiCad calls it PWR_FLAG) is a different thing. It tells the electrical rule checker that a net has a source. The KiCad manual says it indicates to the checker that a net is powered, and that the same result can be reached by connecting a real power output to the net (KiCad 9.0 documentation, Eeschema, PWR_FLAG). A power flag does not create voltage and does not connect anything. It only silences a rule check. A missing power flag is a warning about the drawing, not a fault on the board. A net with no source is a real problem, and the checker reports it in the same way, so read the message before you add a flag.

Decoupling capacitors and pull resistors: patterns to look for

Experienced readers scan a board for a few standard patterns before they read the detail. A decoupling capacitor sits beside each supply pin of a chip, and it supplies the short current bursts that the chip draws when its gates switch, so the supply does not dip. A pull resistor on a button or an I2C line sets the default level of that line (lesson 16). A connector has a designator and a set of pins, and each pin is connected to one net. When you find those three things, the rest of the drawing becomes readable, because the rest is mostly signals and their names.

Reading a Pico pinout

A pinout is a table from pin names to the board's physical pins. The Pico exposes 26 of the RP2040's 30 GPIO pins by routing them to header pins. GPIO0 to GPIO22 are digital only, and GPIO26 to GPIO28 can be used as digital pins or as ADC inputs, chosen in software (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, section 2.1, Raspberry Pi Pico pinout). Two other GPIOs are used on the board for jobs you should not reuse: GPIO23 controls the regulator's power-save pin, and GPIO24 watches whether VBUS, the USB 5 V, is present (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, power chain section).

The most common trap is the name. A schematic names a net GP14, which means the RP2040's GPIO14. That pin is package pin 17 on the chip (RP2040 datasheet, Table 615, GPIO pins). The number printed on the Pico's board is a different number again, so look up GP14 on the Pico pinout in Figure 2 or Figure 4 of the board datasheet, and do not count pins by eye. Also note that GPIO26 to GPIO29 have a reverse diode to the 3.3 V IO supply, so a voltage above about 300 mV over that supply should not be applied to them (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, section 2.1). Board schematics usually have a decoupling capacitor beside each supply pin, pull-ups on buttons and I2C lines, and a connector for each external signal. Find those three things first, and the rest of the drawing becomes readable.

A worked example: the ADC_IN divider

Take the schematic in Figure 1. The supply net 3V3 is at 3.3 V, and it reaches ground through two 10 kΩ resistors in series. The junction between them is the net ADC_IN. Predict its voltage before you measure. The current in the chain is 3.3 V ÷ (10 kΩ + 10 kΩ) = 0.165 mA. The voltage across the bottom resistor is that current times 10 kΩ, which is 1.65 V. Because the two resistors are equal, the junction sits at half the supply. The divider formula from lesson 8 gives the same answer: 3.3 V × 10 kΩ ÷ (10 kΩ + 10 kΩ) = 1.65 V.

Now add the capacitor C1, which is 100 nF on the same net. A capacitor draws no current once it has charged, so the steady voltage on ADC_IN is still 1.65 V. What changes is the speed. Seen from C1, the two resistors act as one resistance in parallel, Rth = 10 kΩ ∥ 10 kΩ = 5 kΩ. The time constant is τ = R × C = 5 kΩ × 100 nF = 0.5 ms. In one time constant the net moves about 63 % of the way to its final value, and after about five time constants it is within about 1 % of it. So if 3V3 switches on at time zero, ADC_IN takes about 2.5 ms to settle. That is the delay an analogue input has to respect before it samples. Lesson 13 introduced the capacitor's charging curve, and this is the same curve on a real net.

Last, a prediction you can test. If R2 becomes 20 kΩ, the junction voltage becomes 3.3 V × 20 kΩ ÷ (10 kΩ + 20 kΩ) = 2.2 V. The top resistor stays the same, so the supply to the net has not changed, only the ratio. Before you measure, write the prediction, then read the probe. The gap between prediction and reading is where the faults hide.

Each line is one element of the drawing. The first line holds the simulator's global settings, and you can leave it alone. The v line is the supply, drawn from the bottom node up to the top node, and its 3.3 V is the voltage you set. The first w line is a wire from the supply's top terminal to the top of R1, and the r line after it is R1, with its end points and then 10000 Ω as the last value. The second r line is R2, and the second w line closes the loop back to the supply's bottom terminal, which is the ground reference. The O line is an output probe at the junction, the net ADC_IN. Its label reads "out" by default, and it can show the voltage if you enable that option.

The netlist leaves out C1, because the purpose of the exercise is the DC divider. Add a capacitor afterwards if you want to see the 0.5 ms settling, using the element's own dialog.

  1. Open the circuit with the button above, and check it against Figure 1. The top resistor is R1, the bottom is R2, and the probe between them is the net ADC_IN.
  2. Before you measure, predict the voltage at ADC_IN. The divider gives 3.3 V × 10 kΩ ÷ (10 kΩ + 10 kΩ), and you should write 1.65 V on paper.
  3. Read the probe's voltage. The reading should be 1.65 V, and it should match your prediction.
  4. Now change R2 to 20 kΩ, predict the new value, then set it and read the probe again. Your prediction is 2.2 V.
Hint

Trace the net, not the wire. The probe sits on the node between the resistors, so its voltage is the voltage of the junction. If the reading is 3.3 V, one of the resistors is missing or set to zero.

The reading should be 1.65 V. With R2 at 20 kΩ, the prediction is 2.2 V. If your second reading does not match, check that you changed R2 and not R1, because the two resistors look the same on the drawing.

The netlist, explained

The CircuitJS netlist is a plain-text description of the drawing, one element per line. The letter at the start of a line says what the element is. Those letters are fixed in the simulator's source: v is a voltage source, r a resistor, w a wire and O an output probe (CircuitJS source, VoltageElm.java, getDumpType), (ResistorElm.java), (OutputElm.java). The first four numbers after the letter are the element's two end points on the screen, then come its flags and its own settings. That is why a resistor line ends with a number of ohms, and a voltage source line carries its voltage among its settings.

Read the netlist in the same order you read the schematic. Start from the supply, follow the wire to R1, follow R1 to the probe, then to R2 and back to ground. That is a trace of one net, written as text. The discipline of tracing nets in the drawing is the same discipline the netlist asks for.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

The first mistake is to assume two crossing wires are connected. In the drawing they look joined, and the designer intended them to be. The symptom on the board is a pin that is floating, or a signal that never arrives, and the rule checker reports the pin as unconnected. The fix is to check for a junction dot at every crossing that should be a connection, and to trace the net end to end before you assume it is complete.

The second mistake is a misspelt net label. A label reading ADC_IN on one sheet and ADC_1N on another is two nets, not one. The symptom is a net that has a source on one side and no load on the other, and a board where the signal is present at the connector but not at the chip. The fix is to search the schematic for each label and count its occurrences, and to check the label type, because a local label does not join another sheet at all.

The third mistake is to add a power flag to clear a warning without reading the warning. The flag removes the message, and the board still has no source on that rail. The symptom is a 3V3 rail that reads 0 V on the board, after the drawing passed the rule check. The fix is the one the KiCad manual gives: find the real power output that should feed the net, and connect it. Use the flag only when the source exists and the checker cannot see it.

The fourth mistake is to confuse the GPIO name with the pin number. A reviewer reads GP14 on the schematic, then counts pins on the board to find it, and lands on the wrong pin. The symptom is a button that works on the wrong pin, or a LED that does not light, when the code is correct. The fix is the rule from the pinout section: use the net name to find the pin in the board's own figure, and check that figure, not your count.

In real projects

A professional schematic review is a checklist, and the first item is the power. Each supply pin on each chip has a source, a decoupling capacitor and a ground connection. Each rail has its voltage and its maximum load written next to it. Each reset line has its pull and any reset capacitor. Each connector pin has its net named. Only after those checks does the reviewer read the signal paths, because a board that is powered wrongly cannot be debugged by reading its signals.

Bring-up is the practical version of the same habit. An engineer applies power with a current limit set low, reads each rail with a meter, and compares it with the predicted value from the schematic. A rail that reads 0 V points to a missing source. A rail that reads low points to a load that is too big or a connection that is open. Reading the drawing first is what makes these measurements meaningful, because you know which node to probe and what it should read.

Interviewers ask about schematics because the question tests whether you can follow a design without help. A strong answer names the supplies first, then the reset and the debug connector, then the pinout against the chip's datasheet, and it says what you would measure at each step before power is applied.

Summary

  • A schematic shows connections, not placement. Crossing wires are joined only at a junction dot.
  • A reference designator names a part on the drawing, such as R1, C3 or U1. It is not the manufacturer's part number.
  • A net is a set of points at one voltage. Trace nets, not wires.
  • A local label connects within one sheet, and a global label connects across sheets. Check the label type before you assume a link.
  • A power flag tells the rule checker a net has a source. It does not create voltage or connect anything.
  • The Pico exposes 26 of the RP2040's 30 GPIOs, and GP23 and GP24 are used on the board for the regulator and VBUS sense.
  • An RC net settles in about five time constants, where τ = R × C. For two 10 kΩ resistors and 100 nF, τ is 0.5 ms.

"You get a board schematic. Walk me through how you would check the power and reset pins before you power it on." Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

I would find the supply nets and their sources first, then check that each supply pin of the MCU has its decoupling capacitor and that the ground pins are connected to one ground net. I would trace the reset net, check its pull-up or pull-down and any reset capacitor, and confirm the debug connector's pins. I would then check the pinout against the MCU datasheet, so every GPIO is used with the function the drawing claims. Only then would I apply power, with a current limit set.
