Reading schematics

The circuit drawing of a board and how to trace it

By the end of this lesson you can identify the parts, reference designators and nets of a small schematic, follow a Pico GPIO pin to its net, and predict the voltage at a labelled net by reading the schematic.

One drawing for the whole board

The last few lessons treated parts in isolation: how a resistor drops voltage in lesson 8, how a MOSFET switches a load in lesson 12, how a regulator wastes power in lesson 15 and how a pull-up holds a pin high in lesson 16. A real board puts all of them into one drawing with dozens of parts and a few hundred connections. That drawing is the schematic, from which both the PCB layout and the parts list are derived, and whoever debugs the board uses it first to decide what to measure.

The situation this lesson prepares you for is the first hour with a board someone else drew. You receive a schematic and a board that does not work, and you have to say which pin should read what and where each signal should appear. Without reading the drawing you can only probe at random, whereas with it you can predict the voltage on a net before picking up the meter, so that the meter either agrees or points to the fault.

Symbols, wires and junctions

A schematic shows electrical connections only. It says nothing about where parts sit on the board, how long a connection is or how wide a track must be, since those belong to the layout, the next design step. Each part is drawn as a symbol, a simplified picture with pins at its ends: a resistor is a box or a zig-zag, a capacitor a pair of parallel plates and a connector a set of numbered pins. The symbol conventions are described in the KiCad 9 schematic editor manual, the reference for the drawing tool used on most open-source boards (KiCad 9.0 documentation, Eeschema, Electrical Connections). A schematic tells you what is connected to what, never how long a wire is or where the parts sit. Those belong to the PCB layout.

Two lines that cross on the drawing are not automatically connected. The KiCad manual states explicitly that crossing wires are not joined, and that a connection exists only where a wire ends on another wire or where a junction dot is placed (KiCad 9.0 documentation, Eeschema, Wire Junctions). This convention makes a missing dot a real bug in the drawing, which is why every crossing on a critical net deserves a check.

Reference designators and values

Each part carries a reference designator, a letter for its type followed by a number, such as R1 for a resistor, C3 for a capacitor, U1 for an integrated circuit, SW1 for a switch and J2 for a connector. Its value, such as 10 kΩ or 100 nF, sits next to it. The bill of materials and the board both refer to the part by its designator, so that is the name to write in your notes while debugging. The designator is not the manufacturer's part number, which is a separate field in the parts list.

Nets, net labels and power symbols

A net is a set of connected points that all share one voltage. It may be a single wire or many wires and labels joined into one electrical node, and since the net is what has a voltage, you trace nets rather than wires. A net label such as ADC_IN is a name attached to a net, and the KiCad manual distinguishes three kinds. A local label connects only within one sheet, a global label connects anywhere in the schematic, even across sheets, and a hierarchical label joins a sub-sheet to its parent (KiCad 9.0 documentation, Eeschema, Labels). Two sheets that both say ADC_IN are therefore connected only if the label is global, so reading the label type is part of reading the net.

A power symbol such as 3V3 or GND is a label for a supply net and joins every point with that name, as a global label does. A power flag, which KiCad calls PWR_FLAG, is something else: it tells the electrical rule checker that a net has a source. The KiCad manual describes it as an indication to the checker that a net is powered, and notes that connecting a real power output to the net achieves the same result (KiCad 9.0 documentation, Eeschema, PWR_FLAG). A power flag creates no voltage and connects nothing; it only silences a rule check.

A missing power flag is a warning about the drawing, not a fault on the board. A net with no source is a real problem, and the checker reports it in the same way, so read the message before adding a flag.
  1. Every part has a reference designator and a value: R1 and R2 are 10 kΩ resistors, C1 is a 100 nF capacitor. The designator is how the parts list, the board and the schematic refer to one part.
  2. Power symbols are labels for supply nets. Every 3V3 symbol on the sheet is the same net, even where no wire joins them, and so is every GND.
  3. A net label works the same way. The ADC_IN flag at the divider and the ADC_IN flag at the Pico's GP26 pin are one net, so the divider feeds GP26 although no wire is drawn between them.
  4. Two wires that cross are not connected. A connection exists only where a wire ends on another, or where a junction dot is drawn. Check every crossing on a critical net.
  5. Now read the circuit. At DC, C1 draws no current, so ADC_IN is the divider's midpoint: 3.3 V × 10k ÷ (10k + 10k) = 1.65 V.

Decoupling capacitors, pull resistors and connectors

Experienced readers scan a board for a few standard patterns before reading the detail. A decoupling capacitor sits beside each supply pin of a chip and supplies the short current bursts the chip draws as its gates switch, so that the supply does not dip. A pull resistor on a button or an I2C line sets that line's default level, as lesson 16 showed. A connector has a designator and a set of pins, each connected to one net.

A schematic resembles a metro map, which shows which stations connect while drawing the lines straight and tidy, and says nothing about where the stations stand on the street. Two stations side by side on the map can be far apart in the city, and in the same way two parts drawn next to each other may sit on opposite sides of the board. The board layout is the street map. Unlike a metro map, though, a schematic is exact about every connection, so a missing line is a missing wire.

The Pico pinout

A pinout maps pin names to a board's physical pins. The Pico exposes 26 of the RP2040's 30 GPIO pins on its headers: GPIO0 to GPIO22 are digital only, and GPIO26 to GPIO28 can serve as digital pins or as ADC inputs, selected in software (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, section 2.1, Raspberry Pi Pico pinout). Two other GPIOs have jobs on the board that you should not take over, since GPIO23 controls the regulator's power-save pin and GPIO24 senses whether VBUS, the USB 5 V, is present (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, power chain section).

The most common trap lies in the names. A schematic net called GP14 means the RP2040's GPIO14, which is package pin 17 on the chip (RP2040 datasheet, Table 615, GPIO pins), and the number printed on the Pico board is different again. Look GP14 up on the Pico pinout in Figure 2 or Figure 4 of the board datasheet rather than counting pins by eye. Note also that GPIO26 to GPIO29 have a reverse diode to the 3.3 V IO supply, so no voltage more than about 300 mV above that supply should be applied to them (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, section 2.1). Board schematics usually have a decoupling capacitor beside each supply pin, pull-ups on buttons and I2C lines, and a connector for each external signal. Finding those three things first makes the rest of the drawing readable.

Worked example: the ADC_IN divider

Take the schematic in Figure 1. The supply net 3V3 at 3.3 V reaches ground through two 10 kΩ resistors in series, and the junction between them is the net ADC_IN. We predict its voltage before measuring anything. The current in the chain is 3.3 V ÷ (10 kΩ + 10 kΩ) = 0.165 mA, and the voltage across the bottom resistor is that current times 10 kΩ, or 1.65 V. Because the resistors are equal, the junction sits at half the supply, and the divider formula from lesson 8 confirms it:

VADC_IN = 3.3 V × 10 kΩ ÷ (10 kΩ + 10 kΩ) = 1.65 V(1)

Adding C1, 100 nF on the same net, leaves this steady value unchanged, since a charged capacitor draws no current. What changes is the speed. Seen from C1, the two resistors act as one resistance in parallel, Rth = 10 kΩ ∥ 10 kΩ = 5 kΩ, so the time constant is

τ = Rth × C1 = 5 kΩ × 100 nF = 0.5 ms(2)

where Rth is the resistance seen by the capacitor. In one time constant the net moves about 63 % of the way to its final value, and after about five it is within about 1 %, so if 3V3 switches on at time zero, ADC_IN settles in about 2.5 ms. That is the delay an analogue input must respect before sampling, and it is the charging curve from lesson 13 appearing on a real net.

Finally, a prediction you can test. If R2 becomes 20 kΩ, the junction voltage becomes 3.3 V × 20 kΩ ÷ (10 kΩ + 20 kΩ) = 2.2 V, because the ratio has changed while the supply has not. Write the prediction down before measuring and then read the probe, so that any mismatch between the two points to a fault.

Try it in CircuitJS

The schematic above is a divider from 3V3 to ground with a filter capacitor. At DC the capacitor draws no current, so the value at ADC_IN depends only on R1 and R2. The netlist below is the built-in voltage divider in tools/maintainer/circuitjs/voltdivide.txt with its second branch removed, which keeps the same verified line forms, with the source set to 3.3 V and both resistors at 10 kΩ. Open Falstad CircuitJS, a free browser simulator, and paste the netlist as in the earlier lessons.

v 112 368 112 48 0 0 40.0 3.3 0.0
w 112 48 240 48 0
r 240 48 240 208 0 10000
r 240 208 240 368 0 10000
w 112 368 240 368 0
O 240 208 304 208 1

Each line is one element of the drawing. The first line holds the simulator's global settings and can be left alone. The v line is the supply, drawn from the bottom node up to the top node, and its 3.3 V is the voltage you set. The first w line is a wire from the supply's top terminal to the top of R1, and the r line after it is R1, with its end points followed by 10000 Ω as the last value. The second r line is R2, the second w line closes the loop back to the supply's bottom terminal, which is the ground reference, and the O line is an output probe at the junction, the net ADC_IN. Its label reads "out" by default, and it can display the voltage if you enable that option.

The netlist leaves out C1, because the exercise concerns the DC divider. Add a capacitor afterwards through the element's own dialog if you want to see the 0.5 ms settling.

  1. Open the circuit with the button above and check it against Figure 1. The top resistor is R1, the bottom one R2, and the probe between them is the net ADC_IN.
  2. Before measuring, predict the voltage at ADC_IN with Equation 1 and write 1.65 V on paper.
  3. Read the probe's voltage. It should read 1.65 V, matching your prediction.
  4. Change R2 to 20 kΩ, predict the new value, then set it and read the probe again. The prediction is 2.2 V.
Hint

Trace the net, not the wire. The probe sits on the node between the resistors, so it shows the junction voltage, and a reading of 3.3 V means one of the resistors is missing or set to zero.

The reading should be 1.65 V, and with R2 at 20 kΩ the prediction is 2.2 V. If the second reading does not match, check that you changed R2 rather than R1, because the two resistors look alike on the drawing.

The CircuitJS netlist format

The CircuitJS netlist is a plain-text description of the drawing, one element per line, with a letter at the start of each line naming the element. The letters are fixed in the simulator's source, where v is a voltage source, r a resistor, w a wire and O an output probe (CircuitJS source, VoltageElm.java, getDumpType), (ResistorElm.java), (OutputElm.java). The first four numbers after the letter are the element's two end points on screen, followed by its flags and its own settings, which is why a resistor line ends with a number of ohms and a voltage source line carries its voltage among its settings.

The netlist is best read in the same order as the schematic: start at the supply, follow the wire to R1, then R1 to the probe, then R2 and back to ground. That is a trace of one net written as text, and it asks for exactly the discipline that tracing nets in the drawing does.

You read a schematic's parts, designators and net labels, predicted the voltage on a labelled net and confirmed it by measurement. That is the first step in bringing up a board you did not draw yourself.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

Crossing wires assumed to be connected

On the drawing two wires look joined, and the designer meant them to be. On the board a pin floats or a signal never arrives, and the rule checker reports the pin as unconnected. Check for a junction dot at every crossing that should be a connection, and trace the net from end to end before assuming it is complete.

A misspelt net label

A label reading ADC_IN on one sheet and ADC_1N on another creates two nets instead of one. The net has a source on one side and no load on the other, and the signal is present at the connector but not at the chip. Search the schematic for each label and count its occurrences, and check the label type, because a local label never joins another sheet.

A power flag added to silence a warning

The flag removes the message, but the rail still has no source, so a 3V3 rail reads 0 V on a board whose drawing passed the rule check. Follow the KiCad manual: find the real power output that should feed the net and connect it. Use the flag only where a source exists that the checker cannot see.

GPIO names confused with pin numbers

A reviewer reads GP14 on the schematic, counts pins on the board to find it, and lands on the wrong one. A button then works on the wrong pin or an LED stays dark even though the code is correct. Use the net name to find the pin in the board's own pinout figure, as the pinout section describes, and trust the figure rather than your count.

Schematic reviews and board bring-up

A professional schematic review follows a checklist, and power comes first. Each supply pin on each chip needs a source, a decoupling capacitor and a ground connection, each rail has its voltage and maximum load written beside it, each reset line has its pull and any reset capacitor, and each connector pin has a named net. Only then does the reviewer read the signal paths, because a board that is powered wrongly cannot be debugged by reading its signals.

Bring-up is the practical form of the same habit. An engineer applies power with a low current limit, reads each rail with a meter, and compares it with the value predicted from the schematic. A rail at 0 V points to a missing source, and one reading low to an excessive load or an open connection. Reading the drawing first is what gives these measurements meaning, since you know which node to probe and what it should read.

Summary

  • A schematic shows connections, not placement, and crossing wires are joined only at a junction dot.
  • A reference designator names a part on the drawing, such as R1, C3 or U1, and is not the manufacturer's part number.
  • A net is a set of points at one voltage, so trace nets rather than wires.
  • A local label connects within one sheet and a global label across sheets, so check the label type before assuming a link.
  • A power flag tells the rule checker that a net has a source; it creates no voltage and connects nothing.
  • The Pico exposes 26 of the RP2040's 30 GPIOs, and GP23 and GP24 are used on the board for the regulator and VBUS sensing.
  • An RC net settles in about five time constants, where τ = R × C; for two 10 kΩ resistors and 100 nF, τ is 0.5 ms.
