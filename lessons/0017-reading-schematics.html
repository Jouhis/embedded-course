Reading schematics

A schematic is the board's specification in drawing form. Learning to read it quickly is the difference between debugging the hardware and guessing.

By the end of this lesson you can identify the parts, reference designators and nets of a small schematic, follow a Pico GPIO pin to its net, and predict the voltage at a labelled net by reading the schematic.

The idea

A schematic shows the electrical connections, not the physical layout. Each part is a symbol, and each wire or net label is a connection. Two symbols joined by a line are connected, and two identically named net labels are connected even if they are on different sheets. The symbols follow standard conventions: a box or zig-zag for a resistor, and a pair of parallel plates for a capacitor. The KiCad documentation describes the symbol and label conventions in its schematic editor reference (KiCad documentation). A schematic tells you what is connected to what. It does not tell you how long a wire is or where the parts sit. Those belong to the PCB layout.

Reference designators and values

Each part has a reference designator: a letter for its type and a number, such as R1 for a resistor, C3 for a capacitor, U1 for an integrated circuit, SW1 for a switch and J2 for a connector. The value, such as 10 kΩ or 100 nF, sits next to it. The designator is how the bill of materials and the board refer to the part, so when you debug a board, write the designator in your notes.

Nets, net labels and power symbols

A net is a set of connected points that are all at one voltage. Wires name the net only when a label sits on them. A net label such as ADC_IN connects every point with that name, even on a different sheet, and a power symbol such as 3V3 or GND does the same for supply nets. A power flag (KiCad calls it PWR_FLAG) is a symbol that tells the electrical rule checker that a net has a power source. It does not create any voltage, and it does not connect anything, so adding one is only the fix for a rule-check warning. A missing power flag is a warning about the drawing, not a fault on the board. A net with no source is a real problem, and the checker reports it in the same way, so read the message before you add a flag.

Reading a Pico pinout and a small board

A pinout is a table from pin names to the board's physical pins. The Pico has GP0 to GP22 and GP26 to GP28 as GPIOs, a 3V3 output, a VBUS input for USB at about 5 V, a VSYS input and several GND pins. The datasheet gives the pin map and power section (Raspberry Pi Pico datasheet, pinout and power sections). When a schematic names a net as GP14, the board is connecting that net to the MCU's GPIO 14 pin. Board schematics usually have a decoupling capacitor beside each supply pin, pull-ups on buttons and I2C lines, and a connector for each external signal. Find those three things first, and the rest of the drawing becomes readable.

  2. Before you measure, predict the voltage at ADC_IN. The divider gives 3.3 V × 10 kΩ ÷ (10 kΩ + 10 kΩ).
  3. Read the probe's voltage. Compare with your prediction.
  4. Now change R2 to 20 kΩ in your head, predict the new value, then set it and read the probe again.
Hint

Trace the net, not the wire. The probe sits on the node between the resistors, so its voltage is the voltage of the junction. If the reading is 3.3 V, one of the resistors is missing or set to zero.

The reading should be 1.65 V. With R2 at 20 kΩ, the prediction is 2.2 V.

You read a schematic's parts, designators and net labels, predicted the voltage on a labelled net, and confirmed it by measurement. That is the first step when you bring up a board you have not drawn yourself.

"You get a board schematic. Walk me through how you would check the power and reset pins before you power it on." Answer out loud in 60 seconds, then compare.

Model answer

I would find the supply nets and their sources first, then check that each supply pin of the MCU has its decoupling capacitor and that the ground pins are connected to one ground net. I would trace the reset net, check its pull-up or pull-down and any reset capacitor, and confirm the debug connector's pins. I would then check the pinout against the MCU datasheet, so every GPIO is used with the function the drawing claims. Only then would I apply power, with a current limit set.
