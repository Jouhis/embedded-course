Diodes, LEDs, and current-limiting resistors

One-way conductors, and the resistor that sets an LED's current

By the end of this lesson you can choose the resistor for an LED on a 3.3 V GPIO, using R = (Vsupply − Vf) ÷ I and an E12 value, and check the result against the RP2040 pin's drive limit.

Components that do not follow Ohm's law

Lessons 5 to 8 covered Ohm's law, power, series and parallel networks and dividers, all of which treated components as resistors that obey V = I × R, so their current rises in proportion to the voltage. An LED behaves quite differently. Once a threshold is passed its current rises very steeply, so Ohm's law alone cannot predict it, and this lesson covers what Ohm's law leaves out.

The situation appears on every board. A GPIO pin is a digital output that drives a status LED, a relay driver or a transistor gate, and an LED connected straight to the pin draws a current set by the pin's internal resistance and the LED itself rather than by your design. The status lights on a 3D printer's control board are driven this way, and a board that burns out an LED has usually skipped the step described here. Choosing the resistor is the first design decision that balances a pin's limits against a component's needs.

Diodes and forward voltage

A diode passes current in one direction only. Connected one way round it conducts, and reversed it passes almost nothing. The transition is abrupt: below a threshold voltage the diode carries almost no current, and above it the current rises very steeply for a small increase in voltage.

That threshold is the forward voltage, Vf, the voltage across the diode while it conducts, which depends on the semiconductor material. The All About Circuits textbook covers semiconductors and the diode's I–V curve, which plots current against voltage and shows the sharp knee. The steepness is the problem. Because the current rises so quickly, a small change in voltage produces a large change in current, and a diode connected straight to a supply draws whatever the supply's and the diode's internal resistances allow, which can be far too much or far too little.

The steep rise has a physical cause. A diode is a junction between two kinds of semiconductor, and above the forward voltage charge carriers cross that junction easily, so more voltage means more carriers and therefore more current. Ohm's law cannot describe this, because the resistance of a conducting diode is not a fixed number but changes with the current.

A diode works like a check valve, the one-way flap in a pump's pipe. Pushing in the right direction lifts the flap once the pressure is high enough, and water flows; pushing the other way seals it. The pressure needed to lift the flap corresponds to the forward voltage. The comparison fails at high reverse voltage, since a valve holds until it bursts, whereas a diode starts conducting backwards at its reverse breakdown voltage, which Zener diodes are designed to do deliberately.

LEDs and the series resistor

An LED is a diode that emits light while it conducts. Its colour is determined by the semiconductor's band gap, the energy step an electron drops through when it recombines. A larger step releases more energy per photon, which means a shorter wavelength, so a red LED has a lower forward voltage than a blue one. The band-gap physics is not needed to use an LED, but the colour does indicate which Vf to expect.

A typical red LED has a forward voltage of about 2.0 V; the SparkFun listing for a basic red 5 mm LED gives 2.0 V typical, within a range of 1.8 to 2.2 V. SparkFun basic red 5 mm LED. The value for your own LED is in its datasheet or its supplier's listing, and that is the number to use rather than the one in this lesson.

Since an LED's brightness and lifetime depend on its current, you design it around current rather than voltage. A series resistor makes the supply and the LED share the voltage: the LED takes its forward voltage and the resistor takes the remainder. The required resistance is therefore

R = (Vsupply − Vf) ÷ I(1)

where Vsupply is the pin or supply voltage, Vf the LED's forward voltage and I the chosen current. The subtraction gives the voltage across the resistor, and dividing by the current gives the resistance. The same current flows through the resistor and the LED because they form a single series path, as lesson 7 established. Choosing I is the design decision, and both the LED's rating and the pin's limit constrain it.

Worked example: a red LED on a Pico pin

A Pico GPIO drives a red LED from 3.3 V. Assume Vf = 2.0 V and a target of 4 mA, which is the pin's default drive strength according to section 5.5.2.1 of the RP2040 datasheet; a later section explains why this matters. Estimate the voltage the resistor sees before starting.

  1. Write the knowns in base units: Vsupply = 3.3 V, Vf = 2.0 V, I = 4 mA = 0.004 A.
  2. Find the voltage across the resistor: 3.3 V − 2.0 V = 1.3 V. This is the step that accounts for the LED.
  3. Apply Equation 1: R = 1.3 V ÷ 0.004 A = 325 Ω.
  4. Choose a standard value: the nearest standard values around 325 Ω are 270 Ω and 330 Ω. Round up to 330 Ω so that the current stays at or below the target.
  5. Check the actual current: 1.3 V ÷ 330 Ω = 0.0039 A, or 3.9 mA, just under the target.

It is worth noting what the calculation avoided. Using the full 3.3 V as the resistor's voltage would give 825 Ω and about 1.6 mA, a dim LED, and using the LED's 2.0 V would be equally wrong, since that is not what the resistor sees. The subtraction is the step that most often goes wrong, and the next section explains how to choose between the two standard values.

  1. Below its forward voltage, an LED passes almost no current. At 1 V across it, nothing happens.
  2. Near its forward voltage, about 2.0 V for a red LED, the current climbs steeply. A few hundredths of a volt more can double it, so a voltage alone cannot set an LED's current.
  3. Put a resistor in series. The pin's 3.3 V is shared: the LED keeps about 2.0 V, and the resistor gets the remaining 1.3 V.
  4. The resistor sets the current with Ohm's law: R = 1.3 V ÷ 4 mA = 325 Ω. Round up to the E12 value 330 Ω, and the current is 1.3 ÷ 330 = 3.9 mA.

E12 preferred values and rounding up

Resistors are made in preferred values rather than every value you could calculate. The E12 series has twelve values per decade, 1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 2.2, 2.7, 3.3, 3.9, 4.7, 5.6, 6.8 and 8.2, each multiplied by a power of ten. The series is standardised in IEC 60063, and the course's calculator uses the same twelve values.

Rounding up is the safe direction for an LED. A larger resistor gives a smaller current, so the LED runs slightly dimmer but can never be overdriven. Rounding down to 270 Ω here would give 4.8 mA, above the 4 mA target. Since neighbouring values are about 20 % apart, the direction of rounding matters even though the exact answer is only approximate.

GPIO drive strength and current limits

A pin can supply only a limited current. Each GPIO pad has an output drive strength set in software, which the RP2040 datasheet lists as 2, 4, 8 or 12 mA in section 2.19.4 (Pads), with a default of 4 mA given in the pin types table of section 5.5.2.1. RP2040 datasheet, section 2.19.4 (Pads) and section 5.5.2.1 (default 4 mA). The setting lives in bits 5 and 4 of the GPIO's pad control register, described in section 2.19.6.3. The same datasheet limits the total current across all the IOVDD pins to 50 mA, in section 5.5.3.4, Table 625. The drive strength is a nominal value rather than a hard limit. The datasheet states that a pin can source more than its setting if you accept a lower output voltage. Lesson 51 covers GPIO output from C.

A single LED at 4 mA is therefore comfortably within the pin's limit. The 50 mA figure, however, is a total for all the IOVDD pins together, so eight LEDs at 4 mA, 32 mA in all, fit only if the remaining pins source no more than 18 mA between them. A board with many LEDs must add up the currents, which is the KCL habit from lesson 7 applied to the pins.

Never connect an LED straight to a GPIO without a resistor. The pin holds its output near 3.3 V, and once the LED passes its forward voltage a small rise in voltage produces a large rise in current. Only the pin's own resistance and the LED then limit the current, which can exceed both the pin's drive limit and the LED's rating, so the resistor is not optional.

Sizing the resistor in C

The calculation is simple enough to code, and a helper that rounds up to an E12 value is useful in any tool that sizes parts. The program below computes the exact resistance for the worked example and then rounds it up. 

#include <stdio.h>

/* Smallest E12 value at or above r, for r from 1 to 1000 ohms. */
static float e12_up(float r)
{
    static const float e12[] = {1.0f, 1.2f, 1.5f, 1.8f, 2.2f, 2.7f,
                                3.3f, 3.9f, 4.7f, 5.6f, 6.8f, 8.2f};
    float decade = 1.0f;
    while (r > decade * 10.0f) {
        decade *= 10.0f;
    }
    for (int i = 0; i < 12; i++) {
        if (e12[i] * decade >= r) {
            return e12[i] * decade;
        }
    }
    return 10.0f * decade;
}

int main(void)
{
    float exact = (3.3f - 2.0f) / 0.004f;   /* 1.3 V across 4 mA */
    printf("exact %.0f ohm, E12 %.0f ohm\n", (double)exact, (double)e12_up(exact));
    return 0;
}

The e12 array holds the twelve mantissas, the values before the decade is applied, and is declared static const so it is stored once and cannot change. The helper e12_up takes a resistance and returns the smallest standard value at or above it, which is the round-up rule from the worked example.

The while loop finds the decade by multiplying decade by ten until the target fits below decade × 10; for 325 Ω the decade becomes 100. The for loop then walks the array, multiplying each mantissa by the decade, and returns the first product that is at least r, which for 325 Ω is 3.3 × 100 = 330. If no value in the decade fits, the function returns the next decade's 1.0 value, 10 × decade.

In main, the exact resistance (3.3f − 2.0f) / 0.004f evaluates to 325 Ω, and printf prints it alongside the E12 value, giving the line exact 325 ohm, E12 330 ohm. The f suffixes keep the arithmetic in float, which is precise enough for sizing a resistor.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

An LED with no resistor

The LED is wired straight from the pin to ground. It glows very brightly, then dims or fails within a short time, and the pin runs warm. Always fit the resistor, since the LED has no current limit of its own.

The supply voltage used instead of the resistor's share

Computing R = 3.3 V ÷ 4 mA = 825 Ω gives a noticeably dim LED, because the resistor actually sees only 1.3 V and passes 1.6 mA. A meter across the resistor shows about 1.3 V rather than 3.3 V. Subtract Vf before dividing.

Rounding down to the next E12 value

Choosing 270 Ω because it looks like the nearest value raises the current to 4.8 mA. The difference is barely visible on the LED but obvious on a meter. Round up, as the rule in this lesson says.

A reversed LED

With the cathode on the pin side, the LED stays dark and essentially no current flows. Identify the anode and cathode from the flat side or the shorter lead of a through-hole LED, and connect the anode towards the resistor and the pin.

A current beyond the pin's drive strength

Designing for 20 mA on a pin set to 4 mA leaves the LED dim and the output voltage sagging, so the pin no longer reaches its high level under load. Choose a current the pin supports at its drive setting, or raise the drive strength after checking the datasheet's limits.

Diodes and LED drive in real designs

Every GPIO-driven LED on a board is an instance of this calculation, and a hardware review checks each one. The reviewer asks what current the LED needs, what the pin's drive setting is, and what the whole bank of pins supplies together. A good schematic writes the target current and the resistor value next to the LED, so that nobody has to repeat the arithmetic or can silently change the value.

The same component serves other purposes in real products. A diode placed across a relay or motor coil protects the driver from the voltage spike the coil produces when its current is switched off, and a diode in series with a supply stops a reversed battery from driving current the wrong way. Lesson 14 covers the coil case in detail, but the principle remains the one from this lesson: the diode has a forward voltage, conducts in one direction only, and the surrounding circuit must be designed for the current it passes.

In firmware, a pin is typically configured once and then forgotten, which is where trouble starts. A driver that changes the drive strength in code, or turns on several outputs at once, changes the pin current without any change to the schematic. Good code therefore states the intended current in a comment next to the setup call, and a reviewer checks it against the datasheet; the GPIO code in lesson 51 is the place to practise that habit.

Summary

  • A diode conducts strongly only above its forward voltage, and an LED is a diode that emits light.
  • An LED needs a current-limiting resistor in series, because Ohm's law does not set its current.
  • Compute R = (Vsupply − Vf) ÷ I, using the forward voltage from the LED's datasheet.
  • Round up to the next E12 value so that the current stays at or below the target.
  • The RP2040's default drive strength is 4 mA, and the 50 mA IOVDD total is shared by all the GPIO and QSPI pins.
  • Connect the anode towards the supply side and the cathode towards ground, and confirm the current with a meter rather than by assumption.
RP2040 datasheet: section 2.19.4 (Pads) for the drive strengths 2, 4, 8 and 12 mA, section 5.5.2.1 for the default of 4 mA, and section 5.5.3.4 (Table 625) for the 50 mA total. Read the pad description before you trust any drive-strength number.
