Diodes, LEDs, and current-limiting resistors

An LED does not limit its own current, so the resistor in front of it has to. Choosing that resistor is the first design decision you make about a GPIO pin.

By the end of this lesson you can choose the resistor for an LED on a 3.3 V GPIO, using R = (Vsupply − Vf) ÷ I and an E12 value, and check the result against the RP2040 pin's drive limit.

Where we are and why it matters

Lessons 5 to 8 gave you Ohm's law, power, series and parallel networks, and dividers. Every one of them treated the components as resistors, which obey V = I × R, so the current rises in a straight line with the voltage. An LED does not behave that way. Its current rises very steeply once a threshold is passed, so Ohm's law alone cannot tell you the current. This lesson covers what Ohm's law leaves out.

The problem is one you will meet on every board. A GPIO pin is a digital output that drives a status LED, a relay driver or a gate. Connect an LED straight to the pin and the current is set by the pin's internal resistance and by the LED, not by you. On a 3D printer, the status LEDs and the lights on the control board are all driven this way, and a board that burns out an LED has usually skipped this step. Choosing the resistor is the first decision in which you balance the pin's limits against the LED's needs.

Diodes pass current in one direction

A diode is a component that passes current in one direction. Connect it one way round and current flows; reverse it and almost nothing flows. The change is abrupt. Below a threshold voltage, the diode passes almost no current. Above it, the current rises very steeply for a small increase in voltage.

The threshold is called the forward voltage, abbreviated Vf. It is the voltage across the diode when it is conducting, and it depends on the semiconductor material. The All About Circuits textbook covers semiconductors and the diode's I–V curve, which plots current against voltage and shows the sharp knee. The steepness is the problem. Because the current rises so fast, a small change in voltage gives a large change in current. Connect a diode straight to a supply and the current is set by the supply's internal resistance and the diode's own resistance, which can be far too high or far too low.

The steep rise has a physical cause. A diode is a junction between two kinds of semiconductor, and above the forward voltage, charge carriers cross the junction easily. More voltage means more carriers, and so more current. Ohm's law has no place for this, because the resistance of a conducting diode is not a fixed number. It changes with the current.

A diode is a check valve, the one-way flap in a pump's pipe. Push the right way and, once the pressure is high enough to lift the flap, water flows. Push the other way and the flap seals. The pressure needed to lift the flap is the forward voltage. The picture breaks at a high reverse voltage: a valve holds until it bursts, while a diode starts to conduct backwards at its reverse breakdown voltage, which Zener diodes are built to do on purpose.

LEDs turn current into light

An LED is a diode that emits light when it conducts. The colour is set by the semiconductor's band gap, the energy step that an electron drops through when it recombines. A larger step releases more energy per photon, which is a shorter wavelength, so a red LED has a lower forward voltage than a blue one. You do not need the band-gap physics to use an LED, but the colour does tell you which Vf to expect.

A typical red LED has a forward voltage of about 2.0 V. The SparkFun listing for a basic red 5 mm LED gives a typical forward voltage of 2.0 V and a range of 1.8 to 2.2 V. SparkFun basic red 5 mm LED. The value for your own LED is in its datasheet or its supplier's listing, and it is the number you must use, not the number in this lesson.

Because brightness and lifetime depend on current, you design an LED around current, not voltage. The series resistor makes the supply and the LED share the voltage. The LED takes its forward voltage, Vf, and the resistor takes whatever is left over. Writing that as a formula:

R = (Vsupply − Vf) ÷ I

Read the formula as two steps. First, subtract the LED's drop from the supply, which gives the voltage across the resistor. Second, divide that voltage by the current you want. The current is the same through the resistor and the LED, because they form a series path, which is the lesson 7 rule at work. Choosing I is a design decision, and it is set by the LED's rating and by the pin's limit.

Worked example, step by step

A Pico GPIO drives a red LED at 3.3 V. Assume Vf = 2.0 V, and target 4 mA. The 4 mA is the pin's default drive strength, which section 5.5.2.1 of the RP2040 datasheet gives, and the next section explains why it matters.

  1. Write the knowns in base units: Vsupply = 3.3 V, Vf = 2.0 V, I = 4 mA = 0.004 A.
  2. Find the voltage across the resistor: 3.3 V − 2.0 V = 1.3 V.
  3. Find the exact resistance: R = 1.3 V ÷ 0.004 A = 325 Ω.
  4. Choose a standard value: the nearest standard values around 325 Ω are 270 Ω and 330 Ω. Round up to 330 Ω, so the current stays at or below the target.
  5. Check the actual current: 1.3 V ÷ 330 Ω = 0.0039 A, which is 3.9 mA, just under the target.

Notice what the calculation did not do. It did not use 3.3 V as the voltage across the resistor, which would give 825 Ω and about 1.6 mA, a dim LED. It did not use the LED's 2.0 V as the voltage across the resistor, which is not what the resistor sees. The subtraction is the step that most often goes wrong, and the next section shows how to choose between the two standard values.

  1. Below its forward voltage, an LED passes almost no current. At 1 V across it, nothing happens.
  2. Near its forward voltage, about 2.0 V for a red LED, the current climbs steeply. A few hundredths of a volt more can double it, so a voltage alone cannot set an LED's current.
  3. Put a resistor in series. The pin's 3.3 V is shared: the LED keeps about 2.0 V, and the resistor gets the remaining 1.3 V.
  4. The resistor sets the current with Ohm's law: R = 1.3 V ÷ 4 mA = 325 Ω. Round up to the E12 value 330 Ω, and the current is 1.3 ÷ 330 = 3.9 mA.

E12 values: why you round up

Resistors come in preferred values, not in every value you could calculate. The E12 series has twelve values per decade: 1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 2.2, 2.7, 3.3, 3.9, 4.7, 5.6, 6.8 and 8.2, each multiplied by a power of ten. The series is standardised in IEC 60063, and the course's calculator uses the same twelve values.

Rounding up is the safe direction for an LED. A larger resistor gives a smaller current, so the LED runs a little dimmer but cannot be overdriven. Rounding down, to 270 Ω here, gives 4.8 mA, which is above the 4 mA target. The gap between the two standard values is about 20 %, which is why the choice matters even when the exact answer is only approximate.

The GPIO limit

The pin can supply only so much current. Each GPIO pad has an output drive strength, which you set in software. The RP2040 datasheet describes the drive strengths as 2, 4, 8 or 12 mA, in section 2.19.4 (Pads). The default is 4 mA, which section 5.5.2.1 gives in the pin types table. RP2040 datasheet, section 2.19.4 (Pads) and section 5.5.2.1 (default 4 mA). The drive strength is held in the pad control register, bits 5 and 4 of the GPIO's pad register, described in section 2.19.6.3. The same datasheet limits the total current across all the IOVDD pins to 50 mA, in section 5.5.3.4, Table 625. The drive strength is a nominal value, not a hard limit. The datasheet says a pin can source more than its setting if you accept that the output voltage falls. Lesson 51 covers GPIO output from C.

Those numbers mean a single LED at 4 mA is comfortably within the pin's limit. The 50 mA figure is a total for all the IOVDD pins together, so eight LEDs at 4 mA, which is 32 mA, fit only if the other pins source no more than 18 mA between them. A board with many LEDs must add up the currents. That is the same KCL habit from lesson 7, applied to the pins.

Do not connect an LED straight to a GPIO with no resistor. The pin holds its output near 3.3 V, and once the LED is past its forward voltage, a small rise in voltage gives a large rise in current. Nothing but the pin's own resistance and the LED then limits the current, and that can exceed both the pin's drive limit and the LED's rating. The resistor is not optional.

The code: choosing the resistor in C

The calculation is simple enough to write in C, and a helper that rounds up to an E12 value is useful in any tool that sizes parts. The example below computes the exact resistance and then rounds it up. 

#include <stdio.h>

/* Smallest E12 value at or above r, for r from 1 to 1000 ohms. */
static float e12_up(float r)
{
    static const float e12[] = {1.0f, 1.2f, 1.5f, 1.8f, 2.2f, 2.7f,
                                3.3f, 3.9f, 4.7f, 5.6f, 6.8f, 8.2f};
    float decade = 1.0f;
    while (r > decade * 10.0f) {
        decade *= 10.0f;
    }
    for (int i = 0; i < 12; i++) {
        if (e12[i] * decade >= r) {
            return e12[i] * decade;
        }
    }
    return 10.0f * decade;
}

int main(void)
{
    float exact = (3.3f - 2.0f) / 0.004f;   /* 1.3 V across 4 mA */
    printf("exact %.0f ohm, E12 %.0f ohm\n", (double)exact, (double)e12_up(exact));
    return 0;
}

The e12 array holds the twelve mantissas, the values before the decade is applied. It is static const, so it is stored once and cannot be changed. The helper e12_up takes a resistance and returns the smallest standard value at or above it, which is the round-up rule from the worked example.

The while loop finds the decade. It multiplies decade by ten until the target fits under decade × 10. For 325 Ω, the decade becomes 100. The for loop then walks the array, multiplying each mantissa by the decade, and returns the first product that is at least r. For 325 Ω, 3.3 × 100 = 330 is the first value that is large enough. If none fits in the decade, the function returns the next decade's 1.0 value, which is 10 × decade.

In main, the exact resistance is (3.3f − 2.0f) / 0.004f, which is 325 Ω. The printf line prints the exact value and the E12 value, and the output is exact 325 ohm, E12 330 ohm. The f suffixes keep the arithmetic in float, which is fine for this sizing task.

Common mistakes and how to spot them

  1. No resistor at all. You wire the LED straight from the pin to ground. The symptom is a very bright LED that dims or fails within a short time, and a pin that is warm. The fix is the resistor, always, because the LED has no current limit of its own.
  2. Using the supply instead of the drop. You compute R = 3.3 V ÷ 4 mA = 825 Ω and get a dim LED, because the resistor sees only 1.3 V and passes 1.6 mA. The symptom is an LED that is noticeably dim, and a measured voltage across the resistor of about 1.3 V rather than 3.3 V. The fix is to subtract Vf before you divide.
  3. Rounding down to the next E12 value. You pick 270 Ω because it is the nearest standard value, and the current rises to 4.8 mA. The symptom is a current above the target, which is only slightly visible on the LED but shows on a meter. The fix is to round up, as the rule in this lesson says.
  4. Reversing the LED. You connect the cathode to the pin side, and the LED stays dark. The symptom is no light at all, and the current is essentially zero. The fix is to check the anode and cathode, using the flat side or the shorter lead of a through-hole LED, and to connect the anode toward the resistor and the pin.
  5. Ignoring the pin's drive strength. You design for 20 mA on a pin set to 4 mA, and the LED is dim with the output voltage sagging. The symptom is a pin that does not reach its high level under load. The fix is to choose a current the pin supports at its drive setting, or to raise the drive strength and check the datasheet's limits.

In real projects

Every GPIO-driven LED on a board is an instance of this calculation, and a hardware review checks each one. A review asks three questions: what current does the LED need, what is the pin's drive setting, and what does the whole bank of pins supply together. A good schematic writes the target current and the resistor value next to the LED, so the next person does not have to repeat the arithmetic and cannot silently change the value.

The same component does other jobs in real products. A diode placed across a relay or motor coil protects the driver from the voltage spike that the coil makes when its current is switched off, and a diode in series with a supply stops a reversed battery from driving current the wrong way. Lesson 14 covers the coil case in detail. The principle is the one from this lesson: the diode has a forward voltage, it conducts only in one direction, and the circuit around it must be designed for the current it passes.

In firmware, the pin is set up once and then forgotten, which is where the trouble starts. A driver that changes the drive strength in code, or that turns on several outputs at once, changes the pin's current without any change to the schematic. Good code states the intended current in a comment next to the setup call, and a reviewer checks it against the datasheet. The lesson 51 GPIO code is the place to practise that habit.

Summary

  • A diode conducts strongly only above its forward voltage, and an LED is a diode that emits light.
  • An LED must have a current-limiting resistor in series, because its current is not set by Ohm's law.
  • Compute R = (Vsupply − Vf) ÷ I, using the LED's forward voltage from its datasheet.
  • Round up to the next E12 value, so the current stays at or below the target.
  • The RP2040's default drive strength is 4 mA, and the 50 mA IOVDD total is shared by all the GPIO and QSPI pins.
  • Connect the anode toward the supply side and the cathode toward ground, and check the current with a meter, not by assumption.
You chose a series resistor from the supply, the forward voltage and a target current. You rounded to a real E12 value in the safe direction, and checked the pin's drive limit. That is the calculation behind almost every LED on a board.
